# 1. Localization

## 1.0 Algebra of Rings

(1.0.1) All the rings that we consider in this note have a unit element, and all the modules over a ring are unital. Ring homomorphisms preserve the unit element. Unless explicitly stated otherwise, a subring of a ring is required to contain the unit element of the ring. We mainly consider commutative rings, when we speak of a ring without qualification, we meant a commutative ring. For a ring $\displaystyle{A}$ which is not necessarily commutative, $\displaystyle{A}$-module is a left $A$-module unless otherwise specified.

(1.0.2) Let $\displaystyle{A,B}$ be not necessarily commutative rings, $\displaystyle{\varphi:A\to B}$ be a homomorphism. Every left $\displaystyle{B}$-module $\displaystyle{M}$ can be endowed with a left $\displaystyle{A}$-module structure by setting $\displaystyle{am=\varphi(a)m}$ (reps. $\displaystyle{ma=m\varphi(a)}$); when it is necessary to distinguish the $\displaystyle{A}$-module and $\displaystyle{B}$-module structure on $\displaystyle{M}$, we denote by $\displaystyle{M_{[\varphi]}}$ the left $\displaystyle{A}$-module thus defined. If $\displaystyle{L}$ is an $\displaystyle{A}$-module, a homomorphism $\displaystyle{u:L\to M_{[\varphi]}}$ is therefore a homomorphism of Abelian groups such that $\displaystyle{u(ax)=\varphi(a)u(x)}$ for $\displaystyle{a\in A}$ and $\displaystyle{x \in L}$. We shall also say $\displaystyle{y}$ is a $\displaystyle{\varphi}$-homomorphism $\displaystyle{L\to M}$, and that the pair $\displaystyle{(\varphi,u)}$ (or, by abuse of language, $\displaystyle{u}$) is a di-homomorphism from $\displaystyle{(A,L)}$ to $\displaystyle{(B,M)}$. The pairs $\displaystyle{(A,L)}$, consisting of a ring $\displaystyle{A}$ and an $\displaystyle{A}$-module $\displaystyle{L}$, therefore form category, with di-homomorphisms as morphisms.

(1.0.3) under the assumptions of (1.0.2), let $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{I}}$ be a left (resp. right) ideal of $\displaystyle{A}$, we denote by $\displaystyle{B\mathfrak{I}}$ (resp. $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{I}B}$) the left (resp. right) ideal $\displaystyle{B\varphi(\mathfrak{I})}$ (resp. $\displaystyle{\varphi(\mathfrak{I})B}$) of $\displaystyle{B}$ generated by $\displaystyle{\varphi(\mathfrak{I})}$. It is also the image of the canonical homomorphism $\displaystyle{B\otimes _{A}\mathfrak{I}\to B}$ (resp. $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{I}\otimes _{A}B\to B}$) of left (resp. right) $\displaystyle{B}$-modules.

---

The canonical homomorphism $\displaystyle{B\otimes _{A}\mathfrak{I}\to B}$ is given by $\displaystyle{b\otimes a\to b\varphi(a)}$ for $\displaystyle{b\in B}$ and $\displaystyle{a\in \mathfrak{I}}$, and $\displaystyle{\mathrm{Im}(B\otimes _{A}\mathfrak{I}\to B)=B\mathfrak{I}}$.

---

(1.0.4) let $\displaystyle{A}$ be a (commutative) ring, and $\displaystyle{B}$ be a not necessarily commutative ring. An $\displaystyle{A}$-algebra structure on $\displaystyle{B}$ is equivalent to a ring homomoprhism $\displaystyle{\varphi:A\to B}$ such that $\displaystyle{\varphi(A)\subset Z(B)}$. For all ideals $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{I}}$ of $\displaystyle{A}$, $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{I}B=B\mathfrak{I}}$ is then a two-sided ideal of $\displaystyle{B}$, and for all $\displaystyle{B}$-module $\displaystyle{M}$, $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{I}M}$ is then a $\displaystyle{B}$-module, and $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{I}M=(B\mathfrak{I})M}$.

(1.0.5) we shall not review the notions of finitely generated modules and finitely generated (commutative) algebras; to say that an $\displaystyle{A}$-module $\displaystyle{M}$ is finitely generated means that there exists an exact sequence $\displaystyle{A^{p}\to M\to 0}$. We say $\displaystyle{M}$ admits a finite presentation if $\displaystyle{M}$ is isomorphic to the cokernel of a homomorphism $\displaystyle{A^{p}\to A^{q}}$, in other words, if there exists an exact sequence $A^{p}\to A^{q}\to M\to 0$. Note that over a Noetherian ring $\displaystyle{A}$, every finitely generated $\displaystyle{A}$-module admits a finite presentation.

Recall that an $\displaystyle{A}$-algebra $\displaystyle{B}$ is said to be integral over $\displaystyle{A}$ if every element of $\displaystyle{B}$ is a root of a monic polymomial with coefficients in $\displaystyle{A}$; equivalently every element of $\displaystyle{B}$ is contained in a subalgebra of $\displaystyle{B}$ which is finitely generated as an $\displaystyle{A}$-module. When it is the case, and when $\displaystyle{B}$ is commutative, a subalgebra of $\displaystyle{B}$ generated by a finite subset of $\displaystyle{B}$ is finitely generated as an $\displaystyle{A}$-module; hence, for a commutative $\displaystyle{A}$-algebra $\displaystyle{B}$ to be finitely generated, it is necessary and sufficient that $\displaystyle{B}$ be finitely generated as an $\displaystyle{A}$-module; in this case, $\displaystyle{B}$ is also called a finite integral $\displaystyle{A}$-algebra (or simply a finite $\displaystyle{A}$-algebra, if no confusion can result). Observe that, in these definitions, the homomoprhism $\displaystyle{A\to B}$ is not assumed to be injective.

(1.0.6) an integral ring is a ring whose the product of finite non-zero elements is non-zero; this is equivalent to saying that the product of two elements of the ring with $\displaystyle{0\neq 1}$ is not zero. A prime ideal of a ring $\displaystyle{A}$ is an ideal $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{p}}$ such that $\displaystyle{A/\mathfrak{p}}$ is integral; this therefore implies $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{p}\neq A}$. That a ring has at least one prime ideal is sufficient and necessary for it to be non-zero.

(1.0.7) a local ring $\displaystyle{A}$ is a ring with a unique maximal ideal, which consists of all the non-invertible elements of $\displaystyle{A}$ and contains every proper ideal of $\displaystyle{A}$. Let $\displaystyle{A}$ and $\displaystyle{B}$ be local rings, $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{m}}$ and $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{n}}$ be the maximal ideas of $\displaystyle{A}$ and $\displaystyle{B}$, respectively, a homomorphism $\displaystyle{\varphi:A\to B}$ is called local if $\displaystyle{\varphi(\mathfrak{m})\subset \mathfrak{n}}$ (or, equivalently, $\displaystyle{\varphi ^{-1}(\mathfrak{n})=\mathfrak{m}}$). Passing to the quotients, such a homomorphism induces an injective homomorphism of residue fields $\displaystyle{A/\mathfrak{m}\to B/\mathfrak{n}}$. The compositite of two local homomorphisms is also a local homomorphism.

## 1.1 Radical of an Ideal, Nilradical and Jacobson Radical of a Ring

(1.1.1) let $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{a}}$ be an ideal of a ring $\displaystyle{A}$. The radical of $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{a}}$, denoted by $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{r}(\mathfrak{a})}$, is the set of all $\displaystyle{x \in A}$ such that $\displaystyle{x^{n}\in \mathfrak{a}}$ for some positive integer $\displaystyle{n> 0}$; this is an ideal containing $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{a}}$. We have $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{r}(\mathfrak{r}(\mathfrak{a}))=\mathfrak{r}(\mathfrak{a})}$; $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{a}\subset \mathfrak{b}}$ implies $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{r}(\mathfrak{a})\subset \mathfrak{r}(\mathfrak{b})}$; the radical of finite intersection of ideals is the intersection of their radicals. Let $\displaystyle{\varphi:A'\to A}$ be the homomorphism, one has $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{r}(\varphi ^{-1}(\mathfrak{a}))=\varphi ^{-1}(\mathfrak{r}(\mathfrak{a}))}$. One ideal is the radical of some ideal iff it is an intersection of prime ideals. The radical of an ideal $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{a}}$ is the intersection of prime ideals minimal among those containing $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{a}}$. If $\displaystyle{A}$ is Noetherian, there are only finitely many such minimal prime ideals.

The radical of zero ideal $\displaystyle{(0)}$ is called the nilradical of $\displaystyle{A}$; it is the set of nilpotent elements of $\displaystyle{A}$, denoted by $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{R}}$. Ring $\displaystyle{A}$ is called to be reduced if $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{R}=(0)}$; for all ring $\displaystyle{A}$, the quotient $\displaystyle{A/\mathfrak{R}}$ is reduced.

---

We have

$$\begin{align}
\sqrt{ \mathfrak{a} } & =\bigcap _{\mathfrak{p}\supseteq \mathfrak{a}}\mathfrak{p}
\end{align}$$

the minimal means that, consider the set $\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{a}}}$ of prime ideals that containing $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{a}}$, we have the partial order defined by the inclusion, and the minimal elements of $\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{a}}}$ are the minimal prime ideals containing $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{a}}$

---

(1.1.2) recall that the Jacobi radical $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{R}(A)}$ of a (not necessarily commutative) ring $\displaystyle{A}$ is the intersection of all maximal left ideals of $A$ (and is also the intersection of all maximal right ideals of $A$). The radical of $\displaystyle{A/\mathfrak{R}(A)}$ is $\displaystyle{(0)}$.

## 1.2 Localized Modules and Localized Rings

(1.2.1) a subset $\displaystyle{S}$ of a ring $\displaystyle{A}$ is multiplicative is $\displaystyle{1\in S}$ and the product of every two elements of $\displaystyle{S}$ is in $\displaystyle{S}$. There are two important examples: 1. The set $\displaystyle{S_{f}=\left\{f^{n}:f\in A,n\geqslant 0\right\}}$; 2. The complement $\displaystyle{A\backslash\mathfrak{p}}$ of a prime ideal $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{p}}$.

(1.2.2) let $\displaystyle{S}$ be a multiplicative subset of a ring $\displaystyle{A}$, $\displaystyle{M}$ be a $\displaystyle{A}$-module; on $\displaystyle{M\times S}$, define a relation between pairs $\displaystyle{(m_{1},s_{1})}$ and $\displaystyle{(m_{2},s_{2})}$ by

$$\begin{align}
\exists s \in S, \text{ s.t. } s(s_{1}m_{2}-s_{2}m_{1})=0
\end{align}$$

this is an equivalence relation. We denote by $\displaystyle{S^{-1}M}$ as the quotient set of $\displaystyle{M\times S}$ by this relation, and by $\displaystyle{m/s}$ as the equivalence class of $\displaystyle{(m,s)}$ in $\displaystyle{S^{-1}M}$; we call the map $\displaystyle{i^{S}_{M}:m \in M\to m/1\in S^{-1}M}$ the canonical map (also noted as $\displaystyle{i^{S}}$). This map is neither injective nor surjective in general. The kernel of $\displaystyle{i^{S}}$ is the set of $\displaystyle{m\in M}$ such that $\displaystyle{\exists s\in S}$ such that $sm=0$.

On $\displaystyle{S^{-1}M}$ we define group additive law by taking

$$\begin{align}
\dfrac{m_{1}}{s_{1}}+\dfrac{m_{2}}{s_{2}} & =\dfrac{s_{2}m_{1}+s_{1}m_{2}}{s_{1}s_{2}}
\end{align}$$

(we can verify this is independent of the choice of representatives) moreover, on $\displaystyle{S^{-1}A}$ we define the multiplicative law by taking $\displaystyle{(a_{1}/s_{1})(a_{2}/s_{2})=(a_{1}a_{2})/(s_{1}s_{2})}$, and finally the external law on $\displaystyle{S^{-1}M}$, having $\displaystyle{S^{-1}A}$ as its operator sets, by taking $\displaystyle{(a/s)(m/s')=(am)/(ss')}$. We thus verify that $\displaystyle{S^{-1}A}$ is equipped with the ring structure (the localized ring of $\displaystyle{A}$ by $\displaystyle{S}$) and $\displaystyle{S^{-1}M}$ is equipped with the $\displaystyle{S^{-1}A}$-module structure (the localized module of $\displaystyle{M}$ by $\displaystyle{S}$). For all $\displaystyle{s \in S}$, $s/1$ is invertible in $S^{-1}A$, with inverse $1/s$. The canonical map $\displaystyle{i^{S}_{A}}$ (resp. $\displaystyle{i^{S}_{M}}$) is a ring homomorphism (resp. an $\displaystyle{A}$-module homomorphism, and $\displaystyle{S^{-1}M}$ is regarded as an $\displaystyle{A}$-module via the homomorphism $\displaystyle{i^{S}_{A}:A\to S^{-1}A}$).

(1.2.3) if $\displaystyle{S_{f}=\left\{f^{n}\right\}_{n\geqslant 0}}$ for an $\displaystyle{f\in A}$, we denote by $\displaystyle{A_{f}}$ and $\displaystyle{M_{f}}$ as $\displaystyle{S_{f}^{-1}A}$ as $\displaystyle{S_{f}^{-1}M}$; when $\displaystyle{A_{f}}$ is considered to be an algebra over $\displaystyle{A}$, we force that $\displaystyle{f}$ is invertible and write $\displaystyle{A_{f}=A[1/f]}$. $\displaystyle{A_{f}}$ is isomorphic to the quotient algebra $\displaystyle{A[T]/(fT-1)}$. When $\displaystyle{f=1}$, $\displaystyle{A_{f}}$ and $\displaystyle{M_{f}}$ are canonically identified with $\displaystyle{A}$ and $\displaystyle{M}$. If $\displaystyle{f}$ is nilpotent, then $\displaystyle{A_{f}}$ and $\displaystyle{M_{f}}$ reduce to 0.

When $\displaystyle{S=A\backslash\mathfrak{p}}$, where $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{p}}$ is a prime ideal of $\displaystyle{A}$, we denote by $\displaystyle{A_{\mathfrak{p}}}$ and $\displaystyle{M_{\mathfrak{p}}}$ as $\displaystyle{S^{-1}A}$ and $\displaystyle{S^{-1}M}$; $\displaystyle{A_{\mathfrak{p}}}$ is a local ring, and its unique maximal ideal $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{q}}$ is generated by $\displaystyle{i^{S}_{A}(\mathfrak{p})}$, and we have $\displaystyle{(i^{S}_{A})^{-1}(\mathfrak{q})=\mathfrak{p}}$; passing to quotient, $\displaystyle{i^{S}_{A}}$ induces an injective homomorphism from integral domain $\displaystyle{A/\mathfrak{p}}$ to field $\displaystyle{A_{\mathfrak{p}}/\mathfrak{q}}$, which is the field of fractions of $A/\mathfrak{p}$.

(1.2.4) localized ring $\displaystyle{S^{-1}A}$ and the canonical homomorphism $\displaystyle{i^{S}_{A}}$ are the solution of a universal mapping problem: every homomorphism $\displaystyle{u}$ from $\displaystyle{A}$ to a ring $\displaystyle{B}$ such that $\displaystyle{u(S)}$ consists of invertible elements of $B$ factors uniquely through $i^{S}_{A}$

$$\begin{align}
u:A\xrightarrow{i^{S}_{A}}S^{-1}A\xrightarrow{u^{*}}B
\end{align}$$

where $\displaystyle{u^{*}}$ is a ring homomorphism. Under the same assumptions, let $\displaystyle{M}$ be an $\displaystyle{A}$-module and $\displaystyle{N}$ be a $\displaystyle{B}$-module, $\displaystyle{v:M\to N}$ be an $\displaystyle{A}$-module homomorphism ($\displaystyle{N}$ is regarded as an $\displaystyle{A}$-module by $\displaystyle{u:A\to B}$); then $\displaystyle{v}$ factors uniquely through $\displaystyle{i^{S}_{M}}$

$$\begin{align}
v:M\xrightarrow{i^{S}_{M}}S^{-1}M\xrightarrow{v^{*}}N
\end{align}$$

where $\displaystyle{v^{*}}$ is an $\displaystyle{S^{-1}A}$-module homomorphism ($\displaystyle{N}$ is regarded as an $\displaystyle{S^{-1}A}$-module by $\displaystyle{u^{*}}$).

(1.2.5) we define the $\displaystyle{S^{-1}A}$-module canonical isomorphism $\displaystyle{S^{-1}A\otimes _{A}M\xrightarrow{\sim}S^{-1}M}$, by sending element $\displaystyle{(a/s)\otimes m}$ to $\displaystyle{(am)/s}$, and the inverse isomorphism sends $m/s$ to $(1/s)\otimes m$.

(1.2.6) for every ideal $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{a'}}$ of $\displaystyle{S^{-1}A}$, $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{a}=(i^{S}_{A})^{-1}(\mathfrak{a}')}$ is an ideal of $\displaystyle{A}$, and $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{a}'}$ is the ideal of $\displaystyle{S^{-1}A}$ generated by $\displaystyle{i^{S}_{A}(\mathfrak{a})}$, and is identified as $\mathfrak{a}'=S^{-1}\mathfrak{a}$; the map $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{p'}\to(i^{S}_{A})^{-1}(\mathfrak{p'})}$ is an isomorphism, for the order structure, of the set of prime ideals of $\displaystyle{S^{-1}A}$ to the set of prime ideals of $\displaystyle{A}$ such that $\displaystyle{p\cap S=\varnothing}$. Moreover, the localized ring $\displaystyle{A_{\mathfrak{p}}}$ and $\displaystyle{(S^{-1}A)_{S^{-1}\mathfrak{p}}}$ are then canonically isomorphic.

(1.2.7) when $\displaystyle{A}$ is an integral domain, whose field of fractions is denoted by $\displaystyle{K}$, the canonical map $\displaystyle{i^{S}_{A}:A\to S^{-1}A}$ is injective for every multiplicative subset $\displaystyle{S}$ that does not contain $\displaystyle{0}$, and $\displaystyle{S^{-1}A}$ then identified with a subring of $K$ containing $A$. In particular, for every prime ideal $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{p}}$ of $\displaystyle{A}$, $\displaystyle{A_{\mathfrak{p}}}$ is an local ring containing $\displaystyle{A}$, with the maximal ideal $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{p}A_{\mathfrak{p}}}$, and we have $\displaystyle{\mathfrak{p}A_{\mathfrak{p}}\cap A=\mathfrak{p}}$.

(1.2.8) let $\displaystyle{A}$ be a reduced ring, then $\displaystyle{S^{-1}A}$ is also reduced. Indeed, let $\displaystyle{(x/s)^{n}=0}$ for $\displaystyle{x \in A,s\in S}$, this means that $\displaystyle{\exists s'\in S}$ such that $\displaystyle{s'x^{n}=0}$, hence $\displaystyle{(s'x)^{n}=0}$, then by assumption, it follows that $\displaystyle{s'x=0}$, and therefore $\displaystyle{x/s=0}$

## 1.3 Factorial Properties

(1.3.1) let $\displaystyle{M,N}$ be two $\displaystyle{A}$-modules, $\displaystyle{u:M\to N}$ be an $\displaystyle{A}$-homomorphism. Let $\displaystyle{S}$ be a multiplicative subset of $\displaystyle{A}$, we define a $\displaystyle{S^{-1}A}$-homomorphism $\displaystyle{S^{-1}M\to S^{-1}N}$, denoted by $\displaystyle{S^{-1}u}$, by setting $\displaystyle{(S^{-1}u)(m/s)=u(m)/s}$; let $\displaystyle{S^{-1}M}$ and $\displaystyle{S^{-1}N}$ be canonically identifications $\displaystyle{S^{-1}A\otimes _{A}M}$ and $\displaystyle{S^{-1}A\otimes _{A}N}$ (1.2.5), $\displaystyle{S^{-1}u}$ is then identified by $\displaystyle{1\otimes u}$. Let $\displaystyle{P}$ be a third $\displaystyle{A}$-module, $\displaystyle{v:N\to P}$ be an $\displaystyle{A}$-homomorphism, we have $\displaystyle{S^{-1}(v \circ u)=(S^{-1}v)\circ(S^{-1}u)}$; in other words, $\displaystyle{S^{-1}M}$ is an covariant functor on $\displaystyle{M}$, from the category of $\displaystyle{A}$-modules then maps to the category of $S^{-1}A$-modules ($\displaystyle{A}$ and $\displaystyle{S}$ are fixed)

(1.3.2) the functor $\displaystyle{S^{-1}M}$ is exact; in other words, if the sequence

$$\begin{align}
M\xrightarrow{u}N\xrightarrow{v}P
\end{align}$$

is exact, then the sequence

$$\begin{align}
S^{-1}M\xrightarrow{S^{-1}u}S^{-1}N\xrightarrow{S^{-1}v}S^{-1}P
\end{align}$$

is also exact. In particular, let $\displaystyle{u:M\to N}$ be injective (resp. surjective), then the same holds for $S^{-1}u$; let $\displaystyle{N}$ and $\displaystyle{P}$ are submodules of $\displaystyle{M}$, $\displaystyle{S^{-1}N}$ and $\displaystyle{S^{-1}P}$ are submodules of $\displaystyle{S^{-1}M}$, and we have

$$\begin{align}
S^{-1}(N+P) & =S^{-1}N+S^{-1}P, & S^{-1}(N\cap P) & =(S^{-1}N)\cap(S^{-1}P)
\end{align}$$

(1.3.3) let $\displaystyle{(M_{\alpha},\varphi _{\beta \alpha})}$ be an inductive system of $\displaystyle{A}$-modules; then $\displaystyle{(S^{-1}M_{\alpha},S^{-1}\varphi _{\beta \alpha})}$ is an inductive sustem of $\displaystyle{S^{-1}A}$-modules. Express $\displaystyle{S^{-1}M_{\alpha}}$ and $\displaystyle{S^{-1}\varphi _{\beta \alpha}}$ as tensor products (1.2.5 and 1.3.1), the operation of tensor product commutes with direct limit, and we have a canonical isomorphism

$$\begin{align}
S^{-1}\underrightarrow{\lim}M_{\alpha}\xrightarrow{\sim}\underrightarrow{\lim} S^{-1}M_{\alpha}
\end{align}$$

we express this as the functor $\displaystyle{S^{-1}M}$ (of $\displaystyle{M}$) commutes with inductive limits.

(1.3.4) let $\displaystyle{M}$ and $\displaystyle{N}$ be $\displaystyle{A}$-modules; there exists a canonical isomorphism, functorial in $\displaystyle{M}$ and $\displaystyle{N}$

$$\begin{align}
(S^{-1}M)\otimes _{S^{-1}A}(S^{-1}N)\xrightarrow{\sim}S^{-1}(M\otimes _{A}N)
\end{align}$$

that transform $\displaystyle{(m/s)\otimes(n/t)}$ to $\displaystyle{(m\otimes n)/st}$.

(1.3.5) we have the same homomorphism, functorial in $\displaystyle{M}$ and $\displaystyle{N}$

$$\begin{align}
S^{-1}\mathrm{Hom}_{A}(M,N)\to \mathrm{Hom}_{S^{-1}}(S^{-1}M,S^{-1}N)
\end{align}$$

qui, à $\displaystyle{u/s}$, fait correpondre l'homomorphsime $\displaystyle{m/t\to u(m)/st}$. When $\displaystyle{M}$ admits a finite presentation, the previous homomorphism is an isomorphism; this is immediate when $\displaystyle{M}$ is forme $\displaystyle{A^{r}}$ and we pass to the general case by using an exact sequence $A^p\to A^q\to M\to 0$, and used the exactness of the functor $\displaystyle{S^{-1}M}$ and the left exactness of functor $\displaystyle{\mathrm{Hom}_{A}(M,N)}$ of $\displaystyle{M}$. We note that this is always true when $\displaystyle{A}$ is Noetherian and the $\displaystyle{A}$-module $\displaystyle{M}$ is finitely generated.

## 1.4 Change of Multiplicative Subset

(1.4.1) let $\displaystyle{S}$ and $\displaystyle{T}$ be two multiplicative subsets of a ring $\displaystyle{A}$ such that $\displaystyle{S\subset T}$; there exists a canonical homomorphism $\displaystyle{\rho ^{T,S}_{A}}$ (or simply $\displaystyle{\rho ^{T,S}}$) from $\displaystyle{S^{-1}A}$ to $\displaystyle{T^{-1}A}$ by sending $\displaystyle{a/s}$ of $\displaystyle{S^{-1}A}$ to $\displaystyle{a/s}$ of $\displaystyle{T^{-1}A}$. We have $\displaystyle{i^{T}_{A}=\rho ^{T,S}_{A}\circ i^{S}_{A}}$. For every $\displaystyle{A}$-module $\displaystyle{M}$, similarly, there exists a $\displaystyle{S^{-1}A}$-linear map from $\displaystyle{S^{-1}M}$ to $\displaystyle{T^{-1}M}$ (the latter is considered to be $\displaystyle{S^{-1}A}$-module via the homomorphism $\displaystyle{\rho ^{T,S}_{A}}$), that sends $\displaystyle{m/s}$ of $\displaystyle{S^{-1}M}$ to $\displaystyle{m/s}$ of $\displaystyle{T^{-1}M}$; we denote this map by $\displaystyle{\rho _{M}^{T,S}}$, or simply $\displaystyle{\rho ^{T,S}}$ and we have $\displaystyle{i^{T}_{M}=\rho ^{T,S}_{M}\circ i^{S}_{m}}$; by the canonical identification (1.2.5), $\displaystyle{\rho ^{T,S}_{M}}$ is identified with $\displaystyle{\rho ^{T,S}_{A}\otimes 1}$. The homomorphism $\displaystyle{\rho ^{T,S}_{M}}$ is a natural transformation from the functor $\displaystyle{S^{-1}(-)}$ to the function $\displaystyle{T^{-1}(-)}$, in other words, the diagram

```tikz
\usepackage{tikz-cd,amsmath,amssymb}
\begin{document}\begin{tikzcd}
S^{-1}M\arrow{r}{S^{-1}u}\arrow{d}{\rho _{M}^{T,S}} & S^{-1}N \arrow{d}{\rho ^{T,S}_{N}}\\
T^{-1}M\arrow{r}{T^{-1}u} & T^{-1}N
\end{tikzcd}\end{document}
```

Is commutative for all homomorphism $\displaystyle{u:M\to N}$; in addition, we note that $\displaystyle{T^{-1}u}$ is entirely determined by $\displaystyle{S^{-1}u}$, for $\displaystyle{m\in M}$ and $\displaystyle{t\in T}$, we have

$$\begin{align}
(T^{-1}u)(m/t) & =(t/1)^{-1}\rho _{N} ^{T,S}((S^{-1}u)(m/1))
\end{align}$$

(1.4.2) with the same notations, for two $\displaystyle{A}$-modules $\displaystyle{M}$ and $\displaystyle{N}$, the diagrams (cf. (1.3.4) and (1.3.5))

```tikz
\usepackage{tikz-cd,amsmath,amssymb}
\begin{document}\begin{tikzcd}
(S^{-1}M)\otimes _{S^{-1}A}(S^{-1}N)\arrow{r}{\sim}\arrow{d}{} & S^{-1}(M\otimes _{A}N)\arrow{d}{} \\
(T^{-1}M)\otimes _{T^{-1}A}(T^{-1}N)\arrow{r}{\sim} & T^{-1}(M\otimes _{A}N)
\end{tikzcd}\quad\begin{tikzcd}
S^{-1}\mathrm{Hom}_{A}(M,N)\arrow{r}{}\arrow{d}{} & \mathrm{Hom}_{S^{-1}A}(S^{-1}M,S^{-1}N) \\
T^{-1}\mathrm{Hom}_{A}(M,N)\arrow{r}{} & \mathrm{Hom}_{T^{-1}A}(T^{-1}M,T^{-1}N)
\end{tikzcd}\end{document}
```

Is commutative.

(1.4.3) there is an important case in which $\displaystyle{\rho ^{T,S}}$ is bijective, namely when every element in $\displaystyle{T}$ divides some element of $\displaystyle{S}$; in this case, we identify $\displaystyle{S^{-1}M}$ with $\displaystyle{T^{-1}M}$ via $\displaystyle{\rho ^{T,S}}$. A multiplicative subset $\displaystyle{S}$ is said to be saturated if every divisor in $\displaystyle{A}$ of an element of $\displaystyle{S}$ also belons to $\displaystyle{S}$. Replacing $\displaystyle{S}$ by set $\displaystyle{T}$ of all divisors of elements of $\displaystyle{S}$, which is itself multiplicative and saturated, we see that, if desired, we may always restrict attention to localizations $\displaystyle{S^{-1}M}$ with $\displaystyle{S}$ saturated.

(1.4.4) let $\displaystyle{S,T,U}$ be three multiplicative subsets of $\displaystyle{A}$ such that $\displaystyle{S\subset T\subset U}$, we have

$$\begin{align}
\rho ^{U,S} & =\rho ^{U,T}\circ \rho ^{T,S}
\end{align}$$

(1.4.5) consider a directed increasing family $\displaystyle{(S_{\alpha})}$ of multiplicative subsets of $\displaystyle{A}$ (we write $\displaystyle{\alpha\leqslant \beta}$ for $\displaystyle{S_{\alpha}\subset S_{\beta}}$), and let $\displaystyle{S}$ be the multiplicative subset $\displaystyle{\bigcup _{\alpha}S_{\alpha}}$; put $\displaystyle{\rho _{\beta \alpha}=\rho ^{S_{\beta},S_{\alpha}}_{A}}$ for $\displaystyle{\alpha\leqslant \beta}$; by (1.4.4) the homomorphisms $\displaystyle{\rho _{\beta \alpha}}$ form a direct system of rings $\displaystyle{(S^{-1}_{\alpha}A,\rho _{\beta \alpha})}$, and let $\displaystyle{A'}$ be its direct limit. Let$\displaystyle{\rho _{\alpha}}$ be the canonical map $\displaystyle{S^{-1}_{\alpha}A\to A'}$, and put $\displaystyle{\varphi _{\alpha}=\rho _{A}^{S,S_{\alpha}}}$; and $\displaystyle{\varphi _{\alpha}=\varphi _{\beta}\circ\rho _{\beta \alpha}}$ for $\displaystyle{\alpha\leqslant \beta}$ by (1.4.4), we can uniquely define a homomorphism $\displaystyle{\varphi:A'\to S^{-1}A}$ such that the diagram

```tikz
\usepackage{tikz-cd,amsmath,amssymb}
\begin{document}\begin{tikzcd}
 & S_{\alpha}^{-1}A \arrow{ddl}{\rho _{\alpha}} \arrow{ddr}{\varphi _{\alpha}} \arrow{d}{\rho _{\beta \alpha}} & \\
 & S_{\beta}^{-1}A \arrow{dl}{\rho _{\beta}} \arrow{dr}{\varphi _{\beta}} & \\
 A' \arrow{rr}{\varphi} & & S^{-1}A
\end{tikzcd}\end{document}
```

Is commutative. Indeed, $\displaystyle{\varphi}$ is an isomorphism: this is immediately true by the construction that $\displaystyle{\varphi}$ is surjective. On the other hand, let $\displaystyle{\rho _{\alpha}(a/s_{\alpha})\in A'}$ sych that $\displaystyle{\varphi(\rho _{\alpha}(a/s_{\alpha}))=0}$, this means that $\displaystyle{a/s_{\alpha}=0}$ in $\displaystyle{S^{-1}A}$, this is to say $\displaystyle{\exists s\in S}$ such that $\displaystyle{sa=0}$; but there is an $\displaystyle{\beta\geqslant \alpha}$ such that $\displaystyle{s\in S_{\beta}}$, therefore $\displaystyle{\rho _{\alpha}(a/s_{\alpha})=\rho _{\beta}(sa/ss_{\alpha})=0}$, we then conclude that $\displaystyle{\varphi}$ is injective. We treat the same way the case of $\displaystyle{A}$-modules $\displaystyle{M}$, and we thus define the canonical isomorphisms

$$\begin{align}
\underrightarrow{\lim}S_{\alpha}^{-1}A\xrightarrow{\sim}S^{-1}A,\quad \underrightarrow{\lim}S_{\alpha}^{-1}M\xrightarrow{\sim}S^{-1}M
\end{align}$$

the second isomorphism is functorial in $\displaystyle{M}$.

(1.4.6) let $\displaystyle{S_{1}}$ and $\displaystyle{S_{2}}$ be two multiplicative subsets of $\displaystyle{A}$; then $\displaystyle{S_{1}S_{2}}$ is also a multiplicative subset of $\displaystyle{A}$. Let $\displaystyle{S_{2}'}$ denote the canonical image of $\displaystyle{S_{2}}$ in the ring $\displaystyle{S_{1}^{-1}A}$; it is a multiplicative subset of $\displaystyle{S_{1}^{-1}A}$. For every $\displaystyle{A}$-module $\displaystyle{M}$, there is a functorial ismorphism

$$\begin{align}
S_{2}'^{-1}(S_{1}^{-1}M)\xrightarrow{\sim}(S_{1}S_{2})^{-1}M
\end{align}$$

by sending $\displaystyle{(m/s_{1})/(s_{2}/1)}$ to $\displaystyle{m/(s_{1}s_{2})}$.

## Change of Rings
