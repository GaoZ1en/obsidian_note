# Finite-Temperature Supersymmetric Matter

**EC-28 — DRAFT study chapter; bounded review recorded.**

A supersymmetric Hamiltonian and a supersymmetric thermal state are different objects. We calculate the ordinary and graded traces of a declared four-dimensional free multiplet, construct its periodic and antiperiodic thermal Green functions, and evaluate an interacting scalar thermal curvature. The interacting example is explicitly a separate N=1 Wess–Zumino theory, not an interacting completion of the free N=2 reference.

Use physical signature $(-,+,+,+)$ and $c=\hbar=k_B=1$. The background is fixed Minkowski space, with Hamiltonian generating the chosen rest-frame time t. Temperature is $T=\beta^{-1}>0$. The Euclidean circle used below represents a trace of that Hamiltonian; it is not a dynamical spacetime compactification.

## 1. A Specified Four-Dimensional N=2 Reference

Take the free Abelian vector multiplet of [the rigid N=2 construction](<MAG-06.md#L182-L194>), with canonical prepotential $\mathscr F_0=ia^2/2$. Its independent propagating fields are a complex scalar a, a real gauge potential $B_\mu$, and two Weyl fields $\psi^A,\chi^A$. The chosen action has eight real supersymmetry charges. This is model input from its full supersymmetric action, not a conclusion from equal numbers of modes.

After classical auxiliary elimination, its free component density, up to the stated spacetime divergences, is

$$\begin{align}
\mathcal L_0=-\partial_\mu\bar a\partial^\mu a -\frac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} +i\psi^\dagger s^\mu\partial_\mu\psi +i\chi^\dagger s^\mu\partial_\mu\chi, \quad s^\mu=(I,\sigma_i),\quad F_{\mu\nu}=2\partial_{[\mu}B_{\nu]}. \tag{1.1}
\end{align}$$

The [physical spinor dictionary](<AST-15.md#L80-L169>) has $H=i\partial_t$, $P_i=-i\partial_i$ and $\{\gamma^a,\gamma^b\}=-2\eta^{ab}I$. The unprimed upper Weyl fields are the fundamental positive-$\gamma_5$ block. Their conjugates are present; no physical weak-left field has been silently relabelled. This reference is not an identification with Standard Model matter.

### 1.1 Define the Infrared-Regulated Field Theory

Put space on a flat three-torus of side L and volume $V=L^3$. Choose periodic spatial spin structure for all fields. Work in the trivial Abelian bundle with no harmonic flux sector. Before quantization omit the complete spatial Fourier multiplet at $\mathbf k=0$, including its canonical variables, fermions, auxiliaries and gauge modes. Thus

$$\begin{align}
\mathbf k=\frac{2\pi}{L}\mathbf n,\qquad \mathbf n\in\mathbb Z^3\setminus\{0\},\qquad \omega_{\mathbf k}=|\mathbf k|>0. \tag{1.2}
\end{align}$$

This is an infrared definition of a nonzero-spatial-mode free field model. It is not a normalizable Hilbert-space projector onto a zero-mode vacuum. The unrestricted massless scalar zero mode is a free particle on a noncompact target, not an oscillator of positive frequency. Its ordinary thermal trace would diverge. Constant gauge and fermion modes also need their own treatment; they are not included in this reference.

The free transformations are linear and preserve spatial momentum, so removing the whole block preserves their zero-temperature algebra on the remaining fields. An optional common cutoff $|\mathbf k|\le\Lambda$ likewise retains complete multiplets. No bosonic occupation-number truncation is made. This restriction is spatially nonlocal at finite L; it is a regulator, not a claim about the unregulated zero-mode theory.

Gauss reduction leaves two transverse photon polarizations and two real scalar modes. The two Weyl fields give four fermionic particle/antiparticle modes. For each retained momentum, therefore, there are four physical bosonic oscillators and four fermionic occupation modes. The same frequency occurs in both sectors:

$$\begin{align}
H_{L,\Lambda}=\sum_{\mathbf k} \omega_{\mathbf k}\left[ \sum_{b=1}^4\left(N_{b\mathbf k}+\frac12\right) +\sum_{f=1}^4\left(N_{f\mathbf k}-\frac12\right)\right] =\sum_{\mathbf k}\omega_{\mathbf k} \left(\sum_bN_{b\mathbf k}+\sum_fN_{f\mathbf k}\right). \tag{1.3}
\end{align}$$

Here $N_b=0,1,\ldots$ and $N_f=0,1$. The zero-point cancellation occurs at the common finite cutoff, before either infinite vacuum sum is taken. The vacuum energy and the normalization of the supersymmetry algebra are thereby fixed together. This physical Hamiltonian defines the quantum reference; no uncomputed Lorentzian auxiliary-field integration contour is being used to normalize it.

## 2. Ordinary Gibbs Trace, Graded Trace and a Thermal Ward Test

Let $\Pi=(-1)^{\mathsf F}$ be physical fermion parity. Define

$$\begin{align}
Z_L(\beta)=\operatorname{Tr}e^{-\beta H_L},\qquad \rho_\beta=Z_L^{-1}e^{-\beta H_L},\qquad I_L(\beta)=\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi e^{-\beta H_L}). \tag{2.1}
\end{align}$$

The last expression is a graded trace. Calling it an index additionally requires the supersymmetry, domain and trace-class conditions that make it invariant. It is not a positive Gibbs state.

For $x_{\mathbf k}=e^{-\beta\omega_{\mathbf k}}$, the occupation sums give

$$
\begin{aligned}
 Z_L&=\prod_{\mathbf k\ne0}
 \left(\frac{1+x_{\mathbf k}}{1-x_{\mathbf k}}\right)^4,
 & I_L&=\prod_{\mathbf k\ne0}
 \frac{(1-x_{\mathbf k})^4}{(1-x_{\mathbf k})^4}=1,\\
 U_L&=-\partial_\beta\log Z_L
 =4\sum_{\mathbf k\ne0}\omega_{\mathbf k}
 [n_B(\omega_{\mathbf k})+n_F(\omega_{\mathbf k})],
 &n_{B,F}(E)&=\frac1{e^{\beta E}\mp1}.
 \end{aligned}
 \tag{2.2}
$$

The Bose sum is unbounded, not a finite two-state substitute. For example, in one momentum block the ordinary level-N multiplicity is $\sum_{f=0}^{\min(4,N)}\binom4f\binom{N-f+3}{3}$, whereas inserting $(-1)^f$ cancels every positive level. The multiplet and its supercharges were specified before this combinatorial check.

At fixed L every retained frequency has a positive lower bound. For $\beta>0$, $\sum_{\mathbf k}\omega_{\mathbf k}^j e^{-\beta\omega_{\mathbf k}}$ converges for fixed nonnegative j. This controls both the thermal product and the energy-weighted traces after the common ultraviolet cutoff is removed. All trace manipulations may first be made at finite cutoff on the finite-particle core, with the Boltzmann factor providing convergence of the unbounded bosonic sums.

### 2.1 Why Commuting with the Density Operator Is Insufficient

The conserved physical charges, not merely superspace differential symbols, satisfy the mixed algebra

$$\begin{align}
\{Q^{AI},(Q^{BJ})^\dagger\} =2\delta^{IJ}P^{AB'},\qquad P^{AB'}=(H I_2+P_i\sigma_i)^{AB'},\quad I,J=1,2. \tag{2.3}
\end{align}$$

For an odd observable A its supersymmetry variation uses the graded bracket $\{Q,A\}$. Ordinary cyclicity, even when $[\rho_\beta,Q]=0$, gives

$$\begin{align}
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_\beta\{Q,Q^\dagger\}) =2\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_\beta Q^\dagger Q), \qquad \sum_{A,I}\langle\{Q^{AI},(Q^{AI})^\dagger\}\rangle_\beta =8U_L>0. \tag{2.4}
\end{align}$$

The second identity follows by tracing the two spinor indices of (2.3), then summing the two supersymmetries. The ordinary thermal trace supplies no minus sign when an odd operator is cycled. Thus conservation of Q and commutation with the density operator do not imply the ordinary thermal supersymmetry Ward identity. The Hamiltonian can remain supersymmetric while its positive-temperature state fails this Ward test. In the free model the energies remain paired; unequal Bose and Fermi occupation is already sufficient.

For the graded trace, $\Pi Q=-Q\Pi$ supplies the missing sign. If the weighted products are trace class,

$$\begin{align}
\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\Pi e^{-\beta H}\{Q,A\}\bigr)=0 \quad(A\text{ odd}),\qquad \partial_\beta I_L=-\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi H e^{-\beta H})=0. \tag{2.5}
\end{align}$$

This justifies the cancellation in (2.2) for the regulated field model. It does not say that physical thermal energy or entropy vanishes. Nor does the formal limit of its value one restore the omitted noncompact zero-mode trace: a bosonic divergence and a zero fermionic supertrace cannot be multiplied without a regulator and domain prescription.

## 3. Spin Structure, Gauge Ghosts and Charge Insertions

The Euclidean time-ordered representation of the ordinary trace has

$$\begin{align}
\phi(\tau+\beta)=\phi(\tau),\qquad \psi(\tau+\beta)=-\psi(\tau),\qquad \omega_n=2\pi nT,\quad \nu_n=(2n+1)\pi T. \tag{3.1}
\end{align}$$

Here phi denotes a physical boson and psi a physical fermion. These are the periodic and antiperiodic spin structures along the thermal circle. Inserting physical fermion parity changes the physical fermions to periodic boundary conditions. It does not change Grassmann integration into commuting integration.

Constant rigid supersymmetry parameters cannot preserve the ordinary thermal boundary conditions: a scalar variation proportional to $\varepsilon\psi$ would be antiperiodic rather than periodic. Making the parameter time dependent introduces derivative-of-parameter terms and is not the same conserved rigid transformation. This boundary statement agrees with (2.4); it does not make the physical charges disappear from the zero-temperature theory.

Gauge ghosts are a separate case. A periodic gauge potential is transformed by a periodic gauge parameter. Accordingly the Faddeev–Popov ghosts in a covariant description are periodic, although they are Grassmann odd. They are not physical fermions counted by $\mathsf F$. In Feynman gauge the Gaussian powers are

$$\begin{align}
\underbrace{(\det{}'K_P)^{-2}}_{\text{four vector coefficients}}
 \underbrace{(\det{}'K_P)^{+1}}_{\text{complex gauge ghost}}
 \underbrace{(\det{}'K_P)^{-1}}_{\text{complex scalar}}
 \underbrace{(\det{}'K_{AP})^{+2}}_{\text{two Weyl fields}}.
 \tag{3.2}
\end{align}$$

Here $K=-\partial_\tau^2-\nabla^2$. The prime removes every spatial-zero Fourier block, not only the four-dimensional zero eigenvalue. The physical Gauss-reduced trace fixes the gauge-volume and Gaussian normalization; the displayed powers check its four-plus-four spectrum. They are not an absolute normalization of an unregulated functional measure. The graded trace replaces the last AP by P, so the powers cancel. A numerator from Berezin integration alone does not turn an ordinary thermal partition function into a Hilbert-space supertrace.

For one equal-frequency boson/fermion factor inside (2.2), the canonically normalized factors are

$$\begin{align}
Z_B=\frac1{2\sinh(\beta E/2)},\quad Z_F=2\cosh(\beta E/2),\quad I_F=2\sinh(\beta E/2). \tag{3.3}
\end{align}$$

Thus $Z_BZ_F=\coth(\beta E/2)$, whereas $Z_BI_F=1$. The equal zero-point energies cancel in each product; the ordinary thermal factors do not. This is a factorization of an already specified field theory, not a construction of four-dimensional supersymmetry from a quantum-mechanical pair.

### 3.1 Chemical Potentials and R Twists Are Additional Data

All physical Gibbs ensembles computed here have zero real chemical potential; the separate unitary R-weighted traces below are not those ensembles. A proposed grand-canonical generator $H-\mu\mathcal C$ needs a conserved gauge-singlet charge $\mathcal C$ on the physical Hilbert space. Its bosonic spectrum must still give a trace-class weight; charged bosonic modes can otherwise make the trace diverge. A gauge constraint is not an arbitrary matter-number chemical potential. Even if $[\mathcal C,Q]=0$, an ordinary grand-canonical trace still does not acquire graded cyclicity.

For an insertion $U=e^{i\alpha R}$ define the operator-field charge by $U\Phi U^{-1}=e^{i\alpha r_\Phi}\Phi$. The ordinary twisted trace then imposes

$$\begin{align}
\Phi(\tau+\beta)=(-1)^{f_\Phi} e^{i\alpha r_\Phi}\Phi(\tau). \tag{3.4}
\end{align}$$

This convention distinguishes the charge of a field operator from that of a state created by its adjoint. A candidate refined index is instead $\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi Ue^{-\beta H})$. Its usual supersymmetric cancellation requires the extra insertion U to commute with the chosen charge, as well as trace-class/domain control. A generic R rotation of a charged supercharge fails this condition.

For orientation, the free vector action permits the classical assignment $r(a)=2$, $r(\psi)=r(\chi)=1$, $r(B_\mu)=0$ and $r(Q)=-1$. With the vacuum R charge normal ordered to zero, its one-momentum physical states have scalar charges $+2,-2$, two fermionic states of each charge $+1,-1$, and two neutral photon states. For $x=e^{-\beta E}$ and $y=e^{i\alpha}$ their actual weighted traces are

$$\begin{align}
Z_R(x,y)=\frac{(1+xy)^2(1+x/y)^2} {(1-x)^2(1-xy^2)(1-x/y^2)},\qquad I_R(x,y)=\frac{(1-xy)^2(1-x/y)^2} {(1-x)^2(1-xy^2)(1-x/y^2)}. \tag{3.5}
\end{align}$$

At $y=1$ these give the ordinary factor and one respectively. Generic $I_R$ depends on x, so it is not a protected temperature index. At the special half rotation $y=-1$, $Z_R=1$ and $I_R=Z_R(x,1)$: grading and this particular R insertion exchange the two traces rather than make the ensembles identical. If a real R chemical potential were introduced, convergence of the charged bosonic sums would require $2|\mu_R|<E_{\min}=2\pi/L$; there is no fixed nonzero such chemical potential in the uncondensed thermodynamic-limit free ensemble. Moreover $[H-\mu_R R,Q]=\mu_R Q$, not zero. No such ensemble is used for (2.2) or (4.1).

A generic R twist is therefore different. In the separate cubic model below, $r(z)=2/3$ and $r(\xi)=-1/3$. Its cubic interaction does not conserve an independent scalar-particle number. Any quantum R-twisted index would additionally need a conserved, nonanomalous charge and an appropriate background. None is substituted for the ordinary Gibbs trace here.

## 4. Evaluated Thermodynamic Limit

After the fixed-L ultraviolet limit, take $L\to\infty$ at fixed positive T in the thermal densities. The removed spatial point has zero continuum measure. From (2.2),

$$\begin{align}
p=4T\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3} \left[\log(1+e^{-|\mathbf k|/T})- \log(1-e^{-|\mathbf k|/T})\right]. \tag{4.1}
\end{align}$$

Expanding the logarithms leaves odd windings only. Radial integration gives $\int_0^\infty k^2e^{-\ell k/T}dk=2T^3/\ell^3$, while $\sum_{\ell\ge1,\ell\text{ odd}}\ell^{-4}=\pi^4/96$. Consequently

$$\begin{align}
p=\frac{8T^4}{\pi^2}\sum_{\ell\text{ odd}}\frac1{\ell^4} =\frac{\pi^2T^4}{12},\qquad u=T\frac{dp}{dT}-p=\frac{\pi^2T^4}{4},\qquad s=\frac{dp}{dT}=\frac{\pi^2T^3}{3}. \tag{4.2}
\end{align}$$

In particular the finite-box Ward limit is

$$\begin{align}
\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac1V \sum_{A,I}\langle\{Q^{AI},(Q^{AI})^\dagger\}\rangle_\beta =8u=2\pi^2T^4>0. \tag{4.3}
\end{align}$$

These are bulk field-theory results. At finite L the discrete sums, rather than (4.2), are the answer. In infinite volume a Gibbs operator need not be trace class at all; the limiting equilibrium state is understood through thermal densities and local correlations. We have not asserted a global infinite-volume density matrix, supercharge domain or index merely by taking a limit of numbers.

## 5. Four-Dimensional Thermal Green Functions

Let $D_P$ and $D_{AP}$ denote scalar inverses of the second-order kinetic operator with the indicated temporal boundary condition. At finite L their definition in this regulator is

$$\begin{align}
D_\sigma(\tau,\mathbf x)=\frac TV \sum_{n}\sum_{\mathbf k\ne0} \frac{e^{i\omega_n^\sigma\tau+i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}} {(\omega_n^\sigma)^2+|\mathbf k|^2}, \qquad \sigma=P,AP. \tag{5.1}
\end{align}$$

Its source is $\delta_\sigma(\tau)[\delta_L^3(\mathbf x)-1/V]$. The subtraction records the omitted spatial block. In the thermodynamic limit the usual local source is recovered.

### 5.1 Time-Circle Normalization

For one positive spatial energy E, define the inverse on $0<\tau<\beta$:

$$\begin{align}
g_P(\tau;E)=\frac{\cosh[E(\beta/2-\tau)]}{2E\sinh(\beta E/2)},\qquad g_{AP}(\tau;E)=\frac{\sinh[E(\beta/2-\tau)]}{2E\cosh(\beta E/2)}. \tag{5.2}
\end{align}$$

Both solve $(-\partial_\tau^2+E^2)g=0$ off the source. Their endpoint values have the required periodic or antiperiodic sign. Moreover $g_P'(0^+)-g_P'(\beta^-)=-1$ and $g_{AP}'(0^+)+g_{AP}'(\beta^-)=-1$. The latter uses the antiperiodic image at $0^-$. These source jumps fix the normalization, giving the actual frequency sums

$$\begin{align}
T\sum_n\frac1{(2\pi nT)^2+E^2} =\frac{\coth(\beta E/2)}{2E} =\frac1{2E}+\frac{n_B(E)}E,
\end{align}$$

$$\begin{align}
T\sum_n\frac1{[(2n+1)\pi T]^2+E^2} =\frac{\tanh(\beta E/2)}{2E} =\frac1{2E}-\frac{n_F(E)}E. \tag{5.3}
\end{align}$$

The negative thermal term in the second inverse is not a negative physical probability. It is a statement about an antiperiodic second-order kernel. A fermion functional determinant also has its own Grassmann sign.

### 5.2 Spatial Dependence and Short-Distance Subtraction

Now take the thermodynamic limit; at finite L retain (5.1). For $r=|\mathbf x|>0$, the spatial Fourier inverse is $e^{-|\omega_n|r}/(4\pi r)$, with zero frequency understood by its positive-mass limit. Summing the convergent geometric series gives

$$\begin{align}
D_P(\tau,r)=\frac{T}{4\pi r} \frac{\sinh(2\pi Tr)}{\cosh(2\pi Tr)-\cos(2\pi T\tau)},\qquad D_{AP}(\tau,r)=\frac{T}{2\pi r} \frac{\sinh(\pi Tr)\cos(\pi T\tau)}{\cosh(2\pi Tr)-\cos(2\pi T\tau)}. \tag{5.4}
\end{align}$$

These solve $(-\partial_\tau^2-\partial_r^2-2r^{-1}\partial_r)D=0$ away from coincidence. Shifting tau by beta preserves the first and reverses the second. Both approach $D_0=1/[4\pi^2(r^2+\tau^2)]$ as T tends to zero at fixed noncoincident arguments.

At equal time the formulas reduce to

$$\begin{align}
D_P(0,r)=\frac{T}{4\pi r}\coth(\pi Tr),\qquad D_{AP}(0,r)=\frac{T}{4\pi r}\operatorname{csch}(\pi Tr),
\end{align}$$

$$\begin{align}
\lim_{r\to0}[D_P(0,r)-D_0(0,r)]=\frac{T^2}{12},\qquad \lim_{r\to0}[D_{AP}(0,r)-D_0(0,r)]=-\frac{T^2}{24}. \tag{5.5}
\end{align}$$

At large r the periodic inverse retains $T/(4\pi r)$ from the bosonic zero Matsubara mode. The antiperiodic inverse decays as $Te^{-\pi Tr}/(2\pi r)$. Thus the two temporal spin structures lead to different long-distance field correlations.

The antiperiodic scalar inverse is not itself the spinor propagator. For example choose auxiliary Euclidean matrices $\Gamma_4=\gamma^0$, $\Gamma_i=-i\gamma^i$, so $\{\Gamma_a,\Gamma_b\}=2\delta_{ab}I$. The massless Dirac inverse is $-\Gamma_a\partial_aD_{AP}$. Its momentum denominator is $\nu_n^2+|\mathbf k|^2$, with the matrix numerator required by the first-order equation. Analytic continuation gives $-(\omega+i0)^2+|\mathbf k|^2$: the free real-time poles remain massless. The lowest antiperiodic frequency $\pi T$ is not a Lorentz-scalar bare fermion mass.

## 6. A Separate Interacting N=1 Field Model

Now use one gauge-singlet chiral multiplet with canonical scalar z and Weyl field $\xi^A$, and the [Wess–Zumino component and Berezin conventions](<AST-15.md#L173-L310>). Choose real $g>0$ and

$$\begin{align}
W(\Phi)=\frac g3\Phi^3,\qquad \mathcal L=-\partial_\mu z^*\partial^\mu z +i\xi^\dagger s^\mu\partial_\mu\xi -g^2|z|^4-\bigl[gz\xi\xi+\mathrm{h.c.}\bigr]. \tag{6.1}
\end{align}$$

This is N=1, not an interaction assigned to the N=2 scalar a. The displayed on-shell component action defines the quantum model. Its loop densities below are computed on flat infinite space with ordinary thermal boundary conditions and zero chemical potential; no zero-mode-state projector is carried over from the free reference. Classical auxiliary elimination gives $F=-gz^{*2}$; no quantum equality of differently chosen auxiliary integration contours is assumed. The coupling is understood as a zero-temperature renormalized $g(\mu_{\rm ren})$, small at the scales in use. The renormalization scale is not a chemical potential.

The ordered mass convention matters. If $M=W''(z)=2gz$, then $\xi\xi=2\xi^0\xi^1$ and order-reversing conjugation gives $\overline{\xi\xi}=-2\bar\xi_{0'}\bar\xi_{1'}$. Thus the mass density is $-M\xi^0\xi^1+M^*\bar\xi_{0'}\bar\xi_{1'}$.

### 6.1 The Paired Weyl Gaussian Fixes Phases and Degrees

After $t=-i\tau$, Euclidean barred Grassmann variables are independent integration variables. In an intermediate box use $\xi(x)=(\beta V)^{-1/2}\sum_pe^{ipx}\xi_p$ and $\bar\xi(x)=(\beta V)^{-1/2}\sum_pe^{-ipx}\bar\xi_p$, followed by the volume limit for loop densities. Since $[\xi(x)]=L^{-3/2}$, each Fourier Grassmann variable has units $L^{1/2}$. For one antiperiodic Fourier pair $p,-p$ (necessarily distinct), put

$$\begin{align}
K_p=i\nu_nI+\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf k,\qquad K_{-p}=-K_p,\qquad P_E^2=\nu_n^2+|\mathbf k|^2. \tag{6.2}
\end{align}$$

The Euclidean quadratic block is

$$
\begin{aligned}
 S_{F,\{p,-p\}}={}&\bar\xi_pK_p\xi_p+
 \bar\xi_{-p}K_{-p}\xi_{-p}\\
 &+M(\xi_p^0\xi_{-p}^1-\xi_p^1\xi_{-p}^0)
 -M^*(\bar\xi_{p0'}\bar\xi_{-p1'}-
 \bar\xi_{p1'}\bar\xi_{-p0'}).
 \end{aligned}
 \tag{6.3}
$$

Choose the Berezin orientation so that the ordered top monomial $\xi_p^0\xi_p^1\xi_{-p}^0\xi_{-p}^1\bar\xi_{p0'}\bar\xi_{p1'}\bar\xi_{-p0'}\bar\xi_{-p1'}$ integrates to one. With precisely that action and orientation,

$$\begin{align}
\int d\mu_F\,e^{-S_{F,\{p,-p\}}} =\frac1{4!}\int d\mu_F\,S_{F,\{p,-p\}}^4 =(P_E^2+|M|^2)^2. \tag{6.4}
\end{align}$$

The exponential terminates at its fourth power. The eight-variable measure has units $L^{-4}$, matching the result. The sign and phase are fixed already at $M=0$, where the two frequency blocks give a positive $(P_E^2)^2$. The conjugate mass term's written minus sign is essential. Each pair is counted once, so (6.4) supplies one logarithm of $P_E^2+|M|^2$ per individual four-momentum. This is a Weyl/Majorana field with two physical fermionic states, not a Dirac field with four. No arbitrary square-root phase or doubled determinant is inserted.

### 6.2 Canonical Background Masses

Write $z=(\phi_1+i\phi_2)/\sqrt2$ and evaluate about the constant real background $(\phi_1,\phi_2)=(\varphi,0)$. The real fields have kinetic coefficient one half. The actual potential Hessian and fermion mass are

$$\begin{align}
V=\frac{g^2}{4}(\phi_1^2+\phi_2^2)^2,\qquad m_1^2=3g^2\varphi^2,\quad m_2^2=g^2\varphi^2,\quad m_f^2=|2gz|^2=2g^2\varphi^2. \tag{6.5}
\end{align}$$

At a nonzero background the scalar masses are not both equal to the fermion mass. Indeed $m_1^2+m_2^2-2m_f^2=0$ but $m_1^4+m_2^4-2m_f^4=2g^4\varphi^4$. One may not discard the entire zero-temperature determinant by invoking equal zero-point energies off the supersymmetric vacuum.

## 7. Evaluating the Interacting Thermal Curvature

Define the thermal part at the same background by

$$\begin{align}
\Delta V_T(\varphi)=V_{1\text{-loop}}(T,\varphi) -V_{1\text{-loop}}(0,\varphi). \tag{7.1}
\end{align}$$

Zero-temperature counterterms and the definition of g remain those of the chosen renormalized model. Per real bosonic degree or physical fermionic state, the finite thermal integrals are

$$\begin{align}
\mathcal V_B(m)=\frac{T}{2\pi^2}\int_0^\infty k^2 \log(1-e^{-\beta\sqrt{k^2+m^2}})dk,
\end{align}$$

$$\begin{align}
\mathcal V_F(m)=-\frac{T}{2\pi^2}\int_0^\infty k^2 \log(1+e^{-\beta\sqrt{k^2+m^2}})dk,\qquad \Delta V_T=\mathcal V_B(m_1)+\mathcal V_B(m_2)+2\mathcal V_F(m_f). \tag{7.2}
\end{align}$$

These expressions follow from the scalar Gaussian and the paired Weyl Gaussian with ordinary thermal boundary conditions. Differentiation with respect to $m^2$ yields one half of the thermal occupation integral, with a positive sign for both physical statistics:

$$\begin{align}
\left.\partial_{m^2}\mathcal V_B\right|_0 =\frac{I_B}{2}=\frac{T^2}{24},\qquad \left.\partial_{m^2}\mathcal V_F\right|_0 =\frac{I_F}{2}=\frac{T^2}{48},
\end{align}$$

$$\begin{align}
I_B=\frac1{2\pi^2}\int_0^\infty\frac{k\,dk}{e^{k/T}-1} =\frac{T^2}{12},\qquad I_F=\frac1{2\pi^2}\int_0^\infty\frac{k\,dk}{e^{k/T}+1} =\frac{T^2}{24}. \tag{7.3}
\end{align}$$

For fermions the negative thermal term in the antiperiodic inverse (5.3) is multiplied by the negative sign of the fermion contribution to the effective potential. Subtracting the same-background vacuum part leaves the positive derivative in (7.3). This sign is not obtained by replacing an ordinary Gibbs trace with an index.

Combining the actual masses, with the Weyl multiplicity two, gives the quadratic thermal term and static curvature

$$\begin{align}
\Delta V_T(\varphi)=-\frac{\pi^2T^4}{24} +\left(\frac{g^2T^2}{6}+\frac{g^2T^2}{12}\right)\varphi^2 +\text{higher background terms},
\end{align}$$

$$\begin{align}
\boxed{\delta m_{\rm curv}^2 =\left.\partial_\varphi^2\Delta V_T\right|_{0} =\underbrace{\frac{g^2T^2}{3}}_{\text{scalar loops}} +\underbrace{\frac{g^2T^2}{6}}_{\text{Weyl loop}} =\frac{g^2T^2}{2}.} \tag{7.4}
\end{align}$$

Here the derivative denotes the second derivative with respect to the canonical real background. The result is the leading one-loop thermal contribution. It has inverse-length-squared units and is positive. It is not a universal N=2 thermal mass formula: both its coupling normalization and its two-plus-two N=1 field content were supplied in (6.1).

### 7.1 Zero Matsubara Modes and What the Approximation Omits

The expansion assumes $g\ll1$ at the relevant renormalization scale and $g|\varphi|\ll T$. The bosonic zero Matsubara mode is responsible for a nonanalytic higher term. Its explicitly subtracted three-momentum integral is

$$\begin{align}
\frac{T}{4\pi^2}\int_0^\infty k^2 \left[\log\left(1+\frac{m^2}{k^2}\right) -\frac{m^2}{k^2}\right]dk =-\frac{Tm^3}{12\pi},\qquad m>0. \tag{7.5}
\end{align}$$

The subtraction isolates the nonanalytic term from the local analytic mass-squared contribution. For (6.5), the unresummed cubic term is $-g^3T(3\sqrt3+1)|\varphi|^3/(12\pi)$. Its presence does not make a truncated potential a complete phase diagram. Soft scalar modes acquire a scale of order gT; ring resummation changes the curvature at order $g^3T^2$, beyond (7.4). Higher-loop, wavefunction and running-coupling corrections also require their own calculation.

The tree potential already has its minimum at zero, so a positive leading thermal curvature here is not evidence for restoring a separately postulated broken phase. The calculation supplies a local static response, not a cosmological transition.

A thermal fermion self-energy depends on frequency, spatial momentum and the heat-bath rest frame. Rotationally invariant Weyl kernels can contain distinct identity and $\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\widehat{\mathbf k}$ structures. Determining their retarded poles requires that self-energy, not just a scalar effective-potential derivative. Neither (7.4) nor the antiperiodic frequency gap supplies a generic Lorentz-scalar bare fermion mass or a complete plasma dispersion law.

## 8. Interpretation and Remaining Inputs

The free N=2 example establishes a controlled ordinary thermal field trace, its distinct graded cancellation, a nonzero energy Ward test and explicit four-dimensional correlations. The interacting N=1 example establishes a normalized positive scalar thermal curvature for a declared action, with the Weyl determinant, same-background vacuum subtraction and infrared limitations visible. The models are separate; no interaction correction to the free vector multiplet was inferred by relabelling the Wess–Zumino field.

The [equilibrium discussion](<EC-10.md>) keeps the state and its time generator as input. The [metric–affine lift discussion](<MAG-06.md#L281-L365>) likewise prevents replacing the rigid reference by an arbitrary nonmetric background without a new coupling and symmetry construction. None of the flat calculations supplies that lift, a local supergravity system, a chiral N=2 Standard Model, a quantum-gravity thermal state or an ultraviolet completion.
