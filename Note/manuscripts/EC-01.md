# Spin, Torsion and Bouncing Cosmological Models

Einstein-Cartan gravity allows the connection that transports vectors to carry torsion. In its simplest form, torsion is fixed algebraically by the spin current of matter rather than by an independent wave equation. Eliminating it produces local matter interactions. An averaged spin-fluid model gives a useful setting in which to calculate how such interactions can alter cosmological evolution. The calculation also illustrates an important distinction: finding a bounce in a specified homogeneous model is not the same as constructing a nonsingular stellar collapse solution.

The needed background is elementary tensor calculus and the Einstein equation. The conventions are those of the [shared reference](<../CONVENTIONS.md>): signature $(- ,+,+,+)$, derivative-first connection slots, and $c=\hbar=1$ unless units are explicitly restored. Greek indices run from 0 to 3. Spatial indices run from 1 to 3. A bar denotes the Levi-Civita connection of the metric under discussion.

## 1. How Torsion Determines a Metric-Compatible Connection

A connection specifies a covariant derivative. Here

$$\begin{align}
\nabla_\mu V^\rho=\partial_\mu V^\rho+\Gamma^\rho{}_{\mu\sigma}V^\sigma,\qquad T^\rho{}_{\mu\nu}=\Gamma^\rho{}_{\mu\nu}-\Gamma^\rho{}_{\nu\mu}. \tag{1}
\end{align}$$

The torsion tensor measures the antisymmetric part of the connection in a coordinate basis. Unlike a connection coefficient, it transforms as a tensor. A metric-compatible connection preserves scalar products under parallel transport:

$$\begin{align}
\nabla_\lambda g_{\mu\nu}=\partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu}-\Gamma^\rho{}_{\lambda\mu}g_{\rho\nu}-\Gamma^\rho{}_{\lambda\nu}g_{\mu\rho}=0. \tag{2}
\end{align}$$

The metric already determines one such connection, the torsion-free Levi-Civita connection $\bar\Gamma$. Subtracting two connections removes the inhomogeneous coordinate-transformation term. Their difference is therefore a tensor, the contorsion:

$$\begin{align}
K^\rho{}_{\mu\nu}=\Gamma^\rho{}_{\mu\nu}-\bar\Gamma^\rho{}_{\mu\nu},\qquad K_{a b c}=g_{a\rho}K^\rho{}_{b c}. \tag{3}
\end{align}$$

Subtracting the two metric-compatibility equations gives

$$\begin{align}
K_{a b c}=-K_{c b a},\qquad T_{a b c}=K_{a b c}-K_{a c b}. \tag{4}
\end{align}$$

The antisymmetry is between the first and third slots, not the first two. Its position follows from the derivative-slot convention in (1).

These relations determine $K$ uniquely. To see the actual inversion, use the first relation to write

$$\begin{align}
T_{a b c}=K_{a b c}+K_{b c a},\quad T_{b c a}=K_{b c a}+K_{c a b},\quad T_{c a b}=K_{c a b}+K_{a b c}.
\end{align}$$

The first equation minus the second plus the third leaves twice the desired component. Hence

$$\begin{align}
K_{a b c}=\frac12\left(T_{a b c}-T_{b c a}+T_{c a b}\right). \tag{5}
\end{align}$$

For existence, substitute this expression into (4) and use $T_{a b c}=-T_{a c b}$. For uniqueness, the difference of two possible answers has zero torsion. Such a tensor is symmetric in its last two slots and antisymmetric in its outer slots. Alternating these two symmetries takes it to its own negative, so it vanishes.

There are 64 connection-difference components in four dimensions. Metric compatibility imposes 40 independent linear conditions and leaves 24 components. A torsion tensor also has $4\binom42=24$ components. Equation (5) is the explicit isomorphism between these two descriptions. The construction uses nondegeneracy of the metric, not a choice of timelike sign.

## 2. The Algebraic Spin-Torsion Equation

Einstein-Cartan gravity is a first-order theory: one may use a tetrad and an independent metric-compatible spin connection as variational fields. A tetrad $e^a{}_\mu$ is an invertible field of frames satisfying $g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{ab}e^a{}_\mu e^b{}_\nu$; it converts coordinate components into locally orthonormal components. The spin connection specifies parallel transport in those frames. Its antisymmetry in the frame indices expresses metric compatibility. The gravitational term is linear in curvature. Matter contributes a spin current through its dependence on that connection. This chapter takes the resulting field equations as input and fixes the spin-current normalization by writing them as

$$\begin{align}
G_{\mu\nu}(\Gamma)=\kappa\Sigma_{\mu\nu},\qquad T^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}+\delta^\lambda_\mu t_\nu-\delta^\lambda_\nu t_\mu=-\kappa J^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}, \tag{6}
\end{align}$$

where $\kappa=8\pi G$, $t_\mu=T^\rho{}_{\mu\rho}$, and $J^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}=-J^\lambda{}_{\nu\mu}$. The source $\Sigma$ is the tetrad or canonical source appropriate to these first-order equations; it is not silently identified with a Hilbert stress tensor from a different variational problem. Reviews of the variational framework are listed at the end.

To solve the second equation, it is useful to invert its left-hand side once for a general tensor. Define

$$\begin{align}
(\mathcal A X)^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}=X^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}+\delta^\lambda_\mu x_\nu-\delta^\lambda_\nu x_\mu,\qquad x_\mu=X^\rho{}_{\mu\rho}.
\end{align}$$

If $Y=\mathcal A X$, tracing the first and third slots gives

$$\begin{align}
y_\mu=Y^\rho{}_{\mu\rho}=x_\mu+x_\mu-4x_\mu=-2x_\mu.
\end{align}$$

Substitute $x_\mu=-y_\mu/2$ back into the definition. The inverse and the torsion solution are

$$\begin{align}
(\mathcal A^{-1}Y)^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}=Y^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}+\tfrac12\delta^\lambda_\mu y_\nu-\tfrac12\delta^\lambda_\nu y_\mu,
\end{align}$$

$$\begin{align}
T^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}=-\kappa\left(J^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}+\tfrac12\delta^\lambda_\mu j_\nu-\tfrac12\delta^\lambda_\nu j_\mu\right),\qquad j_\mu=J^\rho{}_{\mu\rho}. \tag{7}
\end{align}$$

No derivatives of torsion appear in this equation. Within this theory, a specified spin current fixes torsion pointwise; a vanishing current gives vanishing torsion. This statement would need reconsideration after adding terms that give the independent connection its own kinetic dynamics.

The absence of an independent torsion wave does not mean the induced interaction is zero. Schematically, substituting $\Gamma=\bar\Gamma+K$ into a curvature-linear action gives a Levi-Civita term, a divergence and terms quadratic in $K$. Solving the algebraic connection equation then produces local spin-dependent matter terms. Their detailed coefficients depend on the matter model and its normalization.

## 3. Separating Levi-Civita Curvature from Contorsion

Fix the curvature convention by

$$\begin{align}
R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}=\partial_\mu\Gamma^\rho{}_{\nu\sigma}-\partial_\nu\Gamma^\rho{}_{\mu\sigma}+\Gamma^\rho{}_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\sigma}-\Gamma^\rho{}_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\sigma},\qquad R_{\sigma\nu}=R^\rho{}_{\sigma\rho\nu}. \tag{8}
\end{align}$$

Insert $\Gamma=\bar\Gamma+K$. Terms with no $K$ give $\bar R$. Terms containing one derivative of $K$, together with the connection-contorsion cross terms, combine into Levi-Civita covariant derivatives. The remaining terms are quadratic:

$$\begin{align}
R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}=\bar R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu} +\bar\nabla_\mu K^\rho{}_{\nu\sigma}-\bar\nabla_\nu K^\rho{}_{\mu\sigma} +K^\rho{}_{\mu\lambda}K^\lambda{}_{\nu\sigma}-K^\rho{}_{\nu\lambda}K^\lambda{}_{\mu\sigma}. \tag{9}
\end{align}$$

For example, the first derivative in this expression means

$$\begin{align}
\bar\nabla_\mu K^\rho{}_{\nu\sigma}=\partial_\mu K^\rho{}_{\nu\sigma} +\bar\Gamma^\rho{}_{\mu\lambda}K^\lambda{}_{\nu\sigma} -\bar\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}K^\rho{}_{\lambda\sigma} -\bar\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\sigma}K^\rho{}_{\nu\lambda}.
\end{align}$$

Subtracting the expression with $\mu,\nu$ exchanged cancels the terms containing the symmetric coefficient $\bar\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}$ and reproduces exactly the cross terms in the direct expansion of (8). Contracting (9) gives

$$\begin{align}
R_{\sigma\nu}=\bar R_{\sigma\nu} +\bar\nabla_\lambda K^\lambda{}_{\nu\sigma}-\bar\nabla_\nu K^\lambda{}_{\lambda\sigma} +Q_{\sigma\nu}, \tag{10}
\end{align}$$

$$\begin{align}
Q_{\sigma\nu}=K^\lambda{}_{\lambda\rho}K^\rho{}_{\nu\sigma}-K^\rho{}_{\nu\lambda}K^\lambda{}_{\rho\sigma}. \tag{11}
\end{align}$$

This identity is the bridge needed to eliminate torsion from the metric equation. It does not require the Ricci tensor of the full connection to be symmetric.

## 4. A Spin-Fluid Closure and Its Effective Source

A Weyssenhoff-type constitutive relation transports an antisymmetric spin density with the fluid velocity:

$$\begin{align}
J^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}=u^\lambda s_{\mu\nu},\qquad s_{\mu\nu}=-s_{\nu\mu},\qquad s_{\mu\nu}u^\nu=0,\qquad u^\mu u_\mu=-1. \tag{12}
\end{align}$$

The orthogonality condition says that the spin density is spatial in the local fluid rest frame. It gives $j_\mu=u^\rho s_{\mu\rho}=0$, so (7) and (5) reduce to

$$\begin{align}
T^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}=-\kappa u^\lambda s_{\mu\nu},\qquad K_{a b c}=-\frac\kappa2\left(u_a s_{b c}+u_b s_{a c}+u_c s_{a b}\right). \tag{13}
\end{align}$$

Contracting this expression gives $K^\rho{}_{\rho\mu}=0$. Define the nonnegative scalar

$$\begin{align}
s^2=\frac12s_{\mu\nu}s^{\mu\nu}. \tag{14}
\end{align}$$

It is nonnegative because $s$ is spatial: in a rest frame it is half the sum of the squares of its spatial components. It can vanish, for example in a spinless state.

The quadratic curvature term can now be evaluated without assuming a preferred spin direction. In a rest frame the nonzero contorsion patterns are

$$\begin{align}
K^0{}_{ij}=-\frac\kappa2s_{ij},\qquad K^i{}_{0j}=K^i{}_{j0}=\frac\kappa2s_{ij}.
\end{align}$$

In $Q_{ij}$, the terms with one summed index equal to zero cancel pairwise. The mixed components vanish. The time-time component is

$$\begin{align}
Q_{00}=-\sum_{i,j}K^i{}_{0j}K^j{}_{i0} =-\frac{\kappa^2}{4}\sum_{i,j}s_{ij}s_{ji} =\frac{\kappa^2}{2}s^2.
\end{align}$$

These components reconstruct the tensor statement

$$\begin{align}
Q_{\mu\nu}=\frac{\kappa^2}{2}s^2u_\mu u_\nu,\qquad Q=g^{\mu\nu}Q_{\mu\nu}=-\frac{\kappa^2}{2}s^2. \tag{15}
\end{align}$$

To obtain a homogeneous effective model, an averaging prescription is needed in addition to this local algebra. We work on a prescribed coarse-grained metric with a fixed macroscopic velocity field. Fluctuations of these fields and additional nonlinear corrections from averaging the geometry are neglected. In particular, $\langle s^2u_\mu u_\nu\rangle=\langle s^2\rangle u_\mu u_\nu$. On this mean background we adopt an unpolarized closure with three further properties: the mean spin and the relevant mean terms linear in spin vanish; averaging commutes with the Levi-Civita derivatives occurring in (10); and the averaged canonical source is represented by a perfect fluid,

$$\begin{align}
\langle\Sigma_{\mu\nu}\rangle=(\varepsilon+p)u_\mu u_\nu+p g_{\mu\nu}. \tag{16}
\end{align}$$

These are constitutive and coarse-graining assumptions. They are not consequences of metric compatibility, and need not describe a polarized or inhomogeneous fluid. Below, $s^2$ denotes the averaged quadratic scalar. A zero mean spin does not imply a zero mean square.

Using (10) in (6), discarding only the linear terms removed by this specified closure, and moving the quadratic curvature contribution to the right gives

$$\begin{align}
\bar G_{\mu\nu}=\kappa T^{\mathrm{eff}}_{\mu\nu},\qquad T^{\mathrm{eff}}_{\mu\nu}=(\varepsilon+p)u_\mu u_\nu+p g_{\mu\nu} -\frac1\kappa\left(Q_{\mu\nu}-\tfrac12g_{\mu\nu}Q\right).
\end{align}$$

Equation (15) therefore gives

$$\begin{align}
T^{\mathrm{eff}}_{\mu\nu}= \left(\varepsilon+p-\frac{\kappa s^2}{2}\right)u_\mu u_\nu +\left(p-\frac{\kappa s^2}{4}\right)g_{\mu\nu}. \tag{17}
\end{align}$$

Compare this with $(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}+p_{\mathrm{eff}})u_\mu u_\nu+p_{\mathrm{eff}}g_{\mu\nu}$, or contract with $u^\mu u^\nu$ and $h^{\mu\nu}/3$, where $h^{\mu\nu}=g^{\mu\nu}+u^\mu u^\nu$ is the rest-space projector. Both procedures give

$$\begin{align}
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}=\varepsilon-\frac{\kappa s^2}{4},\qquad p_{\mathrm{eff}}=p-\frac{\kappa s^2}{4}. \tag{18}
\end{align}$$

The spin contribution in this closure has equal negative energy density and pressure. This is the input needed for the cosmological calculation; no unaveraged gradient stress tensor is required.

## 5. Conservation and Cosmological Evolution

Take a Friedmann-Lemaitre-Robertson-Walker metric in cosmic proper time,

$$\begin{align}
\mathrm ds^2=-\mathrm dt^2+a(t)^2\left(\frac{\mathrm dr^2}{1-kr^2}+r^2\mathrm d\Omega^2\right),\qquad k\in\{-1,0,+1\},\quad H=\frac{\dot a}a. \tag{19}
\end{align}$$

The scale factor has dimensions of length in this convention; $r$ is dimensionless. Dots denote derivatives with respect to this $t$, not a separately defined coordinate $ct$. The relevant Levi-Civita Einstein components are

$$\begin{align}
\bar G_{tt}=3\left(H^2+\frac{k}{a^2}\right),\qquad \bar G^i{}_j=-\left(2\frac{\ddot a}a+H^2+\frac{k}{a^2}\right)\delta^i{}_j.
\end{align}$$

Using the fluid components with signature $(- ,+,+,+)$ yields

$$\begin{align}
H^2+\frac{k}{a^2}=\frac\kappa3\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}},\qquad \frac{\ddot a}a=-\frac\kappa6\left(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}+3p_{\mathrm{eff}}\right). \tag{20}
\end{align}$$

The Levi-Civita Bianchi identity requires conservation of the effective source:

$$\begin{align}
\dot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}+3H(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}+p_{\mathrm{eff}})=0. \tag{21}
\end{align}$$

For a conserved particle current, $\bar\nabla_\mu(nu^\mu)=0$ becomes $\dot n+3Hn=0$, so $n\propto a^{-3}$. Adopt the unpolarized spin-fluid constitutive coefficient $s^2=n^2/8$ in natural units. Then

$$\begin{align}
s^2\propto a^{-6},\qquad \varepsilon_S=p_S=-\frac{\kappa s^2}{4}\propto-a^{-6}. \tag{22}
\end{align}$$

If the ordinary fluid also obeys its adiabatic continuity equation and $p=w\varepsilon$ with constant $w$, then $\varepsilon\propto a^{-3(1+w)}$. These assumptions solve (21): an $a^{-6}$ contribution with equal pressure and energy is separately conserved. Particle production, entropy generation or spin polarization would require a different closure and additional evolution equations.

## 6. Radiation Plus Spin: When a Bounce Occurs

For radiation, introduce positive constants $R_0$ and $C_0$ by

$$\begin{align}
\varepsilon=\frac{R_0}{a^4},\qquad \varepsilon_S=-\frac{C_0}{a^6},\qquad p=\frac{\varepsilon}{3},\qquad p_S=\varepsilon_S.
\end{align}$$

The constants absorb the normalization of the scale factor. Define $A=\kappa R_0/3>0$ and $B=\kappa C_0/3>0$. The Friedmann equation is

$$\begin{align}
H^2=\frac{A}{a^4}-\frac{B}{a^6}-\frac{k}{a^2}. \tag{23}
\end{align}$$

### Spatially Flat Model

When $k=0$, the allowed region is $a^2\ge B/A$. The minimum scale factor is

$$\begin{align}
a_{\min}^2=\frac BA. \tag{24}
\end{align}$$

At this point $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}=0$, while $p_{\mathrm{eff}}=-2R_0/(3a_{\min}^4)$. Thus (20) gives

$$\begin{align}
\left.\frac{\ddot a}a\right|_{\min}=\frac{\kappa R_0}{3a_{\min}^4}>0.
\end{align}$$

The zero of $H$ is consequently a local minimum of $a$, not a recollapse. The metric and these fluid densities remain finite there. This conclusion concerns the specified homogeneous effective solution.

The time dependence follows from the actual differential equation. On the expanding branch,

$$\begin{align}
\mathrm dt=\frac{a^2\mathrm da}{\sqrt A\sqrt{a^2-a_{\min}^2}}.
\end{align}$$

Set $x=a/a_{\min}$ and choose $t=0$ at the minimum. Integrating gives

$$\begin{align}
\frac{\sqrt A}{a_{\min}^2}t =\frac12x\sqrt{x^2-1}+\frac12\log\left(x+\sqrt{x^2-1}\right),\qquad x\ge1. \tag{25}
\end{align}$$

Differentiating the right-hand side gives $x^2/\sqrt{x^2-1}$, which reconstructs the preceding integral. The contracting branch has the opposite sign of $t$. At large $x$, the leading term is $x^2/2$, and the usual radiation scaling $a\propto t^{1/2}$ is recovered.

### Closed Model

The spatial-curvature term must be retained when $k=+1$. Multiplying (23) by $a^6$ and setting $y=a^2$, a turning point satisfies

$$\begin{align}
y^2-Ay+B=0.
\end{align}$$

For $A^2>4B$, the two positive roots are

$$\begin{align}
y_-=\frac{A-\sqrt{A^2-4B}}2,\qquad y_+=\frac{A+\sqrt{A^2-4B}}2. \tag{26}
\end{align}$$

The allowed interval is $y_-\le a^2\le y_+$. The acceleration equation gives

$$\begin{align}
\frac{\ddot a}a=-\frac A{a^4}+\frac{2B}{a^6}.
\end{align}$$

At a turning point, $B=Ay-y^2$, so this becomes $(A-2y)/y^2$. It is positive at $y_-$ and negative at $y_+$: the first is a bounce and the second a maximum radius. If $B/A^2\ll1$, expansion of the smaller root gives

$$\begin{align}
y_-=\frac BA+\frac{B^2}{A^3}+O\left(\frac{B^3}{A^5}\right). \tag{27}
\end{align}$$

The flat-model minimum is therefore a controlled leading approximation in that limit, not the exact answer for a closed universe. The limiting case $A^2=4B$ is degenerate; the preceding two-distinct-turning-points argument does not apply.

## 7. A Density Maximum Is Not a Bounce Condition

A simple dust-like closure helps distinguish two different questions. Let the ordinary energy density be $mn$, keep $s^2=n^2/8$, and write $\alpha=\kappa/32>0$. Then

$$\begin{align}
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)=mn-\alpha n^2,\qquad p_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)=-\alpha n^2. \tag{28}
\end{align}$$

The maximum of this quadratic as a function of $n$ occurs at

$$\begin{align}
n_*=\frac m{2\alpha},\qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff,max}}=\frac{m^2}{4\alpha}. \tag{29}
\end{align}$$

A flat homogeneous bounce instead requires $H=0$, hence $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}=0$. The nonzero solution is

$$\begin{align}
n_b=\frac m\alpha=2n_*. \tag{30}
\end{align}$$

Its acceleration is positive because $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}+3p_{\mathrm{eff}}<0$ there. The ordinary matter density $mn_b$ is not the maximum of the *effective* density in (29).

To restore SI units, use $\kappa_{\mathrm{SI}}=8\pi G/c^4$. If $j_{ab}$ denotes physical angular-momentum density, the curvature-source convention used here corresponds to $s_{ab}=c\,j_{ab}$; the scalar closure is then $s^2=(\hbar c n)^2/8$. Restoring units in this particular quadratic gives

$$\begin{align}
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)=mc^2n-\frac{\pi G\hbar^2}{4c^2}n^2,
\end{align}$$

$$\begin{align}
n_*=\frac{2mc^4}{\pi G\hbar^2},\quad n_b=\frac{4mc^4}{\pi G\hbar^2},\quad \frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff,max}}}{c^2}=\frac{m^2c^4}{\pi G\hbar^2}. \tag{31}
\end{align}$$

For $m=1.67\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}$, the last quantity is about $9.66\times10^{57}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$. This is an illustration of the quadratic model, not a universal upper density of matter. A gas driven to extreme density generally cannot remain pressureless and nonrelativistic; its equation of state, correlations and particle content have to be supplied before using such a number as a physical prediction.

The distinction also matters for singularity theorems. In an effective perfect fluid,

$$\begin{align}
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}+p_{\mathrm{eff}}=\varepsilon+p-\frac{\kappa s^2}{2},\qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}+3p_{\mathrm{eff}}=\varepsilon+3p-\kappa s^2. \tag{32}
\end{align}$$

The first controls null convergence through the Einstein equation: for a null vector $k^\mu$,

$$\begin{align}
\bar R_{\mu\nu}k^\mu k^\nu=\kappa(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}+p_{\mathrm{eff}})(u_\mu k^\mu)^2.
\end{align}$$

The second enters timelike focusing in the isotropic setting. Penrose's trapped-surface theorem uses a null-convergence hypothesis, together with its causal and global assumptions; it is not invalidated merely by checking the second expression. Failure of a theorem's hypothesis removes that particular implication, but is not itself a proof of geodesic completeness. The bounce argument above supplies its own local dynamical calculation instead.

## 8. Black-Hole Interiors and a Regular Metric Example

For the Schwarzschild geometry, write $\mu=GM/c^2$ for the geometric mass. In units $c=1$,

$$\begin{align}
\mathrm ds^2=-f(r)\mathrm dt^2+f(r)^{-1}\mathrm dr^2+r^2\mathrm d\Omega^2,\qquad f(r)=1-\frac{2\mu}r. \tag{33}
\end{align}$$

The norm of the gradient of the areal radius is $g^{ab}\partial_a r\partial_b r=g^{rr}=f$. Thus the radius gradient is spacelike outside $r=2\mu$ and timelike inside. The coordinate $r$ can play a temporal role in the interior patch. The singular limit $r=0$ is a boundary of the Schwarzschild spacetime, not a regular hypersurface already included in the manifold.

To replace a collapsing interior by an Einstein-Cartan bounce, one would have to provide the interior matter solution, match it to an exterior, and analyze the resulting global geometry. The homogeneous solutions in Sections 5-7 do not perform that construction. A useful separate example of finite central curvature is instead supplied by the static Hayward metric,

$$\begin{align}
f(r)=1-\frac{2\mu r^2}{r^3+2\mu\ell^2},\qquad \mu>0,\quad\ell>0. \tag{34}
\end{align}$$

This is a specified effective geometry, not a derived solution of the spin-fluid equations above. At small radius,

$$\begin{align}
f(r)=1-\frac{r^2}{\ell^2}+O(r^5).
\end{align}$$

The leading metric is de Sitter, with $\Lambda_{\mathrm{core}}=3/\ell^2$. Its effective stress has positive energy and $p=-\varepsilon$, unlike the negative stiff contribution $p_S=\varepsilon_S<0$ in (22). The two descriptions should not be identified by an equation-of-state argument.

For the static spherical metric in (33), direct curvature contraction gives the Kretschmann scalar

$$\begin{align}
\mathcal K=R_{abcd}R^{abcd}=(f'')^2+\frac4{r^2}(f')^2+\frac4{r^4}(f-1)^2. \tag{35}
\end{align}$$

The three terms make the central limit transparent. Substituting $f=1-r^2/\ell^2+O(r^5)$ gives respectively $4/\ell^4$, $16/\ell^4$ and $4/\ell^4$, so

$$\begin{align}
\lim_{r\to0}\mathcal K=\frac{24}{\ell^4}. \tag{36}
\end{align}$$

Schwarzschild instead gives $\mathcal K=48\mu^2/r^6$. If $\mu=1$ and $\ell=0.2$ in one consistent length unit, the Hayward horizon equation is $r^3-2r^2+0.08=0$. Its two positive roots are approximately 0.211495 and 1.979585. Finite central curvature is a useful local diagnostic, but does not replace analysis of extension, global completeness, matter sourcing or inner-horizon stability.

## 9. Results to Retain for Subsequent Calculations

Metric compatibility and torsion determine the connection through the explicitly inverted relation (5). With the field equations and normalization in (6), the spin-torsion relation is algebraic and is solved by (7). The curvature identity (10) then separates the Levi-Civita geometry from linear and quadratic contorsion terms.

A specified unpolarized spin-fluid closure converts that algebra into the effective source (18). Conserved particle number makes its negative stiff contribution grow as $a^{-6}$, so a homogeneous radiation-plus-spin model admits the bounces computed in Section 6. The exact turning points depend on spatial curvature. A maximum of an effective-density function, a dynamical bounce, finite curvature of a chosen metric, and a global nonsingularity theorem are distinct statements with distinct requirements.
