# Equilibrium, Nonequilibrium Matter and Spin-Fluid Models

*Study draft.*

A thermal state requires a Hamiltonian, a set of conserved charges and an ensemble. A spin-fluid model additionally requires a prescription for averaging microscopic spin observables and closing their correlations. These are separate inputs. An ideal-gas partition function does not determine a universal torsion stress, an interacting phase diagram or a cosmological bounce.

This chapter builds finite grand-canonical examples first, then takes a controlled free-gas thermodynamic limit. It derives thermal boundary identities, a stationary curved-equilibrium condition and an exact finite nonequilibrium response example. Finally it gives a regulated spin-channel model whose exact partition function can be compared with a mean-field closure.

Use the [shared conventions](<../CONVENTIONS.md>) and the action/quantization dictionaries in [EC-02](<EC-02.md>) and [EC-03](<EC-03.md>). The metric has signature $(-,+,+,+)$; $c=\hbar=1$, and here also $k_B=1$. Temperature and chemical potential have dimensions of energy.

## 1. Begin with a Finite Grand-Canonical System

Take a finite set of fermion orbitals i with occupations $n_i=0,1$. An orbital includes its momentum, spin and particle-species label. Let

$$\begin{align}
H=E_0+\sum_i\epsilon_i n_i,\qquad Q_A=\sum_i q_{Ai}n_i,\qquad K=H-\sum_A\mu_AQ_A.
\end{align}$$

The charges in this construction commute with H and with each other. At $T>0$,

$$\begin{align}
\rho=Z^{-1}e^{-\beta K},\qquad \beta=1/T,\qquad Z=e^{-\beta E_0}\prod_i(1+e^{-\beta\xi_i}),\quad \xi_i=\epsilon_i-\sum_A\mu_Aq_{Ai}. \tag{1.1}
\end{align}$$

The product follows by summing the two states of each orbital. It is finite for all real chemical potentials because a fermion orbital has only two occupation values. A periodic box together with a momentum cutoff provides one explicit field-theory regulator of this form.

The one-orbital probabilities give

$$\begin{align}
f_i=\langle n_i\rangle=\frac1{e^{\beta\xi_i}+1},\qquad \langle (n_i-f_i)^2\rangle=f_i(1-f_i). \tag{1.2}
\end{align}$$

Thus Fermi suppression of occupation fluctuations follows from $n_i^2=n_i$, not from a continuum integral. Define $\Omega=-T\log Z$. Direct differentiation gives

$$\begin{align}
\langle Q_A\rangle=-\partial_{\mu_A}\Omega,\qquad S=-\langle \log\rho\rangle=\log Z+\beta\langle K\rangle,\qquad U=\Omega+TS+\sum_A\mu_A\langle Q_A\rangle. \tag{1.3}
\end{align}$$

For commuting conserved charges,

$$\begin{align}
\frac{\partial\langle Q_A\rangle}{\partial\mu_B} =\beta\langle \Delta Q_A\Delta Q_B\rangle. \tag{1.4}
\end{align}$$

This matrix is positive semidefinite. The equal-time covariance formula uses commutation with K. A source coupled to an operator that does not commute with the Hamiltonian has an imaginary-time susceptibility instead; Section 6 gives an explicit example.

At finite volume the mechanical pressure is $P=-(\partial\Omega/\partial V)_{T,\mu}$, with the variation of energy levels and regulator specified. The relation $P=-\Omega/V$ additionally uses an extensive thermodynamic limit. It should not be imposed on an arbitrary finite cavity spectrum.

For comparison, one Bose orbital has $Z=(1-e^{-\beta\xi})^{-1}$ and $f=(e^{\beta\xi}-1)^{-1}$, provided $\xi>0$. Its occupation space is infinite, even though it is one orbital. A chemical potential reaching a zero-mode threshold requires separate analysis.

### Which Chemical Potentials Are Allowed?

For a stationary density matrix, $[H,\rho]=0$. Assigning a chemical potential to a genuinely conserved charge ensures this condition. A maximum-entropy state constrained by a nonconserved observable can be an initial or quasistationary ansatz, but it is not automatically an equilibrium Gibbs state of the physical H.

When reactions occur, their chemical potentials obey the reaction constraints. For $1+2\leftrightarrow3+4$, energy conservation and

$$\begin{align}
\frac{f_i}{1-f_i}=e^{-\beta(E_i-\mu_i)}
\end{align}$$

show that the forward and reverse occupation factors agree precisely when $\mu_1+\mu_2=\mu_3+\mu_4$. Charges conserved by all reactions supply consistent assignments $\mu_i=\sum_Aq_{Ai}\mu_A$.

For a relativistic particle and antiparticle in chemical equilibrium, the net conserved charge has opposite signs, so their chemical potentials are $\mu$ and $-\mu$. Their sum of occupation numbers need not be conserved by pair-creating interactions. A photon number chemical potential is zero in full equilibrium with number-changing processes, even though the isolated free Hamiltonian conserves each mode occupation.

## 2. A Correctly Counted Massless Dirac Gas

Choose the normal-ordered free Dirac Hamiltonian and global phase charge in a flat periodic box,

$$\begin{align}
H=\sum_{\mathbf p,s}E_{\mathbf p}(b_{\mathbf p s}^\dagger b_{\mathbf p s}+d_{\mathbf p s}^\dagger d_{\mathbf p s}),\qquad Q=\sum_{\mathbf p,s}(b^\dagger b-d^\dagger d).
\end{align}$$

There are two positive-energy spin states s. Antiparticles are a separate family, not another factor secretly hidden in s. The thermal partition function is

$$\begin{align}
\log Z=2\sum_{\mathbf p}\left[ \log(1+e^{-\beta(E_{\mathbf p}-\mu)}) +\log(1+e^{-\beta(E_{\mathbf p}+\mu)})\right]. \tag{2.1}
\end{align}$$

This example excludes the vacuum energy by its declared reference prescription. It is not a calculation of absolute renormalized vacuum stress on a curved background.

Taking the large-volume free-gas limit gives

$$\begin{align}
P=2T\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3} \left[\log(1+e^{-(E_p-\mu)/T})+\log(1+e^{-(E_p+\mu)/T})\right]. \tag{2.2}
\end{align}$$

For $E_p=p$, put $x=p/T$ and $y=\mu/T$. The radial integral is $P=T^4I(y)/\pi^2$, where

$$\begin{align}
I(y)=\int_0^\infty x^2[\log(1+e^{-x+y})+\log(1+e^{-x-y})]dx.
\end{align}$$

For $0<z<1$, expanding the logarithm and integrating $x^2e^{-nx}$ gives $\int_0^\infty x^2\log(1+ze^{-x})dx=-2\operatorname{Li}_4(-z)$. Both sides continue along positive real z without crossing a singularity. One can then evaluate the pair without an expansion restricted to small y by using

$$\begin{align}
F_2(y)=\operatorname{Li}_2(-e^y)+\operatorname{Li}_2(-e^{-y}),\qquad F_2'(y)=-\log(1+e^y)+\log(1+e^{-y})=-y.
\end{align}$$

For real y, $F_2(0)=-\pi^2/6$, hence $F_2=-y^2/2-\pi^2/6$. Define $F_4(y)=\operatorname{Li}_4(-e^y)+\operatorname{Li}_4(-e^{-y})$. It satisfies $F_4''=F_2$, $F_4'(0)=0$ and $F_4(0)=-7\pi^4/360$. Therefore

$$\begin{align}
F_4=-\frac{y^4}{24}-\frac{\pi^2y^2}{12}-\frac{7\pi^4}{360},\qquad I=-2F_4.
\end{align}$$

The correctly normalized pressure is

$$\begin{align}
P_D=\frac{7\pi^2}{180}T^4+\frac{\mu^2T^2}{6}+\frac{\mu^4}{12\pi^2}. \tag{2.3}
\end{align}$$

Differentiating it gives the net charge density, entropy density and energy density:

$$\begin{align}
n_Q=\frac{\mu T^2}{3}+\frac{\mu^3}{3\pi^2},\qquad s=\frac{7\pi^2}{45}T^3+\frac{\mu^2T}{3},\qquad \varepsilon=-P+Ts+\mu n_Q=3P,
\end{align}$$

$$\begin{align}
\chi_Q=\frac{\partial n_Q}{\partial\mu}=\frac{T^2}{3}+\frac{\mu^2}{\pi^2}. \tag{2.4}
\end{align}$$

At $\mu=0$ the net charge is zero, but the particle-plus-antiparticle density is not:

$$\begin{align}
n_{\mathrm{exc}}=4\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac1{e^{p/T}+1} =\frac{3\zeta(3)}{\pi^2}T^3,\qquad \varepsilon=\frac{7\pi^2}{60}T^4.
\end{align}$$

A zero net charge is not an empty gas, and a relativistic gas does not have the dust energy density $mn_{\mathrm{exc}}$. For ideal ultrarelativistic species at negligible chemical potentials, the useful counting rule is

$$\begin{align}
P=\frac{\pi^2T^4}{90}g_*,\qquad g_*=g_B+\frac78g_F,
\end{align}$$

where $g_B,g_F$ count physical one-particle states including antiparticles. One Dirac field has $g_F=4$. These are free-gas thermal formulas; interactions, masses and curvature corrections require further calculations.

## 3. A Static Axial Source Is Not Automatically a Chemical Potential

Use the Dirac matrices fixed in [EC-02](<EC-02.md>), with $\alpha^i=\gamma^0\gamma^i$ and $\beta_D=\gamma^0$. For a free massive field the one-particle Hamiltonian is $h_0=\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf p+m\beta_D$. Its axial generator obeys

$$\begin{align}
[h_0,\gamma_5]=2m\beta_D\gamma_5. \tag{3.1}
\end{align}$$

The mass term therefore prevents the axial charge $Q_5=\int\psi^\dagger\gamma_5\psi\,d^3x$ from being generically conserved. In a quantum interacting theory, anomalous Ward identities and boundary flux must also be checked. Setting m to zero is not, by itself, a proof that every axial charge in every gauge or curved background is conserved.

A real prescribed axial background has Hamiltonian terms

$$\begin{align}
h_B=h_0+B_0\gamma_5+B_i\alpha^i\gamma_5. \tag{3.2}
\end{align}$$

This is Hermitian for real B. It changes the actual energy levels and can polarize an equilibrium state. A damping rate is additional collision or open-system information, not a consequence of making a real static source part of H.

For a finite matrix Hamiltonian, write $U(s)=e^{-sH_\lambda}$. Its parameter derivative satisfies $Y'= -H_\lambda Y-(\partial_\lambda H_\lambda)U$, $Y(0)=0$. Solving this linear matrix equation gives

$$\begin{align}
\partial_\lambda e^{-\beta H_\lambda} =-\int_0^\beta e^{-(\beta-\tau)H_\lambda}(\partial_\lambda H_\lambda)e^{-\tau H_\lambda}d\tau.
\end{align}$$

At fixed chemical potentials, with charges independent of the parameter, apply the identity to $K_\lambda=H_\lambda-\sum_A\mu_AQ_A$. Taking the trace and cycling the factors yields $\partial_\lambda\Omega=\langle \partial_\lambda H\rangle$. The susceptibility generally contains an imaginary-time correlation integral, not just an equal-time variance.

There is a useful restricted chemical-potential example. In a massless free model with no anomaly or chirality-changing process, $Q$ and $Q_5$ are conserved. Define $\mu_R=\mu+\mu_5$, $\mu_L=\mu-\mu_5$. Each Weyl chirality has one particle helicity and its antiparticle, so the total pressure is

$$\begin{align}
P(T,\mu,\mu_5)=\frac{7\pi^2T^4}{180}+\frac{(\mu^2+\mu_5^2)T^2}{6} +\frac{\mu^4+6\mu^2\mu_5^2+\mu_5^4}{12\pi^2},
\end{align}$$

$$\begin{align}
\left.\frac{\partial^2P}{\partial\mu_5^2}\right|_{\mu_5=0}=\frac{T^2}{3}+\frac{\mu^2}{\pi^2}. \tag{3.3}
\end{align}$$

If a constant temporal source enters as $H_B=H_0+B_0Q_5$ in this commuting model, its combination with $-\mu_5Q_5$ is $-(\mu_5-B_0)Q_5$. The sign and equivalence follow from that specified Hamiltonian. For $m\ne0$ the same source is still a valid Hamiltonian parameter, but a generic independent equilibrium chemical potential for $Q_5$ is not.

If chirality relaxes slowly, a time-dependent $\mu_5(t)$ can be a useful quasistationary state parameter during $\tau_{\mathrm{coll}}\ll t\ll\tau_5$. The separation of equilibration and charge-relaxation times is then an assumption to check, not a new exact conservation law.

## 4. Stationary Curved Equilibrium and Tolman–Klein Relations

Consider a static region

$$\begin{align}
ds^2=-N(\mathbf x)^2dt^2+h_{ij}(\mathbf x)dx^idx^j,\qquad N>0,\qquad \xi^\mu=(\partial_t)^\mu.
\end{align}$$

Assume stationary background coefficients and boundary conditions, a well-defined equilibrium Hamiltonian $H_\xi$ generating this Killing time, and conserved global charges. A finite regulated Gibbs state is

$$\begin{align}
\rho=Z^{-1}\exp[-\beta_\infty(H_\xi-\mu_\infty Q)]. \tag{4.1}
\end{align}$$

For a metric-geodesic quasiparticle, the conserved energy is $E_\xi=-p_\mu\xi^\mu$. A static observer has $u^\mu=\xi^\mu/N$ and measures $E_{\mathrm{loc}}=-u\cdot p>0$, so $E_\xi=NE_{\mathrm{loc}}$. The occupation exponent becomes

$$\begin{align}
\beta_\infty(NE_{\mathrm{loc}}-\mu_\infty q) =\frac{E_{\mathrm{loc}}-\mu_{\mathrm{loc}}q}{T_{\mathrm{loc}}},\qquad NT_{\mathrm{loc}}=T_\infty,\quad N\mu_{\mathrm{loc}}=\mu_\infty. \tag{4.2}
\end{align}$$

Thus $\mu/T$ is spatially constant. These relations assume the stated conserved charge and no electrostatic gauge potential; with one present the relevant equilibrium combination is electrochemical and must be defined consistently. An arbitrary externally prescribed axial field is not required to obey a chemical-potential redshift law merely because it appears in an energy level.

The local Fermi distribution has the sign

$$\begin{align}
f_{\mathrm{eq}}(x,p)=\left[\exp\left(\frac{-u\cdot p-\mu_{\mathrm{loc}}q}{T_{\mathrm{loc}}}\right)+1\right]^{-1}.
\end{align}$$

The positive local energy is $-u\cdot p$ in the negative-time metric. On collisionless metric-geodesic trajectories,

$$\begin{align}
p^\nu\bar\nabla_\nu(p_\mu\xi^\mu) =p^\mu p^\nu\bar\nabla_{(\nu}\xi_{\mu)}=0,
\end{align}$$

so the Killing exponent is transported without change. Chemical reaction constraints then supply collision balance.

For a local equation of state satisfying $dp=s\,dT+n\,d\mu$ and $\varepsilon+p=Ts+\mu n$, the same redshift gives

$$\begin{align}
\partial_i p=-(\varepsilon+p)\partial_i\log N. \tag{4.3}
\end{align}$$

An ideal massless gas has $p(x)=p_\infty/N^4$, verifying this equation with $\varepsilon=3p$. In the weak-field limit $N\simeq1+\Phi$, the pressure decreases in the direction of increasing gravitational potential.

Using the flat ideal-gas equation of state locally is a leading local-equilibrium approximation. Thermal wavelengths, mean free paths, geometric variation scales and boundaries must permit it. Exact curved-space thermodynamics instead uses the actual global spectrum or thermal correlation functions. A local curvature term in a squared wave operator is not a universal additive shift of every energy eigenvalue. In infinite volume a thermal state is described by the appropriate thermodynamic/KMS limit rather than a trace-class density matrix with a finite global Z.

## 5. Deriving KMS and Matsubara Identities from One Mode

### Fermionic Boundary Condition

Let $c,c^\dagger$ be one fermion pair, $n=c^\dagger c$, and $K=\xi n$. Imaginary-time evolution uses K:

$$\begin{align}
c(\tau)=e^{\tau K}ce^{-\tau K}=e^{-\xi\tau}c,\qquad f=\frac1{e^{\beta\xi}+1}.
\end{align}$$

Ordinary trace cyclicity gives

$$\begin{align}
\langle c(\tau+\beta)c^\dagger\rangle=\langle c^\dagger c(\tau)\rangle. \tag{5.1}
\end{align}$$

There is no extra minus sign in trace cyclicity. The minus sign enters the fermionic time ordering. Define $G(\tau)=-\langle T_\tau c(\tau)c^\dagger(0)\rangle$. Then

$$
G(\tau)=\begin{cases}
-(1-f)e^{-\xi\tau},&0<\tau<\beta,\\
+fe^{-\xi\tau},&-\beta<\tau<0.
\end{cases}
$$

Since $(1-f)e^{-\beta\xi}=f$, $G(\tau+\beta)=-G(\tau)$. The allowed Fourier frequencies are $\omega_n=(2n+1)\pi T$. Integrating the first branch gives

$$\begin{align}
G(i\omega_n)=\int_0^\beta e^{i\omega_n\tau}G(\tau)d\tau =\frac1{i\omega_n-\xi}. \tag{5.2}
\end{align}$$

Here the boundary exponential is $e^{i\omega_n\beta}=-1$. Using H instead of K for a charged operator would move the chemical potential into the KMS twist; it cannot simply be dropped.

### Bosonic Oscillator and a Sum Identity

For a harmonic oscillator of frequency $\omega>0$ at zero chemical potential, let $q=(a+a^\dagger)/\sqrt{2\omega}$ and $n_B=(e^{\beta\omega}-1)^{-1}$. For $0<\tau<\beta$,

$$\begin{align}
D(\tau)=\langle T_\tau q(\tau)q(0)\rangle =\frac{(n_B+1)e^{-\omega\tau}+n_Be^{\omega\tau}}{2\omega}.
\end{align}$$

Using $n_Be^{\beta\omega}=n_B+1$ makes D periodic. With $\nu_n=2n\pi T$, direct integration gives $D(i\nu_n)=1/(\nu_n^2+\omega^2)$. Its continuous periodic Fourier series at zero therefore yields

$$\begin{align}
T\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}\frac1{\nu_n^2+\omega^2} =D(0)=\frac{2n_B+1}{2\omega} =\frac1{2\omega}\coth\frac{\beta\omega}{2}. \tag{5.3}
\end{align}$$

The coefficients decay as $n^{-2}$, so this equal-time Fourier sum is convergent. The one-half in $n_B+1/2$ is the oscillator's vacuum fluctuation, not a statement that a renormalized quantum-field vacuum energy has been calculated.

## 6. An Exact Finite Response and Closed-Time-Path Example

### A Noncommuting Observable

Take a two-level system

$$\begin{align}
H_0=\frac{\Delta}{2}\sigma_z,\quad \Delta>0,\qquad \rho_0=\frac{I-\tanh(\beta\Delta/2)\sigma_z}{2},\qquad X=\sigma_x.
\end{align}$$

Here $\sigma_z=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1)$ and $\sigma_x$ exchanges the two basis states. It has $\langle X\rangle_0=0$, $\langle X^2\rangle_0=1$. Perturb it by $H'(t)=-h(t)X$. Expanding the interaction-picture evolution to first order gives

$$\begin{align}
\delta\langle X(t)\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt'\,\chi_R(t-t')h(t'),\qquad \chi_R(t)=i\theta(t)\langle [X(t),X(0)]\rangle_0. \tag{6.1}
\end{align}$$

The sign belongs to the chosen negative source coupling. Explicit evolution gives

$$\begin{align}
X(t)=\sigma_x\cos\Delta t-\sigma_y\sin\Delta t,\quad \chi_R(t)=2\theta(t)\tanh(\beta\Delta/2)\sin\Delta t.
\end{align}$$

The static susceptibility, defined with the retarded convergence prescription, is

$$\begin{align}
\chi_{\mathrm{stat}}=\frac{2}{\Delta}\tanh\frac{\beta\Delta}{2} =\int_0^\beta\langle X(\tau)X(0)\rangle_0d\tau. \tag{6.2}
\end{align}$$

It differs from $\beta\,\mathrm{Var}(X)=\beta$. The observable does not commute with $H_0$. Direct differentiation of the exact equilibrium result $\langle X\rangle_h=(h/\Omega_h)\tanh(\beta\Omega_h)$, $\Omega_h=\sqrt{\Delta^2/4+h^2}$, gives the same susceptibility.

### Fluctuation–Dissipation in This Example

Use $C^>(t)=\langle X(t)X(0)\rangle_0$, $C^<(t)=\langle X(0)X(t)\rangle_0$, and Fourier convention $C(\omega)=\int dt\,e^{i\omega t}C(t)$. If $p_g,p_e$ are the ground/excited probabilities, then $p_e=e^{-\beta\Delta}p_g$ and

$$\begin{align}
C^>(\omega)=2\pi[p_g\delta(\omega-\Delta)+p_e\delta(\omega+\Delta)].
\end{align}$$

Thus $C^<(\omega)=e^{-\beta\omega}C^>(\omega)$ on the spectral peaks. The symmetric correlator is $S=(C^>+C^<)/2$, while the response convention in (6.1) gives $\operatorname{Im}\chi_R=(C^>-C^<)/2$. Combining the two weights proves

$$\begin{align}
S(\omega)=\coth(\beta\omega/2)\operatorname{Im}\chi_R(\omega). \tag{6.3}
\end{align}$$

Indeed $S(t)=\cos\Delta t$, and both sides have coefficients $\pi$ at $\omega=\pm\Delta$. This derives the identity for a specified equilibrium system, rather than assuming it for an arbitrary driven state.

### A Finite Closed-Time Path

Let $U_h=\mathcal T\exp[-i\int_{t_i}^{t_f}(H_0-h(t)X)dt]$, where $\mathcal T$ orders real-time factors. For an initial density matrix $\rho_i$ and two sources define

$$\begin{align}
Z[h_+,h_-]=\operatorname{Tr}(U_{h_+}\,\rho_i\,U_{h_-}^\dagger). \tag{6.4}
\end{align}$$

One evolution runs forward and its conjugate runs backward in the density-matrix trace. Equal sources give $Z[h,h]=\operatorname{Tr}(U_h\rho_iU_h^\dagger)=1$ by unitarity and trace normalization. This is a concrete closed-time-path normalization identity, not a new continuum measure theorem.

For a constant pulse h starting at zero,

$$\begin{align}
U_h(t)=\cos(\Omega_ht)I-i\frac{\sin(\Omega_ht)}{\Omega_h}(H_0-hX).
\end{align}$$

Starting in $\rho_0$, the exact expectation is

$$\begin{align}
\langle X(t)\rangle=\frac{h(\Delta/2)}{\Omega_h^2}\,\tanh(\beta\Delta/2) [1-\cos(2\Omega_ht)]. \tag{6.5}
\end{align}$$

Its first derivative in h at zero equals $\int_0^t\chi_R(s)ds$. The finite isolated system oscillates: it does not relax to the new Gibbs state. Unitary evolution preserves the eigenvalues of $\rho$ and its von Neumann entropy. Irreversible relaxation needs further physical input.

## 7. Kinetic Evolution: Choose the Hamiltonian and the Bath

### The Streaming Force Is Model-Dependent

For a specified quasiparticle Hamiltonian $\mathcal H(x,p)$ in canonical spatial coordinates,

$$\begin{align}
\dot x^i=\partial_{p_i}\mathcal H,\qquad \dot p_i=-\partial_i\mathcal H,\qquad \partial_t f+\dot x^i\partial_i f+\dot p_i\partial_{p_i}f=C[f]. \tag{7.1}
\end{align}$$

Mixed derivatives show that this canonical phase flow has zero divergence and preserves $d^3x\,d^3p_i$. Converting to contravariant or orthonormal momenta changes the measure factors. A coordinate Jacobian equal to one in a particular calculation is not a substitute for specifying the phase-space measure.

For the minimally coupled scalar action with constant mass and no additional potential, the leading eikonal phase has $p_\mu=\partial_\mu S$ and $p^2=-m^2$. Since its Levi-Civita Hessian is symmetric,

$$\begin{align}
p^\nu\bar\nabla_\nu p_\mu=\frac12\partial_\mu(p^2)=0.
\end{align}$$

These rays follow metric geodesics. In a static metric their coordinate-time Hamiltonian is $\mathcal H=NE_{\mathrm{loc}}$. The action does not acquire a full-connection autoparallel force merely because another connection with torsion is available. Spinful quasiparticles in an axial texture require their actual band Hamiltonian and spin transport; no universal Abelian torsion force is imposed here.

### A One-Orbital Bath Model

To display genuine relaxation, couple a fermion orbital weakly to a reservoir and assume a Markov rate equation,

$$\begin{align}
\dot f=\gamma_{\mathrm{in}}(1-f)-\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}f =-\Gamma(f-f_{\mathrm{eq}}),\qquad \frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{in}}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}}=e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu q)},
\end{align}$$

$$\begin{align}
\Gamma=\gamma_{\mathrm{in}}+\gamma_{\mathrm{out}}>0,\qquad f(t)=f_{\mathrm{eq}}+[f(0)-f_{\mathrm{eq}}]e^{-\Gamma t}. \tag{7.2}
\end{align}$$

The rates are additional reservoir data, not derived from the free Hamiltonian. Charge may be exchanged with the subsystem while the combined reservoir-system charge is conserved.

For $0<f,f_{\mathrm{eq}}<1$, define the relative entropy

$$\begin{align}
D(f||f_{\mathrm{eq}})=f\log\frac f{f_{\mathrm{eq}}} +(1-f)\log\frac{1-f}{1-f_{\mathrm{eq}}}.
\end{align}$$

The equation gives

$$\begin{align}
\dot D=-\Gamma(f-f_{\mathrm{eq}}) \log\frac{f(1-f_{\mathrm{eq}})}{f_{\mathrm{eq}}(1-f)}\le0. \tag{7.3}
\end{align}$$

The sign follows because the logit $\log[f/(1-f)]$ has positive derivative $1/[f(1-f)]$. This is an actual entropy inequality for the specified Markov model. It is not a proof of a universal quantum Boltzmann equation on every torsionful background.

A phenomenological nonconserved internal density q, or a subsystem density exchanged with a bath, can similarly have $\mathcal F-\mathcal F_{\mathrm{eq}}=(q-q_{\mathrm{eq}})^2/(2\chi)$ with $\chi>0$ and $\dot q=-\Gamma(q-q_{\mathrm{eq}})$. Its free-energy loss is $-\Gamma(q-q_{\mathrm{eq}})^2/\chi$. Interpreted isothermally with the reservoir included, the entropy-production rate is $\Gamma(q-q_{\mathrm{eq}})^2/(T\chi)\ge0$. A reaction-diffusion extension with $D\ge0$ gives $\omega=-i(Dk^2+\Gamma)$ for perturbations $e^{-i\omega t+i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}$; stability is $\operatorname{Im}\omega\le0$. Neither D nor $\Gamma$ is fixed by an algebraic Cartan equation.

## 8. From Spin Fluctuations to a Specified Spin-Channel Model

### A Zero Mean Is Not Zero Spin Fluctuation

For two independent orbitals with opposite spin and common occupation f, set $S_z=(n_+-n_-)/2$. Then

$$\begin{align}
\langle S_z\rangle=0,\qquad \langle S_z^2\rangle=\frac12f(1-f). \tag{8.1}
\end{align}$$

An unpolarized gas can therefore have spin fluctuations. For a parity-invariant, isotropic Dirac thermal state with no axial source and no axial imbalance, the mean axial current vanishes by the stated symmetries. That does not set a regulated local product $\langle a_{5\mu}(x)a_5^\mu(x)\rangle$ to zero. Connected correlations, fermionic exchange and the definition of the coincident composite operator matter.

A spin fluid needs a coarse-graining scale, a state, spin variables and constitutive relations. To obtain its stress, vary the chosen regulated action with its independent fields fixed, then perform the stated average. The current $a_5^\mu=e_a{}^\mu\bar\psi\gamma^a\gamma_5\psi$ itself contains the tetrad; it is not an independently fixed vector in that metric variation. The [Weyssenhoff closure in EC-01](<EC-01.md>) is an additional constitutive model, not a coefficient derived from the ideal Dirac number density above.

### A Finite Regulated Model and Its Exact Auxiliary Integral

To make the approximation transparent, choose a finite set of fermion orbitals, a finite volume V, and a conserved spin-channel charge $Q_s=\sum_i s_i n_i$ commuting with $H_0$ and the other chosen charges. The regulator can be a fixed finite mode set in a periodic box. Consider the particular Hamiltonian

$$\begin{align}
H=H_0-\frac g{2V}Q_s^2,\qquad g>0. \tag{8.2}
\end{align}$$

This is a chosen global single-channel model. It is not the full local Einstein–Cartan interaction or a fixed microscopic value of its coupling. All finite-volume operators are bounded on the finite fermion Fock space.

For a spectral value r of $Q_s$, completing the real Gaussian square gives

$$\begin{align}
-\frac{\beta Vb^2}{2g}+\beta br =-\frac{\beta V}{2g}(b-gr/V)^2+\frac{\beta gr^2}{2V}.
\end{align}$$

Consequently,

$$\begin{align}
Z=\sqrt{\frac{\beta V}{2\pi g}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}db\, e^{-\beta Vb^2/(2g)}Z_0(b),\quad Z_0(b)=\operatorname{Tr}e^{-\beta(H_0-\mu Q-bQ_s)}. \tag{8.3}
\end{align}$$

The equality is exact here because the commuting charges allow the trace to be evaluated in a common eigenbasis. For a noncommuting local spin operator, a static auxiliary integral does not follow from this argument; an imaginary-time construction and its regulator would be needed.

### Mean Field and the Necessary Double-Counting Term

A static saddle uses

$$\begin{align}
\Omega_{\mathrm{MF}}(b)=\Omega_0(b)+\frac{Vb^2}{2g},\qquad \frac b g=\frac{\langle Q_s\rangle_{0,b}}V. \tag{8.4}
\end{align}$$

The positive quadratic term is not optional. For a proposed density q, the exact identity

$$\begin{align}
-\frac g{2V}Q_s^2=-gqQ_s+\frac{gVq^2}{2} -\frac g{2V}(Q_s-Vq)^2
\end{align}$$

shows both the mean-field Hamiltonian and what it neglects. Dropping the fluctuation term gives the first two terms; dropping the constant as well would double count the interaction energy.

Let $\chi_0=\beta\,\mathrm{Var}_0(Q_s)/V$. The local curvature of $\Omega_{\mathrm{MF}}/V$ at a symmetric saddle is $1/g-\chi_0$. Linearizing a probe h gives $q=\chi_0(h+gq)$ and hence

$$\begin{align}
\chi_{\mathrm{MF}}=\frac{\chi_0}{1-g\chi_0},\qquad 1-g\chi_0>0 \tag{8.5}
\end{align}$$

on that locally stable branch. This is the response of the linearized closure, not an exact universal susceptibility. A positive local curvature does not prove global uniqueness of every nonlinear saddle. A pole signals loss of control of that branch, not a demonstrated physical critical temperature.

### An Exact Finite Comparison

Take just two orbitals with $H_0=\epsilon(n_++n_-)$, $Q=n_++n_-$, $Q_s=n_+-n_-=2S_z$ and probe $-hQ_s$. Put $\xi=\epsilon-\mu$. Summing the four states gives

$$\begin{align}
Z_{\mathrm{exact}}=1+2e^{-\beta(\xi-g/2V)}\cosh(\beta h)+e^{-2\beta\xi}. \tag{8.6}
\end{align}$$

At $h=0$, $\langle Q_s\rangle=0$, while

$$\begin{align}
\left.\frac1V\frac{\partial\langle Q_s\rangle}{\partial h}\right|_{h=0} =\frac{2\beta e^{-\beta(\xi-g/2V)}}{V[1+2e^{-\beta(\xi-g/2V)}+e^{-2\beta\xi}]}.
\end{align}$$

It is finite at every $T>0$ and finite V. The exact finite partition function is analytic, so a mean-field bifurcation must not be advertised as an exact finite-system phase transition. Thermodynamic-limit claims require a specified scaling limit and additional control.

Matching a local torsion contact action to such a model would require the cutoff, subtraction scheme, Fierz/exchange channels, state and constitutive closure. No error estimate for that matching or for a Hartree truncation is supplied here. In particular, replacing g by a gravitational coefficient does not turn this example into a prediction of a universal spin fluid, condensate or bounce.

## 9. Dimensional and Modeling Checklist

For density susceptibility $\chi=\partial n/\partial\mu$, the natural energy dimensions are

$$\begin{align}
[T]=[\mu]=[b]=E,\quad [n]=[s]=E^3,\quad [P]=[\varepsilon]=E^4, \quad[\chi]=E^2,\quad[g]=[\kappa]=E^{-2}.
\end{align}$$

The dimensionless two-level observable in Section 6 instead has $[\chi_{\mathrm{stat}}]=E^{-1}$; its susceptibility is not a density susceptibility. Thus $gn^2$ and $b^2/g$ have the dimensions of an energy density. The rate $\Gamma$ has dimension E, the diffusion coefficient D has dimension $E^{-1}$, and $\Gamma(q-q_{\mathrm{eq}})^2/(T\chi)$ has the dimension of an entropy-production density per time. In the finite spin model, Q is dimensionless and $gQ_s^2/V$ has dimension E.

The free Dirac pressure, finite KMS identities and the two-level response are exact within their specified models. The local curved equation of state is a local-equilibrium approximation; the Markov rates are bath inputs; the spin-channel saddle is a stated closure of a regulated model. None supplies a universal equation of state for microscopic spin correlations. Nor do they define all renormalized composite operators or a finite interacting quantum-gravity theory.

When applying these constructions, specify the actual conserved charges, the Hamiltonian and stationary background, the regulator and vacuum prescription, the collision or reservoir model, and the spin constitutive variables. Particle production is a state-and-mode comparison as in [EC-03](<EC-03.md>), not a consequence of any nonzero curvature. Self-consistent gravity and a cosmological conclusion require their own field equations and controlled state approximation.
