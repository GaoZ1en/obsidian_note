# Free Quantum Fields on Curved and Torsionful Backgrounds

*Study draft.*

A free field is governed by a linear equation on prescribed geometry. Quantization has two distinct inputs: a conserved classical canonical structure, and a choice of quantum state. The first can be covariant without selecting a preferred notion of positive frequency. This distinction explains both why the canonical algebra survives on curved spacetime and why particle and vacuum assignments need not be unique.

This chapter constructs these inputs for real and complex scalar fields and Dirac fields, then treats physical Maxwell modes and massive Proca modes. The [shared conventions](<../CONVENTIONS.md>) and [classical actions in EC-02](<EC-02.md>) are assumed: signature $(-,+,+,+)$, derivative-first connection slots and $c=\hbar=1$. The Dirac matrices obey $\{\gamma^a,\gamma^b\}=-2\eta^{ab}$, with $\bar\psi=\psi^\dagger\gamma^0$ and $\Omega_\mu=-\omega_{ab\mu}\gamma^{ab}/4$.

## 1. What Is Prescribed, and What Must Be Assumed

Take an oriented, time-oriented globally hyperbolic spacetime without timelike boundary. Global hyperbolicity supplies Cauchy surfaces: every inextendible causal curve meets each such surface exactly once. We use the standard well-posedness and causal-propagation results for the specified linear wave or Dirac equation as analytical inputs. If a timelike boundary is introduced, boundary conditions preserving the relevant flux must be added; a local mode formula does not supply them.

Let $\Sigma$ have induced Riemannian metric h, volume element $d\Sigma=\sqrt h\,d^3x$, and future unit normal $n^\mu$, with $n^2=-1$. Currents are integrated either over compact surfaces, over periodic boxes in explicit examples, or for data whose side-boundary flux vanishes. Spacelike-compact smooth Cauchy data are a useful starting domain. Spinors additionally require a chosen spin structure and real metric-compatible frame connection.

The metric and any torsion background are fixed classical coefficients. A prescribed real axial torsion coupling keeps the Dirac equation linear. Solving the Einstein–Cartan connection equation in terms of the spinor and substituting it back instead produces a quartic interaction; that is not a free field. Section 9 separates these problems.

A stationary particle interpretation needs more than a stationary metric. The other background coefficients must share the time symmetry, and a suitable self-adjoint evolution generator and spectral splitting must exist. Zero modes, instabilities and a Killing vector that is not globally timelike can obstruct a simple ground-state prescription. No such stationary structure is presumed in the general construction.

## 2. Scalar Solution Space and Its Conserved Symplectic Form

### From the Action to the Current

For a real scalar use

$$\begin{align}
I=-\frac12\int e\left[(\partial\phi)^2+M^2(x)\phi^2\right]d^4x,\qquad P\phi=(\bar\Box-M^2(x))\phi=0, \tag{2.1}
\end{align}$$

where e is $\sqrt{-g}$ and $M^2(x)$ is a real smooth prescribed function. One example is $m^2+\xi\bar R$. The curvature here is Levi-Civita curvature. Neither the scalar action nor P contains the independent connection.

For two solutions f,g, define

$$\begin{align}
j^\mu(f,g)=f\bar\nabla^\mu g-g\bar\nabla^\mu f,\qquad \bar\nabla_\mu j^\mu=fPg-gPf=0.
\end{align}$$

The real antisymmetric bilinear form

$$\begin{align}
\sigma(f,g)=\int_\Sigma n_\mu j^\mu(f,g)d\Sigma =\int_\Sigma(f\,n^\mu\partial_\mu g-g\,n^\mu\partial_\mu f)d\Sigma \tag{2.2}
\end{align}$$

is independent of the Cauchy surface when the side flux vanishes. This follows by integrating the displayed divergence over the region between two surfaces. Its conservation does not refer to frequency or particles.

To see the canonical data in this expression, write

$$\begin{align}
ds^2=-N^2dt^2+h_{ij}(dx^i+N^i dt)(dx^j+N^jdt),\qquad \Pi=\frac{\sqrt h}{N}(\dot\phi-N^i\partial_i\phi) =\sqrt h\,n^\mu\partial_\mu\phi.
\end{align}$$

Then

$$\begin{align}
\sigma(f,g)=\int d^3x(f\Pi_g-g\Pi_f). \tag{2.3}
\end{align}$$

The momentum is a density with respect to coordinate volume. Independent compact Cauchy data f and $\Pi_f$ show nondegeneracy: a solution symplectically orthogonal to all solutions has both data zero and therefore vanishes by Cauchy uniqueness.

### The Canonical Algebra Before a Vacuum

The linear classical observable $F_f(\phi)=\sigma(f,\phi)$ has Poisson bracket $\{F_f,F_g\}=\sigma(f,g)$. Its quantum counterpart obeys

$$\begin{align}
[\hat F_f,\hat F_g]=i\sigma(f,g)I.
\end{align}$$

Equivalently, on a fixed coordinate slice,

$$\begin{align}
[\hat\phi(t,\mathbf x),\hat\Pi(t,\mathbf y)]=i\delta^3(\mathbf x-\mathbf y), \qquad [\hat\phi,\hat\phi]=[\hat\Pi,\hat\Pi]=0. \tag{2.4}
\end{align}$$

These are distributional identities. Fields at individual points are not ordinary operators whose products are automatically defined. The covariant algebra is most safely understood after smearing with test functions or Cauchy data.

Complexify the classical solution space and define the Hermitian Klein–Gordon form

$$\begin{align}
(u,v)_{\mathrm{KG}}=i\sigma(u^*,v). \tag{2.5}
\end{align}$$

It is not positive on the full complexified solution space. A Fock construction chooses a complete positive subspace with modes $u_i$ such that

$$\begin{align}
(u_i,u_j)=\delta_{ij},\qquad (u_i^*,u_j^*)=-\delta_{ij},\qquad (u_i,u_j^*)=0.
\end{align}$$

Writing

$$\begin{align}
\hat\phi=\sum_i(\hat a_i u_i+\hat a_i^\dagger u_i^*),\qquad \hat a_i=(u_i,\hat\phi)_{\mathrm{KG}}
\end{align}$$

gives $[a_i,a_j^\dagger]=\delta_{ij}$. The vacuum condition $a_i|0\rangle=0$ now refers to that chosen subspace. Changing mode phases, or making a unitary basis change within the same positive subspace, does not change this vacuum. Mixing modes with their conjugates can change it.

## 3. Minkowski Modes: Keep the Normalization Convention Visible

### Periodic Volume

Use flat spacetime with periodic spatial coordinates of period L, volume $V=L^3$, and $m>0$. This can be regarded as an infrared regulator or as the flat spacetime $\mathbb R\times T^3$. Set

$$\begin{align}
\mathbf k_{\mathbf n}=\frac{2\pi}{L}\mathbf n,\qquad \omega_{\mathbf n}=\sqrt{|\mathbf k_{\mathbf n}|^2+m^2},\qquad u_{\mathbf n}(t,\mathbf x)=\frac{e^{-i\omega_{\mathbf n}t+i\mathbf k_{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathbf x}}{\sqrt{2\omega_{\mathbf n}V}}. \tag{3.1}
\end{align}$$

At $t=\mathrm{constant}$, $n^\mu=(1,0,0,0)$. The time derivative contributes the factor $2\omega$ multiplying $u^*u$ in $i(u^*\dot u-\dot u^*u)$, and

$$\begin{align}
\int_V e^{i(\mathbf k_{\mathbf n}-\mathbf k_{\mathbf m})\cdot\mathbf x}d^3x =V\delta_{\mathbf n\mathbf m}.
\end{align}$$

Thus the modes have unit KG norm. Their conjugates have negative norm. Fourier completeness is

$$\begin{align}
\frac1V\sum_{\mathbf n}e^{i\mathbf k_{\mathbf n}\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)} =\delta_L^3(\mathbf x-\mathbf y).
\end{align}$$

This V factor is necessary for (2.4). Inertial time evolution selects the positive $\omega$ modes and the usual stationary ground-state representation in this massive box model.

### Continuum Volume

On $\mathbb R^3$, use instead

$$\begin{align}
u_{\mathbf k}=\frac{e^{-i\omega_{\mathbf k}t+i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^3 2\omega_{\mathbf k}}},\qquad \hat\phi=\int d^3k(a(\mathbf k)u_{\mathbf k}+a^\dagger(\mathbf k)u_{\mathbf k}^*),
\end{align}$$

$$\begin{align}
[a(\mathbf k),a^\dagger(\mathbf p)]=\delta^3(\mathbf k-\mathbf p). \tag{3.2}
\end{align}$$

The operators $a(\mathbf k)$ are distributions. A box Kronecker delta cannot be substituted into this expansion. With momentum-cell volume $\Delta k^3=(2\pi)^3/V$, the corresponding regulated relation is $a_{\mathbf n}=\sqrt{\Delta k^3}\,a(\mathbf k_{\mathbf n})$.

For a free complex scalar the expansion has two independent families,

$$\begin{align}
\hat\Phi=\sum_i(a_i u_i+b_i^\dagger u_i^*),\qquad [a_i,a_j^\dagger]=[b_i,b_j^\dagger]=\delta_{ij},
\end{align}$$

with other family commutators zero. Its conjugate momentum is $\Pi_\Phi=\sqrt h\,n^\mu\partial_\mu\Phi^\dagger$. On the finite-particle domain of the chosen mode representation, the normal-ordered global phase charge is $Q=\sum_i(a_i^\dagger a_i-b_i^\dagger b_i)$. No electromagnetic interaction has been introduced by this global symmetry.

## 4. Curved Scalar Modes and Bogoliubov Transformations

### FLRW Canonical Normalization

For $ds^2=a(\eta)^2(-d\eta^2+d\mathbf x^2)$ with $a>0$, choose dimensionless a and length-valued coordinates. Taking $M^2=m^2+\xi\bar R$, $\bar R=6a''/a^3$, write

$$\begin{align}
u_{\mathbf k}=\frac{\chi_k(\eta)e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}}{a(\eta)\sqrt V}.
\end{align}$$

The mode equation and normalization are

$$\begin{align}
\chi_k''+\Omega_k^2(\eta)\chi_k=0,\qquad \Omega_k^2=k^2+m^2a^2+(6\xi-1)\frac{a''}{a},
\end{align}$$

$$\begin{align}
\chi_k\chi_k^{*\prime}-\chi_k'\chi_k^*=i. \tag{4.1}
\end{align}$$

The Wronskian is constant because $\Omega_k^2$ is real. On a conformal-time slice $d\Sigma=a^3d^3x$, $n^\eta=1/a$ and $\Pi=a^2\phi'$. In the commutator, the terms from differentiating $a^{-1}$ cancel:

$$\begin{align}
(\chi/a)(a\chi^{*\prime}-a'\chi^*)-(\chi^*/a)(a\chi'-a'\chi) =\chi\chi^{*\prime}-\chi'\chi^*=i.
\end{align}$$

Hence canonical normalization survives expansion without dropping scale factors. It still does not pick which normalized solution is called positive frequency.

For $m=0$, $\xi=1/6$ and $k>0$, $\chi_k=e^{-ik\eta}/\sqrt{2k}$. The conformal mode prescription has no positive/negative mixing when compared with itself. For contrast, the minimally coupled massless de Sitter mode on the flat patch is

$$\begin{align}
\chi_k=\frac{1-i/(k\eta)}{\sqrt{2k}}e^{-ik\eta},\qquad \eta<0.
\end{align}$$

It solves $\chi_k''+(k^2-2/\eta^2)\chi_k=0$ and has the same unit Wronskian. This is a differential-mode statement for $k>0$. Infrared behavior and the zero mode must be addressed before asserting a global massless minimally coupled de Sitter vacuum; a normalized nonzero-k formula is not that construction.

### Changing the Positive Subspace

Let $u_j$ and $v_i$ be two complete normalized positive families for the same real equation. Define

$$\begin{align}
v_i=\sum_j(\alpha_{ij}u_j+\beta_{ij}u_j^*).
\end{align}$$

Taking KG products gives the Bose identities

$$\begin{align}
\alpha\alpha^\dagger-\beta\beta^\dagger=I,\qquad \alpha\beta^T-\beta\alpha^T=0. \tag{4.2}
\end{align}$$

Indeed, $(v_i,v_l)=\sum_j(\alpha_{ij}^*\alpha_{lj}-\beta_{ij}^*\beta_{lj})=\delta_{il}$; complex conjugation gives the first matrix identity. The corresponding operators are

$$\begin{align}
a_{v,i}=\sum_j(\alpha_{ij}^*a_{u,j}-\beta_{ij}^*a_{u,j}^\dagger),\qquad \langle 0_u|N_{v,i}|0_u\rangle=\sum_j|\beta_{ij}|^2. \tag{4.3}
\end{align}$$

This is an expectation value, not a claim that the u-vacuum is an eigenstate of every v-number operator. The state is generally a superposition of different v-particle occupations.

For one oscillator, the transformation $a'=\cosh r\,a+e^{i\theta}\sinh r\,a^\dagger$ preserves the CCR and has mean occupation $\sinh^2r$. The associated two-by-two matrix preserves $J=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1)$ on the pair $(a,a^\dagger)$. It is not a two-dimensional Hilbert-space representation of a Bose oscillator, whose Fock space is infinite-dimensional.

For infinitely many modes, a valid algebraic Bogoliubov transformation need not be unitarily implementable in the original Fock representation. The relevant Hilbert–Schmidt condition on the beta map is an additional analytical requirement; in a discrete orthonormal description it is $\sum_{ij}|\beta_{ij}|^2<\infty$. A finite particle number per mode is not a proof of this infinite-mode condition.

### An Exact Oscillator Matching Exercise

Consider $q''+\omega(t)^2q=0$ with constant positive frequencies $\omega_i$ before $t=0$ and $\omega_f$ after. This is a piecewise oscillator model, not a discontinuous metric claimed to be smooth geometry. The out-mode is $e^{-i\omega_ft}/\sqrt{2\omega_f}$ at late times. At early times write it as $(\alpha e^{-i\omega_it}+\beta e^{i\omega_it})/\sqrt{2\omega_i}$. Matching q and $q'$ gives

$$\begin{align}
\alpha=\frac12\left(\sqrt{\frac{\omega_i}{\omega_f}}+\sqrt{\frac{\omega_f}{\omega_i}}\right),\qquad \beta=\frac12\left(\sqrt{\frac{\omega_i}{\omega_f}}-\sqrt{\frac{\omega_f}{\omega_i}}\right).
\end{align}$$

One directly checks $|\alpha|^2-|\beta|^2=1$. For $\omega_i=1$, $\omega_f=2$, the out-occupation in the in-vacuum is $1/8$. This calculation exhibits the role of both the frequency equation and the symplectic normalization, without assigning a vacuum to an unspecified background.

### A Smooth Curved Example with Zero Mixing

Time dependence need not produce particles. For $m>0$, $\xi=1/6$ and $\rho>0$, choose

$$\begin{align}
a(\eta)^2=1+\frac{2\rho^2}{m^2}\operatorname{sech}^2(\rho\eta),\qquad \omega_k=\sqrt{k^2+m^2}.
\end{align}$$

The smooth scale factor approaches one at both ends. Every scalar mode has

$$\begin{align}
\Omega_k^2=\omega_k^2+2\rho^2\operatorname{sech}^2(\rho\eta),\qquad \chi_k^{\mathrm{in}}= \frac{\omega_k-i\rho\tanh(\rho\eta)}{\omega_k+i\rho} \frac{e^{-i\omega_k\eta}}{\sqrt{2\omega_k}}. \tag{4.4}
\end{align}$$

Differentiating verifies the equation and unit Wronskian. At early times the prefactor is one. At late times it is $(\omega_k-i\rho)/(\omega_k+i\rho)$, a unit-modulus phase. There is no negative-frequency component: $\beta_k=0$ for all k in these in/out prescriptions. Nevertheless

$$\begin{align}
\bar R(0)=-\frac{12m^2\rho^4}{(m^2+2\rho^2)^2}\ne0.
\end{align}$$

Thus nonzero curvature or a varying mode frequency is not, by itself, a theorem of nonzero production. Particle production is a scattering question for specified modes and state choices.

## 5. Dirac Fields: Positive Inner Product and Volume-Weighted CAR

### Conserved Spinor Data

Use the Hermitian Dirac action from [EC-02](<EC-02.md>) on a prescribed real metric-compatible connection. Its equation is

$$\begin{align}
i\gamma^\mu D_\mu\psi+\frac i2t_\mu\gamma^\mu\psi-m\psi=0,\qquad t_\mu=T^\rho{}_{\mu\rho}.
\end{align}$$

For two solutions, polarization of the conserved current gives $\bar\nabla_\mu(\bar\psi_1\gamma^\mu\psi_2)=0$. The positive inner product is

$$\begin{align}
\langle \psi_1,\psi_2\rangle_\Sigma =-\int_\Sigma n_\mu\bar\psi_1\gamma^\mu\psi_2\,d\Sigma. \tag{5.1}
\end{align}$$

The surface sign differs from the scalar KG formula. In an orthonormal frame adapted to $e_0=n$, it becomes $\int_\Sigma\psi_1^\dagger\psi_2\,d\Sigma$, so its positivity is explicit. Flux conservation makes Cauchy evolution an isometry on the appropriate data space; the well-posedness assumptions give its forward and backward evolution. A positive conserved norm still does not distinguish particle from antiparticle subspaces.

Choose an orthogonal splitting into families U and V with

$$\begin{align}
\langle U_i,U_j\rangle=\langle V_i,V_j\rangle=\delta_{ij},\qquad \langle U_i,V_j\rangle=0,
\end{align}$$

complete in the Cauchy-data Hilbert space. A Fock representation uses

$$\begin{align}
\hat\psi=\sum_i(b_iU_i+d_i^\dagger V_i),\qquad \{b_i,b_j^\dagger\}=\{d_i,d_j^\dagger\}=\delta_{ij}, \tag{5.2}
\end{align}$$

with the other CAR zero. Both sets of classical modes have positive Dirac norm. Negative energy is not a negative Hilbert-space norm.

### Actual Minkowski Modes

In the periodic box choose the periodic spin structure and $m>0$. Let $\alpha^i=\gamma^0\gamma^i$, $\beta_D=\gamma^0$ and

$$\begin{align}
h(\mathbf p)=\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf p+\beta_Dm,\qquad h^2=\omega_{\mathbf p}^2I_4,\qquad P_\pm(\mathbf p)=\frac12(I_4\pm h/\omega_{\mathbf p}). \tag{5.3}
\end{align}$$

The Hermitian projectors have rank two, are orthogonal, and sum to the identity. Choose orthonormal columns $u_s(\mathbf p)$ in $P_+$ and $w_s(\mathbf p)$ in $P_-$. The modes are normalized with $1/\sqrt V$, not the scalar's $1/\sqrt{2\omega V}$ when their spinors already have unit norm.

For a concrete calculation take $p_z=m\sinh r$ and $\omega=m\cosh r$. Let $\sigma_3\xi_\zeta=\zeta\xi_\zeta$, $\zeta=\pm1$, with unit two-component $\xi_\zeta$. In the [EC-02](<EC-02.md>) Weyl representation,

$$
u_\zeta=\frac1{\sqrt{2\cosh r}}
\begin{pmatrix}e^{-\zeta r/2}\xi_\zeta\\ e^{\zeta r/2}\xi_\zeta\end{pmatrix},\qquad
w_\zeta=\frac1{\sqrt{2\cosh r}}
\begin{pmatrix}-e^{\zeta r/2}\xi_\zeta\\ e^{-\zeta r/2}\xi_\zeta\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{5.4}
$$

They obey $hu_\zeta=+\omega u_\zeta$, $hw_\zeta=-\omega w_\zeta$ and form an orthonormal four-column basis. These are first-order Dirac modes, not scalar modes multiplied by arbitrary constant spinors.

A momentum-consistent field expansion is

$$\begin{align}
\hat\psi=\frac1{\sqrt V}\sum_{\mathbf p,s}e^{i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x} \left[b_{\mathbf p s}u_s(\mathbf p)e^{-i\omega t} +d_{-\mathbf p,s}^\dagger w_s(\mathbf p)e^{i\omega t}\right]. \tag{5.5}
\end{align}$$

The minus momentum label on d is explicit: the negative-energy solution in this Fourier sector has spatial wavevector p, while the created antiparticle is conventionally labeled by $-\mathbf p$. Completeness gives

$$\begin{align}
\sum_s(u_su_s^\dagger+w_sw_s^\dagger)=I_4,\qquad \{\psi_\alpha(t,\mathbf x),\psi_\beta^\dagger(t,\mathbf y)\} =\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta_L^3(\mathbf x-\mathbf y).
\end{align}$$

### Curved Slices and the Scale Factor

In an adapted orthonormal frame on a general slice, define $\delta_\Sigma$ by $\int d\Sigma_y\,\delta_\Sigma(x,y)f(y)=f(x)$. The CAR kernel is $I_4\delta_\Sigma$, not a coordinate delta with the volume factor forgotten.

For flat FLRW in cosmic time, $d\Sigma=a^3d^3x$, so

$$\begin{align}
\{\psi_\alpha(t,\mathbf x),\psi_\beta^\dagger(t,\mathbf y)\} =a^{-3}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta^3(\mathbf x-\mathbf y). \tag{5.6}
\end{align}$$

For the torsion-free example, $\chi=a^{3/2}\psi$ has the flat coordinate CAR and obeys $i\partial_\eta\chi=(-i\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\boldsymbol\nabla+\beta_Dma)\chi$. The Hamiltonian is Hermitian; its evolution preserves the mode-data inner product. On a nonstationary geometry, its instantaneous eigenvectors need not evolve into instantaneous eigenvectors. That is where a nontrivial particle/antiparticle splitting comparison can arise.

## 6. Fermionic Bogoliubov Mixing: an Exact Two-Mode Construction

For one particle/antiparticle pair let $\{b,b^\dagger\}=\{d,d^\dagger\}=1$, with other CAR zero. Set

$$\begin{align}
b'=\alpha b+\beta d^\dagger,\qquad d'=\alpha d-\beta b^\dagger,\qquad \alpha=\cos\theta,\quad \beta=e^{i\delta}\sin\theta. \tag{6.1}
\end{align}$$

Then $\{b',b'^\dagger\}=|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1$, and $\{b',d'\}=-\alpha\beta+\beta\alpha=0$. The minus sign in the second transformation is essential. The Nambu-pair matrix is unitary, in contrast to the Bose matrix preserving J.

This can be constructed on the genuine four-state fermionic Fock space $|00\rangle$, $b^\dagger|00\rangle$, $d^\dagger|00\rangle$, $b^\dagger d^\dagger|00\rangle$. A normalized state annihilated by $b',d'$ is

$$\begin{align}
|0'\rangle=\cos\theta\,|00\rangle-e^{i\delta}\sin\theta\,b^\dagger d^\dagger|00\rangle.
\end{align}$$

Direct action of the annihilators verifies both conditions. Conversely,

$$\begin{align}
\langle 00|b'^\dagger b'|00\rangle=\langle 00|d'^\dagger d'|00\rangle=\sin^2\theta\le1. \tag{6.2}
\end{align}$$

The bound is an occupancy bound for one fermionic mode. It is not a universal prediction that a specified curved background produces fewer fermions than bosons. Numerical rates require the actual mode dynamics, masses and state choices. For many modes, full matrix CAR conditions replace the one-pair identity, and infinite-mode unitary implementability again needs additional analysis.

A Weyl field requires the corresponding chiral solution space; a Majorana field requires a charge-conjugation-compatible mode pairing and only one independent particle family. Neither construction follows by promoting commuting classical-spinor bilinear zeros to operator identities. This chapter's explicit CAR construction is for the complex Dirac field; the classical graded-algebra distinction is explained in [EC-02](<EC-02.md>).

## 7. Maxwell Theory: Quantize Physical Modes, Not Four Unconstrained Oscillators

For $F=dA$, the action is $-\int eF^2/4$. Its covariant symplectic current is

$$\begin{align}
j^\mu(A_1,A_2)=A_{1\nu}F_2^{\mu\nu}-A_{2\nu}F_1^{\mu\nu},\qquad \sigma_M(A_1,A_2)=\int_\Sigma n_\mu j^\mu d\Sigma. \tag{7.1}
\end{align}$$

On Maxwell solutions its divergence vanishes. Indeed the products of first derivatives reduce to $F_1F_2/2-F_2F_1/2=0$, leaving only the equations of motion.

For a pure gauge $A_1=d\lambda$, $F_1=0$ and $j^\mu=\bar\nabla_\nu(\lambda F_2^{\mu\nu})$ on shell. Its surface integral reduces to a boundary term. Restricting to gauge parameters with vanishing boundary contribution makes these directions degenerate. The physical phase space is the constraint surface modulo these gauge directions. Nonvanishing boundary gauge transformations can carry charges; they are not discarded by this argument.

For an explicit flat-box radiative construction, exclude the harmonic zero-momentum sector and use $A_0=0$, $\partial_iA_i=0$. For each $\mathbf k\ne0$, take two polarizations with

$$\begin{align}
\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon^{(\lambda)}=0,\quad \epsilon^{(\lambda)*}_i\epsilon^{(\lambda')}_i=\delta_{\lambda\lambda'},\quad \sum_{\lambda=1}^2\epsilon_i^{(\lambda)}\epsilon_j^{(\lambda)*} =P^T_{ij}=\delta_{ij}-\frac{k_i k_j}{k^2}.
\end{align}$$

The projector obeys $(P^T)^2=P^T$, $P^T\mathbf k=0$ and $\mathrm{tr}P^T=2$. Assign $[a_{\mathbf k\lambda},a_{\mathbf p\lambda^{\prime}}^\dagger]=\delta_{\mathbf k\mathbf p}\delta_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}$, with other commutators zero. Use

$$\begin{align}
A_i=\sum_{\mathbf k\ne0,\lambda} \frac{a_{\mathbf k\lambda}\epsilon_i^{(\lambda)}e^{-ikt+i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x} +a_{\mathbf k\lambda}^\dagger\epsilon_i^{(\lambda)*}e^{ikt-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}}{\sqrt{2kV}}.
\end{align}$$

With $\Pi_i^T=\dot A_i$, this gives

$$\begin{align}
[A_i(t,\mathbf x),\Pi_j^T(t,\mathbf y)] =\frac iV\sum_{\mathbf k\ne0}P^T_{ij}(\mathbf k)e^{i\mathbf k\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)}. \tag{7.2}
\end{align}$$

The transverse projector is part of the physical canonical algebra. Four independent copies of a scalar CCR would not implement this reduction. The torus harmonic modes and nontrivial flux sectors are outside this radiative-mode construction. No BRST or general-boundary construction is being inferred from it.

In four spacetime dimensions the combination $\sqrt{-g}\,g^{\mu\alpha}g^{\nu\beta}$ has conformal weight $4-2-2=0$. On spatially flat FLRW, the same coordinate potentials and conformal-time transverse modes therefore give the free radiative action; $A_i$ has conformal weight zero. The conformal prescription yields no positive/negative mixing of these modes. This does not say that every non-conformally-flat geometry produces photons: a suitable static background can preserve its own frequency splitting. Nor does zero conformal-mode mixing imply zero renormalized quantum stress or zero response for every detector prescription.

## 8. Proca Theory: Three Physical Modes and the Longitudinal Normalization

Take the exterior-derivative Proca action $-\int e(F^2/4+m^2A^2/2)$ with constant $m>0$. It has no Maxwell gauge freedom. The equations imply $\bar\nabla_\mu A^\mu=0$. The current (7.1) is still conserved: the extra divergence is $m^2(A_1\cdot A_2-A_2\cdot A_1)=0$. Use $i\sigma_M(A^*,B)$ as the mode form.

For flat momentum $p^\mu=(\omega,0,0,k)$, $\omega^2=k^2+m^2$, choose

$$\begin{align}
\epsilon_{(1)}=(0,1,0,0),\quad \epsilon_{(2)}=(0,0,1,0),\quad \epsilon_{(L)}=(k/m,0,0,\omega/m).
\end{align}$$

They satisfy $p\cdot\epsilon_{(\lambda)}=0$, $\epsilon_{(\lambda)}^*\cdot\epsilon_{(\lambda')}=\delta_{\lambda\lambda'}$ and

$$\begin{align}
\sum_{\lambda=1}^3\epsilon_{(\lambda)}^\mu\epsilon_{(\lambda)}^{\nu*} =\eta^{\mu\nu}+\frac{p^\mu p^\nu}{m^2}. \tag{8.1}
\end{align}$$

The vector modes $\epsilon_\mu^{(\lambda)}e^{-i\omega t+i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}/\sqrt{2\omega V}$ have unit positive mode norm. Expanding the real field in these modes and their conjugates gives three independent Bose families, $[a_{\mathbf p\lambda},a_{\mathbf q\lambda^{\prime}}^\dagger]=\delta_{\mathbf p\mathbf q}\delta_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}$.

The canonical momentum is $\Pi_i=F_{0i}$, not simply $\dot A_i$. Gauss's equation fixes $A_0=-\partial_i\Pi_i/m^2$. The time-component pieces of the longitudinal polarization are essential: the coefficient in $[A_i,\Pi_j]$ is

$$\begin{align}
\frac1\omega\sum_\lambda\epsilon_i^{(\lambda)} (\omega\epsilon_j^{(\lambda)*}-p_j\epsilon_{(\lambda)}^{0*})=\delta_{ij}.
\end{align}$$

Thus the spatial canonical brackets are the usual ones, even though the spatial polarization sum alone is not $\delta_{ij}$.

For a concrete expanding-background calculation, use conformal FLRW with $k>0$ and a normalized real Fourier sector $A_3=g(\eta)\cos kz$, $A_0=q(\eta)\sin kz$, with the common spatial normalization absorbed into g,q. The reduced mode Lagrangian can be normalized as

$$\begin{align}
L=\frac12\left[(g'-kq)^2+m^2a^2(q^2-g^2)\right].
\end{align}$$

The auxiliary variable q obeys $q=kg'/(k^2+m^2a^2)$. Eliminating it gives

$$\begin{align}
L_{\mathrm{red}}=\frac12c_k g'^2-\frac12m^2a^2g^2,\qquad c_k=\frac{m^2a^2}{k^2+m^2a^2}>0. \tag{8.2}
\end{align}$$

The canonical longitudinal variable is $v=\sqrt{c_k}\,g$, including the constant mass in this normalization. Up to a time-boundary term its equation and Wronskian are

$$\begin{align}
v''+\left[k^2+m^2a^2-\frac{(\sqrt{c_k})''}{\sqrt{c_k}}\right]v=0,\qquad vv^{*\prime}-v'v^*=i. \tag{8.3}
\end{align}$$

The two transverse modes instead obey $f''+(k^2+m^2a^2)f=0$. All three can be quantized as canonically normalized modes, but their time-dependent frequencies differ. A particle comparison still needs specified in/out or other positive-frequency choices. Positivity of the reduced kinetic coefficient is a statement about this free constrained model, not a general proof about every interacting vector theory.

## 9. Prescribed Torsion Is Not the Einstein–Cartan Contact Interaction

A real prescribed axial field B can be written into the linear Dirac equation as

$$\begin{align}
(i\gamma^\mu\bar D_\mu-m-B_\mu\gamma^\mu\gamma_5)\psi=0. \tag{9.1}
\end{align}$$

B includes the chosen torsion-coupling coefficient. The principal differential operator is unchanged. Its real axial potential preserves the polarized Dirac current, and hence the same Cauchy inner product and CAR construction, under the stated flux and well-posedness assumptions. It does not select a preferred positive-frequency subspace.

A useful stationary example has a flat metric, constant spatial $B_3=b$, and momentum along z. Then

$$\begin{align}
h_b=k\alpha^3+m\beta_D+b\alpha^3\gamma_5,
\end{align}$$

$$\begin{align}
\det(EI-h_b)=[E^2-(\omega+b)^2][E^2-(\omega-b)^2],\qquad \omega=\sqrt{k^2+m^2}. \tag{9.2}
\end{align}$$

The helicity spinors in (5.4) remain eigenvectors: their eigenvalues are $+\omega+\zeta b$ and $-\omega+\zeta b$. For $|b|<m$, the positive and negative spectra stay separated for every k. The time-independent spectral splitting gives a stationary Fock prescription with no mixing relative to itself, despite nonzero external torsion. At a gap closing this simple assignment needs reconsideration. A flat metric does not require the independently prescribed torsionful connection to have zero curvature.

If instead torsion is eliminated by the minimal Einstein–Cartan field equation, [EC-02](<EC-02.md>) gives the interaction

$$\begin{align}
\mathscr L_{\mathrm{int}}=-\frac{3\kappa}{16} (\bar\psi\gamma_\mu\gamma_5\psi)(\bar\psi\gamma^\mu\gamma_5\psi). \tag{9.3}
\end{align}$$

This is quartic, not a prescribed linear potential. Replacing one bilinear by an expectation value is a mean-field approximation requiring a state, a renormalization prescription and a self-consistency equation. It is not an exact identification with arbitrary fixed B.

At a finite regulator, an auxiliary Gaussian variable can be eliminated by completing its square. That algebra explains the contact term. It does not prove that a continuum functional determinant is finite, that matter loops vanish, or that a quantum Einstein–Cartan theory needs no new counterterms. Products of spinor fields at the same point require their own definition. A free-field Fock construction cannot by itself establish interacting cosmological or singularity-resolution claims.

## 10. Two-Point Functions, Gaussian Notation and the State Input

For a chosen scalar mode vacuum,

$$\begin{align}
W(x,y)=\langle 0|\hat\phi(x)\hat\phi(y)|0\rangle=\sum_i u_i(x)u_i^*(y).
\end{align}$$

Its antisymmetric part is fixed by the canonical algebra. If $E=E_{\mathrm{ret}}-E_{\mathrm{adv}}$ for the operator P in (2.1), with $PE_{\mathrm{ret/adv}}=\delta_g$, then

$$\begin{align}
W(x,y)-W(y,x)=iE(x,y).
\end{align}$$

The causal propagator and its support are supplied by the stated hyperbolic Cauchy theory. The symmetric part of W, positivity and a choice of state are extra information. They are not determined by retarded propagation alone.

Define $D_F(x,y)=\langle T\hat\phi(x)\hat\phi(y)\rangle$ and $G_F=D_F/i$. With the present action and operator signs,

$$\begin{align}
P_xD_F(x,y)=i\delta_g(x,y),\qquad P_xG_F(x,y)=\delta_g(x,y). \tag{10.1}
\end{align}$$

For a single constant-frequency mode, $D_F(t,0)=e^{-i\omega|t|}/(2\omega)$. Its first derivative jumps by $-i$ at zero. Since the scalar P has time part $-(\partial_t^2+\omega^2)$, its delta coefficient is $+i$, fixing the sign in (10.1).

For a matrix $A$ satisfying $A^T=A$ and $\det A\ne0$, with column vectors $x,J$, the finite-dimensional square completion

$$\begin{align}
\frac12x^TAx+J^Tx =\frac12(x+A^{-1}J)^TA(x+A^{-1}J)-\frac12J^TA^{-1}J
\end{align}$$

also displays the source dependence of a Gaussian theory. For a specified Gaussian state, define $Z[J]/Z[0]=\langle T e^{i\int J\hat\phi}\rangle$. Its contractions give the formal expression

$$\begin{align}
\frac{Z[J]}{Z[0]}=\exp\left[-\frac i2\int JG_FJ\right] =\exp\left[-\frac12\int JD_FJ\right].
\end{align}$$

The integrals use the invariant volume measures. A continuum inverse requires the chosen Feynman/state boundary prescription, and determinants require regularization. A finite-dimensional Gaussian identity is not a construction of an infinite-dimensional oscillatory measure. In particular, writing $i\epsilon$ without specifying the state does not select a unique vacuum on a generic nonstationary spacetime.

For physically controlled local products, the Hadamard condition is an additional named input: the two-point function has the standard geometrically determined short-distance singularity and the corresponding positive-frequency wavefront structure. A parametrix is a locally constructed approximate Green kernel with singular coefficients fixed by the operator and geometry. Locally, subtracting the Hadamard parametrix leaves a smooth state-dependent remainder. Normalization alone does not prove that an infinite mode sum has this admissible short-distance structure. Many states satisfy this condition; it does not choose a unique vacuum. Defining $\langle T_{\mu\nu}\rangle$ then requires a renormalization prescription, with allowed finite local curvature terms. No finite value or universal vanishing vacuum stress is obtained merely by writing a mode sum or normal ordering with respect to a convenient basis.

The concrete constructions in this chapter establish canonical algebras and selected free-field mode examples. Global gauge sectors, general BRST quantization, interacting fermion dynamics and semiclassical backreaction need additional constructions; none follows from the normalization identities alone.
