# Conformal Fields and a Solvable Causal-Strip Transfer Model

**EC-11 — DRAFT study chapter; bounded review recorded.**

A conformal field theory supplies local operator products and Ward identities. A triangulation model supplies a class of discrete geometries, weights and a gluing rule. Neither construction specifies the other. This chapter develops two explicit examples: a real free scalar with chiral central charges one, and a rooted two-dimensional causal-strip model whose continuum length Hamiltonian and heat kernel can be calculated. Their normalizations make clear what extra work would be needed to couple matter and geometry.

The four-dimensional background conventions remain signature $(-,+,+,+)$ and $\kappa=8\pi G$, with $c=\hbar=1$. The conformal-field and positive transfer calculations below are **Euclidean two-dimensional models**. Their parameter $\Lambda$ is a two-dimensional area coupling, not an identified four-dimensional cosmological constant.

## 1. Which Symmetry Is Being Tested?

A diffeomorphism pulls back spacetime fields and their indices. A local Lorentz transformation rotates an orthonormal frame and transforms its spin connection while leaving the metric unchanged. A Weyl transformation instead changes the metric at fixed coordinates,

$$\begin{align}
g_{ab}\longmapsto e^{2\sigma(x)}g_{ab}.
 \tag{1.1}
\end{align}$$

A conformal coordinate map preserves a metric only up to such a local factor. These operations have different operands; invariance under one does not establish invariance under the others.

For example, consider the four-dimensional first-order Einstein–Hilbert action with invertible tetrad, metric-compatible Lorentz connection $\omega$ and fixed $\kappa$:

$$\begin{align}
I_g=\frac1{2\kappa}\int d^4x\,e\, e_I{}^\mu e_J{}^\nu R^{IJ}{}_{\mu\nu}(\omega), \qquad e=\sqrt{-g}. \tag{1.2}
\end{align}$$

Holding the independent connection fixed, $e^I{}_{\mu}\mapsto e^{\sigma}e^I{}_{\mu}$ multiplies its integrand by $e^{2\sigma}$. Even a constant rescaling therefore fails to be a symmetry at fixed coupling. Replacing the connection by its Levi-Civita value introduces derivative terms under local rescaling; it does not remove this constant-rescaling obstruction. A compensator or a different gravitational action would define an additional theory.

Masslessness alone is also insufficient. Curved-background scalar conformal symmetry requires the appropriate improvement, and the usual massless fermion or gauge examples require specified connections and couplings. In minimal four-dimensional Einstein–Cartan–Dirac theory, elimination of the auxiliary torsion gives, in the collection's conventions,

$$\begin{align}
\mathscr L_{\mathrm{axial}} =-\frac{3\kappa}{16}J_{5a}J_5^a, \qquad J_5^a=\bar\psi\gamma^a\gamma_5\psi. \tag{1.3}
\end{align}$$

The coefficient belongs to that particular Hermitian minimal action. The current has mass dimension three, so this is a dimension-six operator with a dimension-minus-two coupling. With the four-dimensional massless spinor weight $\psi\mapsto e^{-3\sigma/2}\psi$, its density scales as $e^{-2\sigma}$. It is not a marginal Weyl-invariant interaction at fixed $\kappa$. None of these observations identifies a conformal matter sector with the gravitational gauge symmetries. An interacting fixed point would require a separate renormalization-group construction; engineering dimensions do not decide its existence.

## 2. A Free Scalar: Deriving the Local CFT Normalization

Let $z=x+iy$, $d^2z=dx\,dy$, $\partial=(\partial_x-i\partial_y)/2$ and $\bar\partial=(\partial_x+i\partial_y)/2$. On a positive-definite two-dimensional metric take a real, noncompact, dimensionless scalar,

$$\begin{align}
S_E[\phi;g]=\frac1{8\pi}\int d^2x\sqrt g\, g^{ab}\partial_a\phi\partial_b\phi. \tag{2.1}
\end{align}$$

The field is inert under Weyl rescaling in two dimensions; the metric and volume factors cancel classically. On the plane the quadratic operator is $\Delta_0/(4\pi)$, where $\Delta_0=-(\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2)$ is positive. The distributional identity $\nabla_{\mathrm{flat}}^2\log|z|^2=4\pi\delta^{(2)}(z)$ fixes the Green function to

$$\begin{align}
G(z,w)=\langle\phi(z)\phi(w)\rangle =-\log\frac{|z-w|^2}{\ell^2}. \tag{2.2}
\end{align}$$

Here $\ell$ is an infrared/normal-ordering reference, not a new force scale. The undifferentiated massless field needs a zero-mode prescription; derivative observables and neutral vertex correlators are what we use. One may regulate in a finite region and take the local limit, or divide out the constant-field volume. A noncompact constant mode is not a normalized Gaussian random variable.

Differentiating the holomorphic part at separated points gives

$$\begin{align}
\langle\partial\phi(z)\partial\phi(w)\rangle =-\frac1{(z-w)^2},\qquad J(z)=i\partial\phi(z),\qquad J(z)J(w)\sim\frac1{(z-w)^2}. \tag{2.3}
\end{align}$$

The symbol $\sim$ denotes the singular part of an operator-product expansion: it is an asymptotic statement inside correlators whose other insertions stay away. Normal ordering subtracts contractions in the chosen Gaussian reference state. It does not assert that products of fields at the same point exist before subtraction.

For later sign comparisons define the Euclidean metric stress by a minus variation:

$$\begin{align}
\mathsf T_{ab}=-\frac2{\sqrt g}\frac{\delta S_E}{\delta g^{ab}} =-\frac1{4\pi} \left(\partial_a\phi\partial_b\phi -\frac12g_{ab}(\partial\phi)^2\right). \tag{2.4}
\end{align}$$

In flat complex coordinates our holomorphic stress normalization is

$$\begin{align}
T(z)=2\pi\mathsf T_{zz} =-\frac12:(\partial\phi)^2: =\frac12:J^2:. \tag{2.5}
\end{align}$$

This explicitly fixes the sign; a different metric-stress convention must convert it as well.

### 2.1 The Stress OPE, Including Its Coefficient

Write $a_z=\partial\phi(z)$ and $C=\langle a_z a_w\rangle$. Wick's theorem counts four ways to make one cross-contraction and two ways to make two:

$$\begin{align}
:a_z^2::a_w^2: =:a_z^2a_w^2:+4C:a_z a_w:+2C^2. \tag{2.6}
\end{align}$$

Multiply by $1/4$, put $C=-(z-w)^{-2}$ and Taylor-expand the remaining normal-ordered field around $w$. The double contraction is $1/[2(z-w)^4]$. The single contractions give $-:a_w^2:/(z-w)^2-:(\partial a_w)a_w:/(z-w)$, hence

$$\begin{align}
T(z)T(w)\sim \frac{1/2}{(z-w)^4} +\frac{2T(w)}{(z-w)^2} +\frac{\partial T(w)}{z-w}. \tag{2.7}
\end{align}$$

By definition the fourth-order coefficient is $c_L/2$, so $c_L=1$. The antiholomorphic construction gives $c_R=1$. These are the two chiral charges of one real scalar, not two independent real scalars. Wick's theorem supplies the Gaussian local products used here; a global state-space and sewing construction requires further analytic input [1].

### 2.2 A Primary-Field Ward Computation

Choose vertex normalization

$$\begin{align}
V_\alpha=\ell^{-\alpha^2}:e^{i\alpha\phi}:_{\ell}, \qquad \alpha\in\mathbb R. \tag{2.8}
\end{align}$$

The prefactor absorbs the reference scale so that a neutral pair has unit two-point coefficient. The contraction of $a_z$ with the exponential is $-i\alpha/(z-w)$. Inserting this in $-:a_z^2:/2$ gives

$$\begin{align}
T(z)V_\alpha(w,\bar w)\sim \frac{\alpha^2/2}{(z-w)^2}V_\alpha +\frac1{z-w}\partial V_\alpha, \qquad \partial V_\alpha=i\alpha:\partial\phi V_\alpha:. \tag{2.9}
\end{align}$$

Thus its primary weights and scaling dimension are

$$\begin{align}
(h,\bar h)=(\alpha^2/2,\alpha^2/2),\qquad \Delta_{V_\alpha}=\alpha^2, \qquad \langle V_\alpha(z)V_{-\alpha}(w)\rangle =|z-w|^{-2\alpha^2}. \tag{2.10}
\end{align}$$

More generally the local leading product is $V_\alpha(z)V_\beta(w)\sim|z-w|^{2\alpha\beta}V_{\alpha+\beta}(w)$ with the corresponding derivative terms. Integrating the noncompact constant mode enforces total charge zero in a full correlator. This neutrality and the infrared prescription are not optional qualifications of the plane formulas [1].

## 3. The Curved-Background Weyl Anomaly and the Constant Mode

A nonzero central charge does not disqualify a fixed-background matter theory as a CFT. It appears in its stress OPE and in the response to a curved background. Anomaly cancellation is an additional requirement when Weyl transformations are to be gauged, as in an appropriate worldsheet-gravity system; no ghosts or critical-string condition have been imposed here.

Take a smooth connected closed Riemannian surface, area $A$, scalar Laplacian $\Delta=-\nabla^2$ and fixed renormalization scale $\mu$. Remove its one constant eigenmode. The regularized determinant is the analytic continuation of the product of positive eigenvalues: $\log\det{}'(\Delta/\mu^2)=-\zeta'_{\Delta}(0)$, where $\zeta_{\Delta}(s)=\sum_{\lambda>0}(\lambda/\mu^2)^{-s}$ is continued to zero. With the path integral taken per unit volume of constant shifts, use the reduced determinant normalization

$$\begin{align}
W_{\mathrm{red}}=-\log Z_{\mathrm{red}} =\frac12\log\det{}'(\Delta/\mu^2) -\frac12\log(\mu^2A)+\text{constant}. \tag{3.1}
\end{align}$$

Indeed the normalized zero eigenfunction is $A^{-1/2}$, so its mode coefficient is $q_0=\sqrt A\,\phi_0$. Dividing the integral over $q_0$ by the volume of $\phi_0$ leaves $\sqrt A$, which explains the second term. Metric-independent normalization and fixed-topology constants do not affect the Weyl variation below. The original Hessian is $\Delta/(4\pi)$; its half-log-determinant differs from the one in (3.1) by $-\tfrac12\zeta_{\Delta}(0)\log(4\pi)$. Since $\zeta_{\Delta}(0)=\chi/6-1$ on these connected closed surfaces, that shift is part of the fixed-topology constant.

Use a diffeomorphism-invariant heat-kernel regulator, subtract the area power divergence and set the finite area counterterm to zero. The local scalar coefficient is the analytic input [2]

$$\begin{align}
\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma e^{-\epsilon\Delta}) =\frac1{4\pi\epsilon}\int\sqrt g\,\sigma +\frac1{24\pi}\int\sqrt g\,\sigma R+O(\epsilon). \tag{3.2}
\end{align}$$

The prime subtracts $A^{-1}\int\sqrt g\,\sigma$ from this trace. Since in two dimensions $\delta_\sigma\Delta=-2\sigma\Delta$, the finite determinant variation and the area variation combine as

$$
\begin{aligned}
 \delta_\sigma\!\left(\tfrac12\log\det{}'\Delta\right)
 &=-\frac1{24\pi}\int\sqrt g\,\sigma R
 +\frac1A\int\sqrt g\,\sigma,\\
 \delta_\sigma W_{\mathrm{red}}
 &=-\frac1{24\pi}\int\sqrt g\,\sigma R.
 \end{aligned}
 \tag{3.3}
$$

For the minus-variation stress used in (2.4), $\delta_\sigma W=\int\sqrt g\,\sigma\langle\mathsf T^a{}_a\rangle$. Therefore

$$\begin{align}
\langle\mathsf T^a{}_a\rangle =-\frac{c_L+c_R}{48\pi}R =-\frac{R}{24\pi}. \tag{3.4}
\end{align}$$

The sum of chiral charges is two, while the common charge usually denoted $c$ is one. The sign follows the stated stress and determinant conventions. The nonchiral scalar has no gravitational anomaly: the same regulator preserves the diffeomorphism Ward identity, away from insertion contact terms.

The two-dimensional curvature law $R[e^{2\sigma}g]=e^{-2\sigma}(R[g]+2\Delta_g\sigma)$ integrates (3.3) to

$$\begin{align}
W_{\mathrm{red}}[e^{2\sigma}g]-W_{\mathrm{red}}[g] =-\frac1{24\pi}\int\sqrt g\, \bigl((\nabla\sigma)^2+R\sigma\bigr). \tag{3.5}
\end{align}$$

There is no boundary in this formula. In a flat conformal patch the curvature law reads $R=-2e^{-2\rho}(\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2)\rho$. Boundary anomalies would require specified scalar boundary conditions and additional terms; they are not included by inference.

A useful normalization is a round sphere. Gauss–Bonnet gives $\int R=8\pi$ and $\zeta_\Delta(0)=\chi/6-1=-2/3$. A constant rescaling by $e^{2s}$ changes the primed half-log-determinant by $2s/3$ and the area term by $-s$, leaving $-s/3$, exactly as (3.3). Leaving out the zero-mode area factor would give the wrong result.

A four-dimensional trace anomaly involves different curvature invariants. In a torsionful fermion model, a possible Nieh–Yan-type contribution belongs to an axial Ward identity and requires its own ultraviolet prescription. It is not the metric Weyl response computed here.

## 4. Rooted Causal Strips and Their Transfer Measure

Now define a separate geometry model. Each live spatial slice is an oriented cyclic loop of $i\ge1$ links. One time step is a strip containing $i$ upward and $j\ge1$ downward triangles. A distinguished incoming edge fixes the first upward triangle. The word convention allows short loops and multiple edge identifications; it is not a restriction to strictly embedded simplicial complexes. There is no spatial splitting or merging.

Distribute the $j$ downward triangles among the $i$ gaps following upward triangles. The number of weak compositions is

$$\begin{align}
C_{ij}=\binom{i+j-1}{j},\qquad i,j\ge1. \tag{4.1}
\end{align}$$

This is the count of one incoming-marked strip words. Give each triangle a dimensionless weight $0<g\le1/2$. The weighted one-step matrix and its action are

$$\begin{align}
Q^{(g)}_{ij}=C_{ij}g^{i+j},\qquad (\mathcal T_g f)_i=\sum_{j\ge1}Q^{(g)}_{ij}f_j. \tag{4.2}
\end{align}$$

For example $C_{1j}=1$ and $C_{21}=2$. This choice of cellulations and weights is the model, not a consequence of a four-dimensional Cartan equation [3].

### 4.1 The Critical Stochastic Completion and the Zero-Length Boundary

For the absorbing completion set $C_{i0}=1$, while retaining $i\ge1$. For a generating variable satisfying $|gz|<1$, the identity

$$\begin{align}
\sum_{j=0}^\infty C_{ij}g^{i+j}z^j =\left(\frac{g}{1-gz}\right)^i \tag{4.3}
\end{align}$$

makes $g_c=1/2$ special. Define $P_{ij}=2^{-(i+j)}C_{ij}$ for $i\ge1,j\ge0$. Then

$$\begin{align}
\sum_{j\ge0}P_{ij}=1,\qquad P_{i0}=2^{-i},\qquad \mathbb E_i[j]=i,\qquad \operatorname{Var}_i(j)=2i. \tag{4.4}
\end{align}$$

Equivalently each of $i$ ancestors has a geometric number of descendants, with probabilities $2^{-(r+1)}$, $r\ge0$. These descendants are links within one spatial loop, not separate baby universes. The state zero is an **absorbing completion**: set $P_{00}=1$ and $P_{0j}=0$ for $j>0$. It is not a nondegenerate cylinder with a physical zero-link outgoing loop. On the live states $i,j\ge1$, the row sum is $1-2^{-i}$; thus the transfer is sub-Markov, meaning positivity preserving with row mass at most one. Missing mass is absorption. It must not be removed by renormalizing each live row. Write $P_+=(P_{ij})_{i,j\ge1}$ for this live restriction.

### 4.2 Why the Gluing Measure Is $1/i$

The critical live matrix obeys

$$\begin{align}
\frac1iP_{ij}=\frac1jP_{ji}. \tag{4.5}
\end{align}$$

Choose the overall normalization of the reversible measure as $m_i=1/i$ and use $\ell^2(\mathbb N_+,m)$. As an integral kernel relative to this measure, the same operator has

$$\begin{align}
\mathcal K_{ij}=jP_{ij}=\mathcal K_{ji},\qquad (\mathcal T f)_i=\sum_{j\ge1}\frac1j\mathcal K_{ij}f_j. \tag{4.6}
\end{align}$$

The factor $j$ agrees with marking the outgoing loop as well. Sewing two such kernels therefore uses

$$\begin{align}
\mathcal K^{(2)}_{ik}=\sum_{j\ge1}\frac1j \mathcal K_{ij}\mathcal K_{jk} =k(P_+^2)_{ik}. \tag{4.7}
\end{align}$$

This defines the intermediate marking/symmetry weight. By contrast $P_+$ itself composes by ordinary matrix multiplication; inserting another $1/j$ there would count the measure twice. The zero state is outside this Hilbert space and these gluing sums: there is no $m_0=1/0$.

One can evaluate the distinction exactly:

$$\begin{align}
(P_+^2)_{11} =\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{j}{2^{2j+2}}=\frac19. \tag{4.8}
\end{align}$$

The correct $\mathcal K$ gluing gives the same value. Omitting its $1/j$ gives $5/27$; adding $1/j$ to the $P$ product gives $1/12$. Marking is a normalization choice with calculable consequences.

### 4.3 Positivity as an Operator Statement

Positive entries alone do not prove a positive self-adjoint transfer. Here a Gram representation supplies the missing step:

$$\begin{align}
\mathcal K_{ij}=\int_0^\infty du\,u e^{-2u} \frac{u^{i-1}}{(i-1)!}\frac{u^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}. \tag{4.9}
\end{align}$$

For finitely supported $f$,

$$\begin{align}
\langle f,\mathcal T f\rangle_m =\int_0^\infty du\,u e^{-2u} \left|\sum_{i\ge1}\frac{f_i u^{i-1}}{i!}\right|^2\ge0. \tag{4.10}
\end{align}$$

Reversibility gives symmetry. Jensen's inequality and the sub-Markov row sums give $\|\mathcal T f\|_m\le\|f\|_m$, so it extends to a bounded positive self-adjoint contraction. The Gram representation and the reversible measure supply the operator positivity missing from an entrywise test.

## 5. Critical Scaling from an Exact Generating Function

For the absorbing completion let $F_n(z)$ be the descendant generating function of one initial link after $n$ steps. Iterating $F_1(z)=1/(2-z)$ gives

$$\begin{align}
F_n(z)=\frac{n-(n-1)z}{n+1-nz} =1-\frac{1-z}{1+n(1-z)},\qquad F_0(z)=z. \tag{5.1}
\end{align}$$

Substitution verifies $F_m(F_n(z))=F_{m+n}(z)$. Starting with $i$ links gives $F_n(z)^i$. In particular

$$\begin{align}
\mathbb P_i(j_n=0)=\left(\frac n{n+1}\right)^i. \tag{5.2}
\end{align}$$

Choose spatial spacing and time step $a$, $L=ai$ and $t=an$. For integer sequences with these products tending to fixed positive $L,t$, put $z=e^{-as}$, $s\ge0$. The exact limit is

$$\begin{align}
\lim_{a\to0}F_n(e^{-as})^i =\exp\left[-\frac{Ls}{1+ts}\right],\qquad \mathbb P_L(L_t=0)=e^{-L/t}. \tag{5.3}
\end{align}$$

The limit identifies a branching-diffusion transition law through its Laplace transform. Its infinitesimal generator on smooth test functions is $\mathcal A_0=L\partial_L^2$, equivalently the absorbing diffusion $dL_t=\sqrt{2L_t}\,d\mathcal B_t$, with $\mathcal B_t$ standard Brownian motion. The second moment in (4.4) fixes the factor two. Indeed $\mathbb E[\Delta L]=0$ and $\mathbb E[(\Delta L)^2]=2aL$. The next centered moments are $6a^2L$ and $12a^2L^2+26a^3L$, controlling the Taylor remainder on compact length intervals for smooth test functions.

The one-step absorption probability at fixed $L$ is exponentially small in $1/a$, but cumulative extinction in (5.3) is finite. Paths can first reach short loops. Dropping the zero boundary on the basis of a single-step estimate would miss this effect. Equation(5.3) is finite-time Laplace convergence. Path-space convergence additionally requires tightness, supplied by an appropriate branching-diffusion limit theorem rather than by the displayed moments alone.

### 5.1 A Finite Area Coupling

Fix triangle area $a^2/2$ and choose

$$\begin{align}
g_a=\frac12e^{-\Lambda a^2/2},\qquad \Lambda>0. \tag{5.4}
\end{align}$$

Keep zero absorbing with weight one after extinction. A strip then has the area weight $e^{-\Lambda a(L_i+L_j)/2}$. Define $(D_af)_i=e^{-a\Lambda L_i/2}f_i$. The live transfer is $D_a\mathcal T D_a$, so its leading generator is $\mathcal A_0-\Lambda L$. The real diagonal factors preserve the same reversible measure and the positive/sub-Markov contraction properties. The units are $[L]=[t]=\mathrm{length}$ and $[\Lambda]=\mathrm{length}^{-2}$. Other triangle-area/time conventions rescale these coefficients.

The limit can also be derived without truncating the transfer expansion. For $0<g<1/2$ and $f_g(z)=g/(1-gz)$ set

$$\begin{align}
\delta=\sqrt{1-4g^2},\qquad z_\pm=\frac{1\pm\delta}{2g},\qquad q=\frac{1-\delta}{1+\delta}. \tag{5.5}
\end{align}$$

The cross-ratio $(z-z_-)/(z-z_+)$ is multiplied by $q$ under one application of $f_g$. For $g=g_a$ and $\varpi=\sqrt\Lambda$, $z_\pm=1\pm a\varpi+O(a^2)$ and $q^n\to e^{-2\varpi t}$. Inverting that cross-ratio for initial $e^{-as}$ yields the full weighted Laplace transform $e^{-Lv_t(s)}$, where

$$\begin{align}
v_t(s)=\varpi\frac{s+\varpi\tanh(\varpi t)} {\varpi+s\tanh(\varpi t)},\qquad \partial_t v_t=\Lambda-v_t^2,\qquad v_0(s)=s. \tag{5.6}
\end{align}$$

The weighted atom at zero is $e^{-B_tL}$, $B_t=\varpi\coth(\varpi t)$. Removing that atom gives the live, killed transfer. For $\Lambda>0$ these are Feynman–Kac weights, not a normalized probability law: in addition to absorption there is the nonnegative area potential.

## 6. A Normalized Continuum Kernel

The lattice measure has the Riemann-sum limit

$$\begin{align}
\sum_{i\ge1}\frac1i f(ai) =\sum_{i\ge1}\frac a{L_i}f(L_i) \longrightarrow\int_0^\infty\frac{dL}{L}f(L). \tag{6.1}
\end{align}$$

Thus write the live continuum transfer as

$$\begin{align}
(T_t f)(L)=\int_0^\infty\frac{dL'}{L'}K_t^\Lambda(L,L')f(L'). \tag{6.2}
\end{align}$$

With $A_t=\varpi/\sinh(\varpi t)$, $B_t=\varpi\coth(\varpi t)$ and $B_t^2-A_t^2=\Lambda$, the symmetric kernel is

$$\begin{align}
K_t^\Lambda(L,L')=A_t\sqrt{LL'}\, e^{-B_t(L+L')}I_1(2A_t\sqrt{LL'}). \tag{6.3}
\end{align}$$

Here $I_1(2u)=\sum_{k\ge0}u^{2k+1}/[k!(k+1)!]$. Expanding this series and integrating each nonnegative term proves the normalization, rather than selecting a prefactor by analogy:

$$
\begin{aligned}
 \int_0^\infty\frac{dL'}{L'}K_t^\Lambda(L,L')e^{-sL'}
 &=e^{-B_tL}\sum_{r=1}^\infty
 \frac1{r!}\left(\frac{A_t^2L}{B_t+s}\right)^r\\
 &=e^{-Lv_t(s)}-e^{-B_tL}.
 \end{aligned}
 \tag{6.4}
$$

Tonelli's theorem applies for positive parameters; the final expressions also show finiteness. This is precisely the live part of (5.6). In particular

$$\begin{align}
T_t1(L)=e^{-\varpi L\tanh(\varpi t)} -e^{-\varpi L\coth(\varpi t)}<1. \tag{6.5}
\end{align}$$

As $\Lambda\to0$, (6.3) becomes

$$\begin{align}
K_t^0(L,L')=\frac{\sqrt{LL'}}t e^{-(L+L')/t}I_1(2\sqrt{LL'}/t),\qquad T_t1(L)=1-e^{-L/t}. \tag{6.6}
\end{align}$$

The density relative to ordinary $dL'$ is $K_t/L'$, not $K_t$. Confusing these measures changes both mass and composition. In units with $\Lambda=1$, (6.3) gives $K_1(1,2)=0.05416466018075719$ and the live row mass at $L=t=1$ is $0.19791917057848276$.

The Riccati composition of $v_t$ and the absorbing completion give the killed semigroup. Equivalently the spectral construction below gives

$$\begin{align}
\int_0^\infty\frac{dL'}{L'} K_t(L,L')K_u(L',L'')=K_{t+u}(L,L''). \tag{6.7}
\end{align}$$

For example at $\Lambda=1$, $L=1$, $L''=2$, $t=1/2$, $u=3/4$, the integral is $0.03275886410713114$. The intermediate-loop measure is exactly the one in (6.1).

## 7. The Self-Adjoint Length Hamiltonian and Normalizable Modes

The continuum Hilbert space is

$$\begin{align}
\mathcal H=L^2((0,\infty),dL/L),\qquad H_\Lambda=-L\frac{d^2}{dL^2}+\Lambda L. \tag{7.1}
\end{align}$$

A differential expression alone is not a self-adjoint operator. Start on compactly supported smooth functions and close the nonnegative quadratic form

$$\begin{align}
\mathfrak q[f]=\int_0^\infty dL\, \bigl(|f'|^2+\Lambda|f|^2\bigr) \tag{7.2}
\end{align}$$

in fixed reference units, using the squared form norm $\|f\|_{\mathcal H}^2+\mathfrak q[f]$. Weak differentiation and multiplication by the positive potential give a closable form. The closed-form representation theorem defines its positive self-adjoint Friedrichs operator: $f$ is in its operator domain exactly when $f$ is in the form domain and there is $h\in\mathcal H$ such that $\mathfrak q[v,f]=\langle v,h\rangle$ for every form-domain $v$; then $Hf=h$. Here $\mathfrak q[v,f]=\int_0^\infty(\bar v'f'+\Lambda\bar vf)dL$ is the sesquilinear form.

Integration by parts on the core gives (7.1). Near zero, finite derivative energy gives a trace and finite $\int|f|^2dL/L$ forces that trace to vanish. This implements the killed boundary. The constant function is not a Hilbert-space state. This empty-loop boundary is unrelated to the scalar-field constant mode removed in §3. At infinity the form domain supplies the integrability/decay condition, rather than an unmentioned reflecting wall [5].

For $\varpi=\sqrt\Lambda>0$, put $f(L)=L e^{-\varpi L}y(2\varpi L)$. The eigenvalue equation reduces to

$$\begin{align}
x y''+(2-x)y'+\left(\frac E{2\varpi}-1\right)y=0. \tag{7.3}
\end{align}$$

The associated Laguerre polynomials solve this with $E_n=2\varpi(n+1)$. Their standard orthogonality and completeness in weight $xe^{-x}$ give the normalized modes

$$\begin{align}
\Phi_n(L)=\frac{2\varpi L}{\sqrt{n+1}} e^{-\varpi L}L_n^{(1)}(2\varpi L),\qquad \int_0^\infty\frac{dL}{L}\Phi_n\Phi_m=\delta_{nm}. \tag{7.4}
\end{align}$$

Indeed $x=2\varpi L$ turns the norm into $(n+1)^{-1}\int_0^\infty xe^{-x}[L_n^{(1)}(x)]^2dx=1$. These functions lie in the Friedrichs domain, vanish linearly at zero and decay exponentially. Completeness is an orthogonal-polynomial theorem, not a consequence of checking finitely many modes. For the ground mode, $\Phi_0=2\varpi L e^{-\varpi L}$, $E_0=2\varpi$ and $\langle L\rangle_0=1/\varpi$.

The spectral kernel is

$$\begin{align}
K_t(L,L')=\sum_{n=0}^\infty e^{-2\varpi(n+1)t}\Phi_n(L)\Phi_n(L'). \tag{7.5}
\end{align}$$

To see its relation to (6.3), the Laguerre Poisson-kernel identity [4], for $0<q<1$, $x,y>0$, is

$$\begin{align}
\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{q^n}{n+1}L_n^{(1)}(x)L_n^{(1)}(y) =\frac{e^{-q(x+y)/(1-q)}}{(1-q)\sqrt{xyq}} I_1\left(\frac{2\sqrt{xyq}}{1-q}\right). \tag{7.6}
\end{align}$$

Multiply by $4\varpi^2LL'e^{-\varpi(L+L')}q$ and set $x=2\varpi L$, $y=2\varpi L'$, $q=e^{-2\varpi t}$. The prefactor becomes $\varpi\sqrt{LL'}/\sinh(\varpi t)$ and the exponent becomes $-\varpi(L+L')\coth(\varpi t)$, yielding (6.3). Orthonormality now proves (6.7) and the identity initial operator. The kernel also solves $\partial_tK=-H_{\Lambda,L}K$. This constructs the Euclidean length evolution; identifying it with CFT stress modes would require an additional map between state spaces. The normalizable discrete modes here require $\Lambda>0$; the zero-coupling limit is not obtained by declaring their ground mode unchanged.

## 8. What an Actual Matter Coupling Would Add

The scalar CFT and the pure length transfer above have not yet been coupled. One possible finite-graph matter assignment is

$$\begin{align}
S_T(\varphi)=\frac1{8\pi}\sum_{(uv)\in E(T)}
 w_{uv}(\varphi_u-\varphi_v)^2,\qquad w_{uv}>0, \tag{8.1}
\end{align}$$

with a specified graph, measure and zero-mode/boundary prescription. As a concrete normalization, take a triangle with all weights one and fix one vertex to zero. Writing the other two values as $x,y$, use ordinary Lebesgue measure $dx\,dy$:

$$
S_\triangle=\frac{x^2+y^2+(x-y)^2}{8\pi}
 =\frac12(x,y)M\binom{x}{y},\qquad
 M=\frac1{4\pi}\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}.
 \tag{8.2}
$$

The symmetric Hessian has eigenvalues $1/(4\pi)$ and $3/(4\pi)$, and therefore

$$\begin{align}
\int_{\mathbb R^2}dx\,dy\,e^{-S_\triangle} =\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{\det M}} =\frac{8\pi^2}{\sqrt3}. \tag{8.3}
\end{align}$$

A normalized Gaussian measure would change this prefactor. With the ordinary Lebesgue measure fixed here, pinning agrees with quotienting per unit uniform-shift volume for shift-invariant observables. In the unpinned three-vertex integral the normalized constant coordinate is $q_0=(\phi_0+\phi_1+\phi_2)/\sqrt{3}$. Along a uniform shift $\phi_i\mapsto\phi_i+c_{\rm sh}$, the orbit measures satisfy $dq_0=\sqrt{3}\,dc_{\rm sh}$; quotienting per unit normalized-coordinate volume instead gives $8\pi^2/3$.

On a strip, boundary scalar values would be additional state variables, and gluing would integrate those values with a chosen measure as well as the loop geometry. Integrating them out changes the geometric weights and need not leave a Markov transfer depending only on length. A single pinned graph neither establishes a CFT continuum limit nor transfers the central charge calculation to the triangulation count.

A four-dimensional EC/CDT/CFT identification would require a specified joint action, geometric ensemble, field and gauge measures, boundary state space, consistent constraints and a controlled continuum limit with identified observables. A two-dimensional CFT sewing rule does not supply those maps. The explicit results here are a Gaussian local stress algebra and a solvable two-dimensional geometry transfer, with their separate anomaly, marking and boundary prescriptions.
