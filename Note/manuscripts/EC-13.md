# EC-13 — Self-Bound Compact Stars and High-Density Phase Matching

**DRAFT — bounded review recorded.**

An equation of state specifies local matter; a stellar boundary-value problem turns it into a mass and radius. Neither operation supplies a time evolution or a stability spectrum. We will derive a cold three-flavour bag equation of state, calculate a converged self-bound star and a static mass–radius sequence, and construct a separate causal two-phase example. The distinction between chemical equilibrium, hydrostatic balance and radial stability is essential to interpreting the results.

Physical spacetime has signature $(-,+,+,+)$. Use $c=\hbar=1$ except in the explicit unit-conversion subsection, keep $G$ explicit, and use the Levi-Civita connection. The source is an isotropic perfect fluid with mixed stress $T^\mu{}_\nu=\operatorname{diag}(-\varepsilon,p,p,p)$ in its rest frame. This is a declared spinless-fluid GR baseline, not a derivation that microscopic spin correlations vanish. In an Einstein–Cartan effective model, a spin average, its composite operators and its thermodynamically consistent pressure would have to be supplied separately. In particular, $\langle J_5\rangle=0$ does not imply $\langle J_5^2\rangle=0$.

## 1. A Definite Cold Matter Model

Assume homogeneous matter at zero temperature, with three massless quark flavours $u,d,s$, three colours and two spin states per flavour. Neglect strong-interaction corrections, quark masses, pairing, magnetic stresses and surface tension. The constant $B>0$ is an imposed difference of vacuum energy densities between the quark phase and the exterior vacuum. It is not calculated from QCD here, and it is not an additional cosmological constant outside the star.

Let $\mu_q>0$ be the common quark chemical potential. With this composition, equal flavour densities give zero electric charge, since $2/3-1/3-1/3=0$. Neutrino-free weak equilibrium is consistent with $\mu_d=\mu_s=\mu_u$ and $\mu_e=0$; no electron Fermi sea is needed in this massless symmetric limit. This is not the composition of a generic neutron-star core.

For one flavour the degeneracy is $g_q=6$. The zero-temperature occupied momentum ball gives

$$\begin{align}
n_f=\frac{6}{2\pi^2}\int_0^{\mu_q}q^2\,dq=\frac{\mu_q^3}{\pi^2},\qquad \varepsilon_f=\frac{6}{2\pi^2}\int_0^{\mu_q}q^3\,dq=\frac{3\mu_q^4}{4\pi^2}.
\end{align}$$

For massless particles the isotropic momentum flux contributes a factor $1/3$ to the pressure:

$$\begin{align}
p_f=\frac{6}{6\pi^2}\int_0^{\mu_q}q^3\,dq=\frac{\mu_q^4}{4\pi^2}.
\end{align}$$

Each quark carries baryon number $1/3$. Summing flavours and adding the bag contribution therefore gives

$$\begin{align}
n_B=\frac{n_u+n_d+n_s}{3}=\frac{\mu_q^3}{\pi^2},\qquad \mu_B=3\mu_q,\qquad p=\frac{3\mu_q^4}{4\pi^2}-B,\qquad \varepsilon=\frac{9\mu_q^4}{4\pi^2}+B.
\end{align}$$

The bag term adds energy and subtracts pressure. Its two signs are fixed by the vacuum stress, not by the quark degeneracy. They also make the cold thermodynamic identities consistent:

$$\begin{align}
\varepsilon+p=\mu_Bn_B,\qquad d\varepsilon=\mu_B\,dn_B,\qquad dp=n_B\,d\mu_B.
\end{align}$$

For example, differentiating the energy gives $9\mu_q^3d\mu_q/\pi^2$, while $3\mu_q\,d(\mu_q^3/\pi^2)$ gives the same result. Omitting the $-B$ in pressure would leave an erroneous $B$ in $\varepsilon+p-\mu_Bn_B$.

Eliminating $\mu_q$ gives two useful descriptions:

$$\begin{align}
\varepsilon(n_B)=\frac94\pi^{2/3}n_B^{4/3}+B,\qquad p(n_B)=\frac34\pi^{2/3}n_B^{4/3}-B,\qquad \boxed{p=\frac{\varepsilon-4B}{3}.}
\end{align}$$

For the isolated fluid phase use $p\ge0$, not an arbitrary continuation into negative-pressure matter. Its compressibility is positive and its adiabatic sound speed is

$$\begin{align}
c_s^2=\frac{dp}{d\varepsilon}=\frac13.
\end{align}$$

Also $\varepsilon-p=2p+4B>0$, $\varepsilon+p=4p+4B>0$, and $\varepsilon+3p=6p+4B>0$. Thus this isotropic branch satisfies the dominant, null and strong energy conditions and is causal. These local facts do not prove stellar stability.

### The Finite-Density Surface

Define the surface energy density $\varepsilon_s=4B$. At zero pressure,

$$\begin{align}
\mu_{B,s}=(108\pi^2B)^{1/4},\qquad n_s=\frac{4B}{\mu_{B,s}},\qquad \frac{\varepsilon_s}{n_s}=\mu_{B,s}.
\end{align}$$

The fluid can meet vacuum at nonzero density: this is what self-bound means in this model. No gravitational field is needed to make its zero-pressure density nonzero. Whether such a phase is globally preferred to hadronic matter is another question. Comparing to an externally supplied zero-pressure reference chemical potential $\mu_{\mathrm{ref}}$ gives the model inequality $B<\mu_{\mathrm{ref}}^4/(108\pi^2)$ in natural units. This is a surface-energy comparison, not a calculation of hadronic matter, nucleation barriers or a phenomenologically viable QCD parameter range.

The constancy of $B$ matters. If a different model used $\varepsilon=a n_B^{4/3}+B(n_B)$, the first law would give

$$\begin{align}
p=n_B\frac{d\varepsilon}{dn_B}-\varepsilon =\frac a3n_B^{4/3}-B(n_B)+n_BB'(n_B).
\end{align}$$

Simply replacing $B$ in the constant-bag pressure would miss the last term. Density-dependent interaction or spin corrections must be differentiated consistently; they cannot generally inherit the scaling law derived next.

## 2. From the EOS to a Dimensionless Star

Use areal radius $r$ and the static metric

$$\begin{align}
ds^2=-e^{2\Phi(r)}dt^2+\frac{dr^2}{1-2GM(r)/r}+r^2d\Omega^2.
\end{align}$$

The spherical Einstein equations and conserved-fluid equation, derived in [EC-06](<EC-06.md#1-a-conserved-spherical-perfect-fluid>), are

$$\begin{align}
\frac{dM}{dr}=4\pi r^2\varepsilon,\qquad \frac{d\Phi}{dr}=\frac{G(M+4\pi r^3p)}{r(r-2GM)},\qquad \frac{dp}{dr}=-(\varepsilon+p)\frac{d\Phi}{dr}.
\end{align}$$

We assume a regular center, no rotation, positive static factor $1-2GM/r$, and the pure quark EOS throughout the fluid. There is no hadronic envelope in this calculation.

The scale supplied by $\varepsilon_s$ is

$$\begin{align}
L=(4\pi G\varepsilon_s)^{-1/2},\qquad x=\frac rL,\qquad v=\frac{GM}{L},\qquad y=\frac p{\varepsilon_s},\qquad e=\frac\varepsilon{\varepsilon_s}=1+3y.
\end{align}$$

For example, substituting $M=Lv/G$ and $r=Lx$ into the mass equation gives $dv/dx=4\pi G\varepsilon_sL^2x^2e=x^2e$. All dimensional constants disappear from the structure equations:

$$\begin{align}
\frac{dv}{dx}=x^2(1+3y),\qquad \frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{(1+4y)(v+x^3y)}{x(x-2v)},\qquad \frac{d\Phi}{dx}=\frac{v+x^3y}{x(x-2v)}.
\end{align}$$

A central pressure ratio $y_c>0$ determines a dimensionless equilibrium, stopped at its first zero $y(x_R)=0$. The dimensional outputs are

$$\begin{align}
R=Lx_R,\qquad M_\star=\frac LGv_R,\qquad \frac{2GM_\star}{R}=\frac{2v_R}{x_R}.
\end{align}$$

Consequently $M_\star,R\propto B^{-1/2}$ at fixed $y_c$. Compactness and the dimensionless profile do not depend on $B$. This is dimensional similarity of a selected constant-bag EOS, not a universal compact-star mass bound.

### Center Data without Dividing by Zero

At the center $v(0)=0$, $y(0)=y_c$. Put $e_c=1+3y_c$. Expanding the equations determines

$$\begin{aligned}
 y(x)&=y_c+a_2x^2+a_4x^4+O(x^6),\\
 a_2&=-\frac12(1+4y_c)\left(\frac13+2y_c\right),\qquad a_4=\frac{(1+4y_c)(1+6y_c)(2+13y_c)}{30},\\
 v(x)&=\frac{e_c}{3}x^3+\frac{3a_2}{5}x^5+O(x^7).
\end{aligned}$$

Indeed, the leading pressure force is $-(e_c+y_c)(e_c/3+y_c)x$, which fixes $2a_2$. The mass equation then fixes its fifth-order coefficient. The numerical start at $x=\delta>0$ keeps $y_c+a_2\delta^2$ and both displayed mass terms. Its leading omitted pressure datum is $a_4\delta^4$, which can be tested by decreasing $\delta$.

For numerical robustness one can integrate in $s=\log x$, using $d/ds=x\,d/dx$. For example,

$$\begin{align}
\frac{dv}{ds}=e^{3s}(1+3y),\qquad \frac{dy}{ds}=-\frac{(1+4y)(v+e^{3s}y)}{e^s-2v}.
\end{align}$$

This removes the explicit $1/x$ coefficient from the finite-start equations. It is a change of radial variable, not time evolution. The endpoint must still satisfy the pressure condition; premature termination with positive pressure is not a star surface.

## 3. Baryon Number and the Exterior Clock

From the EOS,

$$\begin{align}
\frac{n_B}{n_s}=(1+4y)^{3/4},\qquad \frac{\mu_B}{\mu_{B,s}}=(1+4y)^{1/4}.
\end{align}$$

The conserved baryon number uses proper spatial volume:

$$\begin{align}
N_B=4\pi\int_0^R\frac{n_B(r)r^2}{\sqrt{1-2GM(r)/r}}\,dr =4\pi n_sL^3z_R,\qquad \frac{dz}{dx}=\frac{x^2(1+4y)^{3/4}}{\sqrt{1-2v/x}}.
\end{align}$$

Its center expansion begins $z=(1+4y_c)^{3/4}x^3/3+O(x^5)$; the computation retains its fifth-order term as obtained from this differential equation. The flat-volume integral without the square-root denominator is a different quantity.

Mechanical and chemical equilibrium also agree locally. Combining $dp=n_Bd\mu_B$ with the pressure equation and $\varepsilon+p=\mu_Bn_B$ gives

$$\begin{align}
\frac{d\log\mu_B}{dr}=-\Phi',\qquad \mu_Be^\Phi=\mu_\infty.
\end{align}$$

Fix the additive lapse constant by matching to Schwarzschild time at the surface:

$$\begin{align}
e^{2\Phi(R)}=1-\frac{2GM_\star}{R}=f_R,\qquad \Phi(x)=\frac12\log f_R-\frac14\log(1+4y(x)),\qquad \mu_\infty=\mu_{B,s}\sqrt{f_R}.
\end{align}$$

This clock is normalized at spatial infinity. Adding a lapse constant without a compensating change of time changes a quoted coordinate frequency. It is not harmless when comparing pulsation frequencies or redshifted chemical potentials.

Since $\mu_{B,s}n_s=\varepsilon_s$ and $4\pi G\varepsilon_sL^2=1$,

$$\begin{align}
\frac{M_\star}{\mu_{B,s}N_B}=\frac{v_R}{z_R},\qquad \frac{\mu_{B,s}N_B-M_\star}{\mu_{B,s}N_B}=1-\frac{v_R}{z_R}.
\end{align}$$

The second expression is a gravitational binding fraction relative to zero-pressure matter of this same EOS. It is not automatically the mass defect relative to free neutrons. Because $n_s\propto B^{3/4}$, the number at fixed dimensionless profile scales as $N_B\propto B^{-3/4}$, consistently with $\mu_{B,s}N_B\propto B^{-1/2}$.

## 5. Matching a Finite-Density Surface to Vacuum

A density discontinuity is not automatically a surface layer. For a timelike static boundary with no surface stress, the Darmois matching conditions require the induced metric and extrinsic curvature to agree. Use the same normal pointing toward increasing $r$ on both sides. In proper surface time,

$$\begin{align}
K_{\tau\tau}=-\sqrt f\,\Phi',\qquad K_{\theta\theta}=r\sqrt f,\qquad K_{\varphi\varphi}=\sin^2\theta\,K_{\theta\theta}.
\end{align}$$

Continuity of $M(R)=M_\star$ and the lapse normalization make the induced metrics and angular curvatures agree. The interior pressure equation gives

$$\begin{align}
\Phi'_{\mathrm{in}}(R)=\frac{G(M_\star+4\pi R^3p(R))}{R(R-2GM_\star)} =\frac{GM_\star}{R(R-2GM_\star)}=\Phi'_{\mathrm{out}}(R)
\end{align}$$

when $p(R)=0$. Thus the proper-time curvature also agrees even though $M'$ jumps from $4\pi R^2\varepsilon_s$ to zero. The interior bag trace supplies a further check:

$$\begin{align}
\mathcal R=8\pi G(\varepsilon-3p)=32\pi GB \quad\text{inside},\qquad \mathcal R=0\quad\text{outside}.
\end{align}$$

The Ricci scalar has a finite one-sided jump, not a required delta-function curvature layer. Adding surface tension or a material shell changes the boundary problem and requires its surface stress and junction equations; no such layer is part of the sequence above.

## 6. A Separate, Explicitly Matched Two-Phase Model

To demonstrate phase matching rather than infer it from a mass curve, introduce a neutral one-component toy hadronic phase H and a quark phase Q. Let $A,m_0>0$, $u=\mu_B/m_0\ge1$, and define

$$\begin{align}
p_H=A(u-1)^2,\qquad \varepsilon_H=A(u^2-1),\qquad p_Q=A\left(\frac{u^4}{8}-1\right),\qquad \varepsilon_Q=A\left(\frac{3u^4}{8}+1\right).
\end{align}$$

For Q to have exactly the massless bag coefficient above, choose $B=A=8m_0^4/(108\pi^2)$. The parameter $m_0$ here is a toy chemical-potential scale, not an identified neutron mass or a prediction of a nuclear EOS.

Differentiate the pressures, rather than guessing the densities:

$$\begin{align}
n_H=\frac{2A}{m_0}(u-1),\qquad n_Q=\frac{A}{2m_0}u^3,\qquad \varepsilon_i+p_i=\mu_Bn_i.
\end{align}$$

Each phase obeys the cold first law. Their squared sound speeds are $(u-1)/u$ and $1/3$, respectively, so the relevant branches are causal and have positive compressibility. At fixed chemical potential the preferred homogeneous phase has the larger pressure, equivalently smaller grand-potential density $-p$. The pressure difference factors as

$$\begin{align}
\frac{p_Q-p_H}{A}=\frac{(u-2)(u^3+2u^2-4u+8)}8.
\end{align}$$

The cubic is positive on $u\ge1$: its derivative is positive there and its value at one is seven. The unique transition in this range is therefore

$$\begin{align}
\mu_*=2m_0,\qquad p_*=A,\qquad (\varepsilon_H,\varepsilon_Q)=(3A,7A),\qquad (n_H,n_Q)=\left(\frac{2A}{m_0},\frac{4A}{m_0}\right).
\end{align}$$

Both pressure and baryon chemical potential agree; density does not. The energy-density jump is $\Delta\varepsilon=4A=\mu_*\Delta n_B$. At zero temperature it should not be confused with a nonzero thermal latent heat $T\Delta s$. Nor is it automatically energy radiated when an entire self-gravitating star converts at fixed baryon number.

If this toy pair were used in a hybrid star, the dimensionless interface pressure would be $y_*=p_*/(4A)=1/4$. The inner and outer energy densities there would be $7/4$ and $3/4$ in units of $4A$. The one-sided mass derivatives differ, while mass, pressure, lapse and no-layer extrinsic curvature match. A finite radial interval of constant pressure cannot represent the Maxwell plateau in an ordinary static star: for $M>0$, $\varepsilon+p>0$ and $f>0$, the TOV pressure derivative is strictly negative. Instead, the ideal sharp interface changes density at one radius.

This is a **different EOS from the pure-Q sequence**. A hybrid configuration must be reintegrated with its H envelope and interface; it cannot be obtained by relabelling one point of the earlier mass–radius curve. Multiple conserved charges, charge screening, surface energy, finite-temperature coexistence and finite-rate conversion would require additional variables and interface conditions. No hybrid-star solution or burning-front speed was calculated here.

## 7. What the Mass Extremum Says About Stability

A local causal EOS and a solution of TOV establish an equilibrium, not that radial disturbances remain bounded. Radial stability concerns perturbations that conserve baryon number and satisfy the chosen adiabatic matter response and moving-surface boundary condition. With mode time dependence $e^{i\omega t}$, a negative $\omega^2$ signals exponential growth; those eigenvalues have not been computed here.

For the same fixed cold EOS, a standard constrained-variation first law for neighbouring regular nonrotating equilibria is $dM_\star=\mu_\infty dN_B$. It assumes the same baryon normalization, no heat, rotation or surface-work term, and no change of model along the sequence. In the variables above it reads

$$\begin{align}
\frac{dv_R}{dy_c}=\sqrt{f_R}\,\frac{dz_R}{dy_c}.
\end{align}$$

Centered numerical derivatives at $y_c=1$ leave residuals about $1.19\times10^{-7}$, $1.18\times10^{-9}$ and $1.18\times10^{-11}$ for steps $10^{-2}$, $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-4}$. This supports the proper-volume number and clock normalization. It is not a proof of the global variational first law. Under that law the mass and baryon-number extrema coincide on a smooth fixed-EOS family; using an extremum to count unstable modes additionally requires the applicable turning-point hypotheses and a known initial mode count. We assign no numerically proven stable/unstable labels to the table.

The self-bound surface deserves particular care in any later radial calculation. Define the adiabatic index by $\Gamma_{\mathrm{ad}}=(n_B/p)(dp/dn_B)$. Here

$$\begin{align}
\Gamma_{\mathrm{ad}}p=\frac{\varepsilon+p}{3} =\frac43(p+B)\longrightarrow\frac{4B}{3}\ne0 \quad\text{as }p\to0.
\end{align}$$

Thus a divergent $\Gamma_{\mathrm{ad}}$ is not permission to discard the surface term. For a radial displacement $\eta(r)$, the standard linearized spherical number/constraint relations give, with $\zeta=r^2e^{-\Phi}\eta$,

$$\begin{align}
\Delta p=-\Gamma_{\mathrm{ad}}p\,\frac{e^\Phi}{r^2}\frac{d\zeta}{dr}.
\end{align}$$

Regularity requires $\eta=O(r)$ at the center. A free vacuum surface requires $\Delta p(R)=0$, which for this EOS imposes $\zeta'(R)=0$, rather than following automatically from $p(R)=0$. The surface moves; setting its displacement to zero would impose a different problem. The lapse must retain the exterior normalization if a calculated $\omega$ is to be interpreted using the clock at infinity. This chapter has not solved that radial operator, nor the additional interface response problem for the toy two-phase EOS.

## 8. Limits of a High-Density Stellar Claim

The bag constant, particle content and phase competition are phenomenological inputs. A microscopic QCD treatment would have to address masses, interactions, pairing, neutrality and weak-equilibration constraints. An Einstein–Cartan spin-averaged correction would likewise need its own state, correlation prescription, energy, pressure and conserved currents; a dimension or mean axial current cannot fix them.

The calculated sequence is a family of pure-phase static solutions. Following it in time would require specified accretion or cooling, baryon and angular-momentum budgets, weak reaction rates, transport and conversion kinetics. Comparing two possible final stars requires fixed conserved quantities, not equal central pressure. Collapse requires a dynamical initial-value problem; an event horizon requires a global causal solution. No collapse, bounce, regular black-hole core or child universe follows from this TOV curve or its mass maximum.
