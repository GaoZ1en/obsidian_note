# problem 1

*suppose $\displaystyle{H_{1}}$ and $\displaystyle{H_{2}}$ are subgroups of $\displaystyle{G}$. prove that $\displaystyle{H_{1}\cap H_{2}}$ is also a subgroup of $\displaystyle{G}$.*

we only need to verify the subgroup criteria:

1. the identity element $\displaystyle{e}$ of $\displaystyle{G}$ is in $\displaystyle{H_{1}\cap H_{2}}$ since $\displaystyle{e\in H_{1}}$ and $\displaystyle{e\in H_{2}}$.
2. if $\displaystyle{a,b\in H_{1}\cap H_{2}}$, then $\displaystyle{a,b\in H_{1}}$ and $\displaystyle{a,b\in H_{2}}$. since $\displaystyle{H_{1}}$ and $\displaystyle{H_{2}}$ are subgroups, we have $\displaystyle{ab\in H_{1}}$ and $\displaystyle{ab\in H_{2}}$. thus, $\displaystyle{ab\in H_{1}\cap H_{2}}$.
3. if $\displaystyle{a\in H_{1}\cap H_{2}}$, then $\displaystyle{a\in H_{1}}$ and $\displaystyle{a\in H_{2}}$. since $\displaystyle{H_{1}}$ and $\displaystyle{H_{2}}$ are subgroups, we have $\displaystyle{a^{-1}\in H_{1}}$ and $\displaystyle{a^{-1}\in H_{2}}$. thus, $\displaystyle{a^{-1}\in H_{1}\cap H_{2}}$.

therefore we conclude that $\displaystyle{H_{1}\cap H_{2}}$ is a subgroup of $\displaystyle{G}$.

# problem 2

*prove that except the identity element, if every element of a group is of order 2, then the group is Abelian*

let $\displaystyle{G}$ be a group such that every element of $\displaystyle{G}$ except the identity element is of order 2. $\displaystyle{\forall a,b\in G}$, we have $\displaystyle{a^{-1}=a,b^{-1}=b}$, thus

$$\begin{align}
(ab)^{-1} & =b^{-1}a^{-1} \\
 & =ba \\
(ab\text{ is of order }2) & =ab
\end{align}$$

thus we conclude that $\displaystyle{G}$ is Abelian.

# problem 4

*up to isomorphism, classify all groups of order $\displaystyle{9}$. show that there are only two groups, $\displaystyle{C_{9}}$ and $\displaystyle{C_{3}\times C_{3}}$*

notice that $\displaystyle{|G|=9=3^{2}}$, the order of group elements can only be $\displaystyle{1,3,9}$. if there exists an element of order $\displaystyle{9}$, then the group is cyclic and isomorphic to $\displaystyle{C_{9}}$. otherwise, all non-identity elements are of order $\displaystyle{3}$. let $\displaystyle{a\in G}$ be a non-identity element, then $\displaystyle{\braket{a}\cong C_{3}}$ is a subgroup of $\displaystyle{G}$. consider the left cosets of $\displaystyle{\braket{a}}$ in $\displaystyle{G}$, we have

$$\begin{align}
G & =\braket{a}\cup g_{1}\braket{a}\cup g_{2}\braket{a}
\end{align}$$

where $\displaystyle{g_{1},g_{2}\in G\setminus \braket{a}}$. since $\displaystyle{g_{1},g_{2}}$ are of order $\displaystyle{3}$, we have $\displaystyle{\braket{g_{1}}\cong C_{3}}$ and $\displaystyle{\braket{g_{2}}\cong C_{3}}$. notice that $\displaystyle{\braket{a}\cap \braket{g_{1}}=\braket{a}\cap \braket{g_{2}}=\braket{g_{1}}\cap \braket{g_{2}}=\left\{e\right\}}$, thus we conclude that $\displaystyle{G\cong C_{3}\times C_{3}}$.

notice that in a group of order $3^{2}$, any subgroup of order $3$ has index $3$ and is therefore normal . hence $\langle a\rangle$ and $\langle g_{1}\rangle$ are normal, distinct, and intersect trivially. their product has size

$$\begin{align}
|\langle a\rangle\langle g_{1}\rangle|=\frac{|\langle a\rangle|\,|\langle g_{1}\rangle|}{|\langle a\rangle\cap \langle g_{1}\rangle|}=\frac{3\cdot 3}{1}=9=|G|
\end{align}$$

so $G=\langle a\rangle\langle g_{1}\rangle$ and (normality + trivial intersection) gives the internal direct product $G\cong \langle a\rangle \times \langle g_{1}\rangle \cong C_{3}\times C_{3}$.

therefore, up to isomorphism, there are only two groups of order $\displaystyle{9}$: $\displaystyle{C_{9}}$ and $\displaystyle{C_{3}\times C_{3}}$.
