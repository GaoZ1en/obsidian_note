# Purpose

The Part III papers are useful not only as a source of past questions. They also show what string theory looks like when a course is examined as a coherent subject rather than as a list of isolated topics.

This note extracts the most important extensions suggested by those papers and states explicitly:

- what current course material each extension is closest to,
- what genuinely new ingredient it adds,
- how Cambridge-style exam questions usually phrase it,
- what to memorize and what to understand,
- how urgent it is for the present course.

# 1. Classical string mechanics

## Closest link to the current course

This is closest to:

- Polyakov action,
- conformal gauge,
- Virasoro constraints,
- mode expansions,
- open-string boundary conditions.

## What Cambridge adds

Cambridge papers often insist that the student can still do the classical theory cleanly before quantization. The typical additions are:

- varying the action carefully,
- deriving Noether charges,
- comparing Polyakov and Nambu-Goto directly,
- writing rigid rotating or stretched-string solutions,
- interpreting NN, DD, ND, and DN boundary conditions geometrically.

## Exam-style questions

The most typical questions are:

1. Derive the equations of motion and boundary conditions from the Polyakov action.
1. Derive the stress tensor and Virasoro constraints.
1. Prove target-space translation or Lorentz invariance and compute the corresponding charges.
1. Write an explicit rotating open-string solution and extract $E$ and $J$.
1. Show how a string stretched between D-branes acquires a classical mass contribution.

## What to prepare

Memorize:

- the bulk variation,
- the boundary variation,
- the stress tensor,
- the standard NN and DD mode expansions,
- the rotating-string ansatz.

Understand:

- why constraints are part of the equations of motion in gauge systems,
- why the classical piece of a stretched string already knows about brane separation and gauge enhancement.

## Priority for the present course

Very high.

This is already inside the technical range of the current course, and it is one of the easiest places for an examiner to ask a substantial but self-contained calculation.

# 2. Vertex operators and tree-level amplitudes

## Closest link to the current course

This is closest to:

- free-boson OPEs,
- conformal weights,
- physical-state conditions,
- state-operator correspondence,
- tachyon/vector/tensor vertex operators.

## What Cambridge adds

The course already teaches the ingredients, but the Part III papers push one step further:

- insert physical states into the path integral,
- divide by the conformal Killing group,
- derive Koba-Nielsen factors,
- interpret poles as physical intermediate states,
- analyze saddle points in hard-scattering limits.

## Exam-style questions

Typical forms are:

1. Derive the tree-level $n$-tachyon amplitude from the Polyakov path integral.
1. Distinguish integrated and unintegrated vertex operators.
1. Explain why the amplitude is independent of insertion points after gauge fixing.
1. Derive the four-point tachyon amplitude and discuss crossing symmetry.
1. Use conformal weight $(1,1)$ or $1$ to recover the on-shell condition.

## What to prepare

Memorize:

- the free-field correlator,
- the Koba-Nielsen factor,
- the sphere factor $g_{s}^{n-2}$,
- the $SL(2,\mathbb C)$ fixing of three insertion points.

Understand:

- why integrated operators must be marginal,
- why factorization poles reflect the physical string spectrum,
- how ghost insertions compensate residual gauge redundancy.

## Priority for the present course

Very high.

This is the most natural next topic if one wants an extension that still feels like the same course rather than a new one.

# 3. Ghosts, BRST, and critical dimension

## Closest link to the current course

This is closest to:

- $bc$ ghosts,
- $\beta\gamma$ ghosts,
- Virasoro and super-Virasoro algebras,
- GSO projection,
- anomaly cancellation.

## What Cambridge adds

Part III papers do not leave the subject at the level of:

- matter central charge,
- ghost central charge,
- therefore $D=26$ or $D=10$.

They often want the student to explain:

- where the ghosts came from,
- how BRST symmetry packages the constraints,
- why nilpotency is the correct consistency condition,
- why cohomology is the right physical-state space.

## Exam-style questions

The common question shapes are:

1. Derive the conformal weights and central charge of a ghost system.
1. Explain why ghosts appear when gauge fixing the Polyakov path integral.
1. Write the BRST charge and explain the meaning of each term.
1. Show that nilpotency requires vanishing total anomaly.
1. Explain the meaning of BRST-closed and BRST-exact states.

## What to prepare

Memorize:

- $c_{bc}=1-3(2\lambda-1)^{2}$ for fermionic $bc$,
- $c_{\beta\gamma}=2-12\lambda(1-\lambda)$ for bosonic $\beta\gamma$,
- the bosonic BRST charge,
- the slogan `physical states = BRST cohomology`.

Understand:

- why BRST is stronger than a mnemonic and is really the quantum form of gauge invariance,
- why critical dimension is a statement about the total gauge-fixed theory rather than the matter sector alone.

## Priority for the present course

Very high.

Among all possible extensions, this is the cleanest conceptual completion of the material already in the cheat sheet.

# 4. Compactification and T-duality

## Closest link to the current course

This is closest to:

- closed-string left/right decomposition,
- open-string boundary conditions,
- momentum and winding,
- Type IIA/Type IIB spectra.

## What Cambridge adds

The standard circle compactification problem is pushed until it reveals:

- momentum-winding exchange,
- self-dual enhancement,
- geometric reinterpretation of boundary conditions,
- chirality flip between Type IIA and Type IIB.

## Exam-style questions

Typical questions:

1. Derive the compact-boson mode expansion and mass formula.
1. Show that the spectrum is invariant under $R\leftrightarrow \alpha'/R$.
1. Explain how T-duality exchanges Neumann and Dirichlet conditions for open strings.
1. Show how extra massless states appear at the self-dual radius.
1. Explain why T-duality exchanges Type IIA and Type IIB.

## What to prepare

Memorize:

- $p_{L,R}=n/R\pm wR/\alpha'$,
- the level-matching relation,
- the self-dual radius,
- the chirality rule for Type IIA versus Type IIB.

Understand:

- why momentum and winding are equally fundamental to closed strings,
- why D-branes are the natural T-dual image of open-string boundary conditions.

## Priority for the present course

High.

This is still very close to material already seen in past exams and in the existing cheat sheet.

# 5. D-branes as physical boundary conditions

## Closest link to the current course

This is closest to:

- DD, NN, ND, DN mode expansions,
- stretched open strings,
- RR fields in Type II spectra.

## What Cambridge adds

Cambridge questions often use D-branes to connect three viewpoints at once:

- worldsheet boundary conditions,
- open-string spectra,
- spacetime charge and geometry.

At the more advanced end they also add:

- BPS saturation,
- no-force conditions,
- gauge enhancement on coincident branes,
- M-theory interpretation of Type IIA branes.

## Exam-style questions

Typical questions:

1. Show that $p+1$ Neumann directions define a D$p$-brane.
1. Derive the mass formula of a string stretched between separated branes.
1. Explain why coincident branes enhance the gauge symmetry.
1. Explain how RR form fields couple to D-branes.
1. Explain the BPS no-force condition for parallel branes.

## What to prepare

Memorize:

- the stretched-string mass term,
- the relation between RR $(p+1)$-forms and D$p$-branes,
- the basic gauge-enhancement story.

Understand:

- why open strings ending on branes turn boundary data into dynamical spacetime objects,
- why D-branes are simultaneously boundary conditions, charged objects, and gauge-theory carriers.

## Priority for the present course

Moderate to high.

The basic part is highly relevant. The BPS and eleven-dimensional interpretation are less urgent unless the course has already moved in that direction.

# 6. Background fields and Weyl-invariance constraints

## Closest link to the current course

This is closest to:

- the worldsheet stress tensor,
- Weyl invariance,
- sigma-model actions,
- CFT anomaly cancellation.

## What Cambridge adds

The papers often push from flat-space quantization to the statement:

`worldsheet consistency constrains spacetime backgrounds`.

Technically, this means:

- add couplings $G_{\mu\nu}(X)$, $B_{\mu\nu}(X)$, $\Phi(X)$,
- compute beta functions,
- require their vanishing,
- interpret the result as spacetime field equations.

## Exam-style questions

Typical forms:

1. Write the sigma-model action in background fields.
1. State the leading beta functions for $G$, $B$, and $\Phi$.
1. Explain why the dilaton equation is not fully independent.
1. Expand a simple curved target-space sigma model around a background point and identify the divergent renormalization diagram.
1. Explain why a given curved space is or is not automatically a consistent string background.

## What to prepare

Memorize:

- the sigma-model action,
- the leading-order beta-function equations,
- the qualitative meaning of $\alpha'$ corrections.

Understand:

- why conformal invariance of the worldsheet theory is the real consistency condition,
- why string equations of motion are renormalization-group equations in disguise.

## Priority for the present course

High conceptually, moderate computationally.

It is one of the best themes for a conceptual long-answer question.

# 7. One-loop partition functions and modular invariance

## Closest link to the current course

This is closest to:

- torus quantization,
- compact bosons,
- GSO projection,
- ghost systems,
- Type II classification.

## What Cambridge adds

Instead of classifying spectra only in Hilbert-space language, this topic asks for a global one-loop consistency check. It explains:

- why not every sector combination is allowed,
- why level matching matters,
- why spin structures must be summed over,
- why heterotic internal lattices are special.

## Exam-style questions

Typical questions:

1. Write the torus partition function.
1. Explain why modular invariance is required.
1. Explain what the $T$ and $S$ transformations test.
1. Show why one spin structure alone is not enough.
1. Explain why heterotic lattices must be even and self-dual.

## What to prepare

Memorize:

- $Z(\tau,\bar\tau)=\operatorname{Tr}(q^{L_{0}-c/24}\bar q^{\tilde L_{0}-\tilde c/24})$,
- $S:\tau\mapsto -1/\tau$,
- $T:\tau\mapsto \tau+1$.

Understand:

- why modular invariance is the one-loop analogue of gauge invariance,
- why it organizes the Type II and heterotic constructions globally.

## Priority for the present course

Moderate to high.

This is very natural if the course already touched modular invariance, but it is slightly less immediate than tree-level amplitudes or BRST.

# 8. Curved exact models and WZW theory

## Closest link to the current course

This is closest to:

- current algebra,
- OPEs,
- the idea that the target space can be curved.

## What Cambridge adds

This is the first place where the worldsheet theory is no longer just free bosons and fermions in flat space, yet remains exactly solvable because symmetry becomes stronger rather than weaker.

## Exam-style questions

Typical questions:

1. Derive the conserved chiral currents of a WZW model.
1. Write the stress tensor in terms of currents.
1. Explain the factorized solution

$$\begin{align}
g(x^{+},x^{-})=g_{+}(x^{+})g_{-}(x^{-}).
\end{align}$$

1. Explain why a curved target can still yield an exact CFT.

## What to prepare

Memorize:

- the current-conservation equations,
- the role of affine symmetry,
- the conceptual meaning of spectral flow if AdS$_3$ is in play.

Understand:

- why this topic is farther from the present course than BRST or amplitudes,
- why it is still part of the same worldsheet-CFT language.

## Priority for the present course

Low for the current exam, unless the instructor explicitly signals interest in AdS$_3$ or exact curved backgrounds.

# A practical ranking for exam preparation

## Highest value

These are the best extensions to prepare if the aim is still the Cheng Peng exam rather than a full Part III course:

1. classical string mechanics,
1. vertex operators and tree-level amplitudes,
1. ghosts, BRST, and critical dimension,
1. compactification and T-duality.

## Second line

These are useful if time allows:

1. D-branes as physical boundary conditions,
1. background fields and Weyl-invariance constraints,
1. one-loop partition functions and modular invariance.

## Lowest priority for the current exam

These are valuable but less likely to be the first payoff:

1. WZW and exact curved-background models,
1. advanced BPS brane physics and M-theory interpretation,
1. Hagedorn thermodynamics beyond the conceptual slogan.

# If one wanted to turn these into new revision sheets

The most efficient next three mini-notes would be:

1. `BRST and ghost logic`
   Because it closes the conceptual gap between constraints and physical states.

1. `Vertex operators and the 4-tachyon amplitude`
   Because it is the most exam-like extension of the existing CFT material.

1. `Classical string solutions and Noether charges`
   Because Cambridge repeatedly treats this as standard string mechanics rather than optional background material.

# Final lesson

The strongest lesson from the Cambridge papers is that string theory is examined as a connected hierarchy:

$$\begin{align}
\text{classical worldsheet}
\to
\text{quantized CFT}
\to
\text{ghost and BRST consistency}
\to
\text{vertex operators and amplitudes}
\to
\text{spacetime interpretation}.
\end{align}$$

If revision is organized in that order, almost every Part III question becomes a variation on a familiar chain rather than a new topic.
