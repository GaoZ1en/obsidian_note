---
The derivation of the neutrino temperature relies on the **conservation of comoving entropy before and after electron-positron annihilation**:

1. **Neutrino decoupling**: At temperature $T \sim 1\text{ MeV}$, neutrinos decouple. Thereafter, their temperature scales with the cosmic expansion as $T_\nu \propto a^{-1}$.
1. **Electron-positron annihilation**: Subsequently, as the temperature drops to the order of the electron mass $T \sim m_e = 0.511\text{ MeV}$, electron-positron pairs annihilate ($e^+ + e^- \to \gamma + \gamma$). The entropy released entirely heats the photon bath, without heating the already decoupled neutrinos.
1. **Change in effective degrees of freedom for entropy**: Before and after this process, the comoving entropy of the system $S \propto g_{*S} (aT)^{3} = \text{const}$ is conserved.
   - Before annihilation (photons + electron-positron pairs): $g_{*S, \text{before}} = g_\gamma + \frac{7}{8}(g_{e^-} + g_{e^+}) = 2 + \frac{7}{8}(2 + 2) = \frac{11}{2}$. Since neutrinos just decoupled, let the photon temperature be $T_{\text{before}} = T_\nu$.
   - After annihilation (only photons remain): $g_{*S, \text{after}} = g_\gamma = 2$. At this point, the photons are heated, yielding a temperature $T_\gamma$.
1. **Derivation result**: Substituting into the entropy conservation equation gives $\frac{11}{2} (aT_\nu)^3 = 2 (aT_\gamma)^3$, which implies:

$$\begin{align}
T_\nu = \left(\frac{4}{11}\right)^{1/3} T_\gamma
\end{align}$$

recombination, $\displaystyle{T\sim \text{eV}}$. atom formation $\displaystyle{e^{-}+p\to H+\gamma}$. when the reaction rate $\displaystyle{\Gamma \sim n_{e}\sigma _{T}}$ is smaller than the expansion rate $\displaystyle{H}$, the reaction freezes out.

the density of these particles is given by

$$\begin{align}
n_{e} & =2\left(\frac{m_{e}T}{2\pi}\right)^{3/2}\exp\left(-\frac{m_{e}-\mu _{e}}{T}\right) \\
n_{p} & =2\left(\frac{m_{p}T}{2\pi}\right)^{3/2}\exp\left(-\frac{m_{p}-\mu _{p}}{T}\right) \\
n_{H} & =4\left(\frac{m_{H}T}{2\pi}\right)^{3/2}\exp\left(-\frac{m_{H}-\mu _{H}}{T}\right)
\end{align}$$

On the other hand, the reaction $e^- + p \rightleftharpoons H + \gamma$ is in thermal equilibrium before recombination, which enforces the chemical potential relation $\mu_e + \mu_p = \mu_H$ (since photons have $\mu_\gamma = 0$). Taking the ratio of these densities, the chemical potential terms perfectly cancel out:

$$\begin{align}
\frac{n_{e}n_{p}}{n_{H}} & = \frac{2\times 2}{4} \frac{\left(m_{e}m_{p}T^{2}/4\pi^{2}\right)^{3/2}}{\left(m_{H}T/2\pi\right)^{3/2}} \exp\left(-\frac{m_{e}+m_{p}-m_{H}}{T}\right) \exp\left(\frac{\mu_{e}+\mu_{p}-\mu_{H}}{T}\right) \\
 & \approx \left(\frac{m_{e}T}{2\pi}\right)^{3/2}\exp\left(-\frac{E_{0}}{T}\right)
\end{align}$$

where $\displaystyle{E_{0}=m_{e}+m_{p}-m_{H}=13.6\text{ eV}}$ is the ionization energy of hydrogen. Here we used the approximation $m_p \approx m_H$ for the non-exponential prefactor. This is the **Saha equation**.

To make this equation more intuitive, we define the ionization fraction as $\displaystyle{X_{e}=\frac{n_{e}}{n_{B}}}$, where the total baryon density is $n_B = n_p + n_H$.
Since the universe is electrically neutral, $n_e = n_p$. Therefore:
- $n_e = n_p = X_e n_B$
- $n_H = n_B - n_p = (1 - X_e) n_B$

Plugging these into the left side of the Saha equation gives $\frac{(X_e n_B)(X_e n_B)}{(1-X_e)n_B} = \frac{X_e^2}{1-X_e} n_B$. Thus we can rewrite the Saha equation as:

$$\begin{align}
\frac{X_{e}^{2}}{1-X_{e}} & =\frac{1}{n_{B}}\left(\frac{m_{e}T}{2\pi}\right)^{3/2}\exp\left(-\frac{E_{0}}{T}\right)
\end{align}$$

**Physical Insight: Why is $T_{\text{rec}} \approx 0.3 \text{ eV}$ much lower than $E_0 = 13.6 \text{ eV}$?**
We can replace the baryon density with $n_B = \eta n_\gamma = \eta \left(\frac{2\zeta(3)}{\pi^2} T^3\right)$, where $\eta \sim 10^{-10}$ is the baryon-to-photon ratio:

$$\begin{align}
\frac{X_{e}^{2}}{1-X_{e}} & \propto \frac{1}{\eta} \left(\frac{m_e}{T}\right)^{3/2} \exp\left(-\frac{E_{0}}{T}\right)
\end{align}$$

Because $\eta$ is extremely small (billions of photons for every baryon), the prefactor $1/\eta \sim 10^{10}$ strongly drives $X_e$ towards $1$ (fully ionized). Even when $T$ drops well below $E_0 = 13.6\text{ eV}$, the energetic tail of the vast photon sea still contains enough photons with $E > 13.6\text{ eV}$ to easily destroy any newly formed hydrogen. The temperature must drop all the way to $\sim 0.32\text{ eV}$ before the high-energy photon tail is depleted enough to allow neutral hydrogen to survive.

 & \approx \eta _{B}\left(\frac{T}{m_{p}}\right)^{3/2}\exp\left(\frac{B_D}{T}\right)
\end{align}$$

where $\eta_B = n_B / n_\gamma \sim 10^{-10}$ is the baryon-to-photon ratio. when $\displaystyle{n_{D}/n_{B}\sim 1}$, we can derive the temperature of deuterium formation as
