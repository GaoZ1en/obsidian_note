## Superspace for 4d $\mathcal{N}=1$ supersymmetry

We keep the same 4d Minkowski background and $\mathcal{N}=1$ notation as before. Superspace extends ordinary spacetime by adding two Grassmann spinor coordinates:
$$
z^M=(x^\mu,\theta^\alpha,\bar\theta_{\dot\alpha}).
$$
The coordinates $\theta^\alpha$ and $\bar\theta_{\dot\alpha}$ anti-commute with each other, so every superfield has a finite Taylor expansion in the fermionic coordinates.

One useful realization of the supercharges as differential operators on superspace is
$$
\begin{align}
Q_\alpha
&= \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta^\alpha}
-i(\sigma^\mu\bar\theta)_\alpha\partial_\mu,\\
\bar Q_{\dot\alpha}
&= -\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar\theta^{\dot\alpha}}
+i(\theta\sigma^\mu)_{\dot\alpha}\partial_\mu.
\end{align}
$$
In this differential-operator representation,
$$
\{Q_\alpha,\bar Q_{\dot\beta}\}
=2i(\sigma^\mu)_{\alpha\dot\beta}\partial_\mu,
$$
which is the superspace version of $\{Q_\alpha,\bar Q_{\dot\beta}\}=2(\sigma^\mu)_{\alpha\dot\beta}P_\mu$ after fixing the convention for the translation generator.

The covariant derivatives are defined with the opposite signs in the spacetime-translation terms:
$$
\begin{align}
D_\alpha
&= \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta^\alpha}
+i(\sigma^\mu\bar\theta)_\alpha\partial_\mu,\\
\bar D_{\dot\alpha}
&= -\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar\theta^{\dot\alpha}}
-i(\theta\sigma^\mu)_{\dot\alpha}\partial_\mu.
\end{align}
$$
They satisfy
$$
\{D_\alpha,\bar D_{\dot\beta}\}
=-2i(\sigma^\mu)_{\alpha\dot\beta}\partial_\mu,
\qquad
\{D,Q\}=\{D,\bar Q\}=\{\bar D,Q\}=\{\bar D,\bar Q\}=0.
$$
Since $D_\alpha$ and $\bar D_{\dot\alpha}$ anti-commute with the supercharges, constraints written in terms of $D$ and $\bar D$ are preserved by supersymmetry. This is the main reason superspace is efficient.

## Superfields and supersymmetry transformations

A superfield is a function on superspace. The most important constrained superfield for matter is the chiral superfield,
$$
\bar D_{\dot\alpha}\Phi=0.
$$
The constraint is solved by using the chiral coordinate
$$
y^\mu=x^\mu+i\theta\sigma^\mu\bar\theta,
$$
so that
$$
\Phi(y,\theta)=\phi(y)+\sqrt{2}\theta\psi(y)+\theta\theta F(y).
$$
The component fields are a complex scalar $\phi$, a Weyl fermion $\psi_\alpha$, and a complex auxiliary scalar $F$. The auxiliary field is needed for off-shell closure of the supersymmetry algebra.

Supersymmetry transformations are generated by
$$
\delta_\epsilon=\epsilon^\alpha Q_\alpha+\bar\epsilon_{\dot\alpha}\bar Q^{\dot\alpha}.
$$
Reading off the components of $\delta_\epsilon\Phi$ gives the off-shell transformations
$$
\begin{align}
\delta_\epsilon\phi
&=\sqrt{2}\epsilon^\alpha\psi_\alpha,\\
\delta_\epsilon\psi_\alpha
&=i\sqrt{2}(\sigma^\mu\bar\epsilon)_\alpha\partial_\mu\phi
+\sqrt{2}\epsilon_\alpha F,\\
\delta_\epsilon F
&=i\sqrt{2}\bar\epsilon_{\dot\alpha}
(\bar\sigma^\mu)^{\dot\alpha\beta}\partial_\mu\psi_\beta.
\end{align}
$$
Together with the conjugate transformations, these are the component rules used in the free Wess-Zumino model.

## Wess-Zumino model in superspace

The interacting Wess-Zumino model is written compactly as
$$
S=\int d^4x\,d^4\theta\,\Phi^\dagger\Phi
+\left(\int d^4x\,d^2\theta\,W(\Phi)+\text{h.c.}\right).
$$
The first term is a $D$-term integral over all fermionic coordinates. It gives the kinetic terms for $\phi$ and $\psi$, together with the auxiliary term $F^*F$. The second term is an $F$-term integral over chiral superspace. It is supersymmetric because the highest chiral component changes by a total derivative.

In components, the superpotential part gives
$$
\mathcal{L}_W
=F W'(\phi)-\frac12 W''(\phi)\psi\psi+\text{h.c.}
$$
The auxiliary-field equation is
$$
F^*=-W'(\phi),
\qquad
F=-W'(\phi)^*.
$$
After eliminating $F$, the scalar potential is
$$
V(\phi)=|W'(\phi)|^2.
$$
Thus holomorphy of the superpotential controls both the Yukawa couplings and the scalar potential. The superspace notation packages this relation into one short expression.

## Super Yang-Mills theory

Gauge fields are described by a real vector superfield
$$
V=V^\dagger.
$$
For a non-Abelian gauge group, the gauge transformation is most naturally written as
$$
e^V\mapsto e^{-i\Lambda^\dagger}e^V e^{i\Lambda},
$$
where $\Lambda$ is a chiral gauge parameter. In the Abelian case this reduces to
$$
V\mapsto V+i(\Lambda-\Lambda^\dagger).
$$

Many components of $V$ are gauge artifacts. In Wess-Zumino gauge,
$$
V_{\mathrm{WZ}}
=-\theta\sigma^\mu\bar\theta A_\mu
+i\theta\theta\bar\theta\bar\lambda
-i\bar\theta\bar\theta\theta\lambda
+\frac12\theta\theta\bar\theta\bar\theta D.
$$
The physical multiplet is
$$
(A_\mu,\lambda_\alpha,D),
$$
where $A_\mu$ is the gauge field, $\lambda_\alpha$ is the gaugino, and $D$ is a real auxiliary field.

The gauge-covariant chiral field-strength superfield is
$$
W_\alpha=-\frac14\bar D^2(e^{-V}D_\alpha e^V).
$$
In the non-Abelian theory the exponential form must be kept, and $W_\alpha$ transforms covariantly under gauge transformations.

The pure super Yang-Mills action is
$$
S_{\mathrm{SYM}}
=\frac{1}{4g^2}\int d^4x\,d^2\theta\,\mathrm{Tr}(W^\alpha W_\alpha)
+\text{h.c.}
$$
In components it contains the Yang-Mills kinetic term, the gaugino kinetic term, and the auxiliary $D$ term:
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SYM}}
=\frac{1}{g^2}\mathrm{Tr}\left(
-\frac14 F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}
-i\bar\lambda\bar\sigma^\mu D_\mu\lambda
+\frac12 D^2
\right).
$$
Matter chiral multiplets in a gauge representation are coupled by replacing the kinetic term with
$$
\int d^4x\,d^4\theta\,\Phi^\dagger e^V\Phi.
$$

## Supersymmetric QCD

Supersymmetric QCD is the $\mathcal{N}=1$ supersymmetric completion of ordinary QCD with gauge group
$$
G=SU(N_c).
$$
The gauge sector is a vector multiplet in the adjoint representation,
$$
V=V^aT^a,\qquad W_\alpha=W_\alpha^aT^a,
$$
with component fields
$$
(A_\mu^a,\lambda_\alpha^a,D^a).
$$
Thus the gluon is accompanied by a gluino, and the auxiliary field $D^a$ closes the vector multiplet off-shell.

The matter sector consists of $N_f$ flavors of chiral superfields
$$
Q_i\in \mathbf{N_c},\qquad
\widetilde Q^i\in \overline{\mathbf{N_c}},
\qquad i=1,\dots,N_f.
$$
Their component fields are
$$
Q_i=(q_i,\psi_{q_i},F_{q_i}),
\qquad
\widetilde Q^i=(\widetilde q^i,\psi_{\widetilde q^i},F_{\widetilde q^i}).
$$
The Weyl fermions $\psi_{q_i}$ and $\psi_{\widetilde q^i}$ are the quark fields in chiral notation, while $q_i$ and $\widetilde q^i$ are their scalar superpartners. we should notice that $\displaystyle{Q_{i}}$ and $\displaystyle{\tilde{Q}^{i}}$ are not conjugate to each other. 

In matrix notation, the superspace action is
$$
\begin{align}
S_{\mathrm{SQCD}}
&=\frac{1}{4g^2}\int d^4x\,d^2\theta\,
\mathrm{Tr}(W^\alpha W_\alpha)+\text{h.c.}\\
&\quad+\int d^4x\,d^4\theta\,
\left(
Q_i^\dagger e^V Q_i
+\widetilde Q_i e^{-V}\widetilde Q^{\dagger i}
\right)\\
&\quad+\left(
\int d^4x\,d^2\theta\,W_{\mathrm{SQCD}}
+\text{h.c.}
\right).
\end{align}
$$
The massless theory has
$$
W_{\mathrm{SQCD}}=0.
$$
A supersymmetric quark mass deformation is
$$
W_{\mathrm{SQCD}}=m_i{}^j\,\widetilde Q^i Q_j.
$$

For $m_i{}^j=0$, the standard non-anomalous continuous global symmetry is
$$
SU(N_f)_L\times SU(N_f)_R\times U(1)_B\times U(1)_R.
$$
The baryon number assignments are opposite for $Q$ and $\widetilde Q$.

The auxiliary $D^a$ field gives the scalar potential
$$
V_D=\frac{g^2}{2}\sum_a
\left(
q_i^\dagger T^a q_i-\widetilde q_i T^a\widetilde q_i^\dagger
\right)^2.
$$
For the massless theory the classical vacua are described by $F=0$ and $D=0$ modulo gauge transformations. Useful gauge-invariant chiral operators are the mesons
$$
M^i{}_j=\widetilde Q^i Q_j,
$$
and, when $N_f\geq N_c$, the baryons
$$
B\sim Q^{N_c},
\qquad
\widetilde B\sim \widetilde Q^{N_c}.
$$
These operators are the natural coordinates for discussing the moduli space of SQCD.

## Supersymmetric extension of the Standard Model

The minimal supersymmetric extension of the Standard Model is the MSSM. Its gauge group is the same as the Standard Model gauge group,
$$
SU(3)_c\times SU(2)_L\times U(1)_Y.
$$
The corresponding vector superfields are
$$
V_3,\qquad V_2,\qquad V_1,
$$
whose fermionic components are the gluino, wino, and bino.

For each generation $i=1,2,3$, the matter chiral superfields are

| superfield | representation |
|---|---|
| $Q_i$ | $(\mathbf{3},\mathbf{2})_{1/6}$ |
| $U_i^c$ | $(\overline{\mathbf{3}},\mathbf{1})_{-2/3}$ |
| $D_i^c$ | $(\overline{\mathbf{3}},\mathbf{1})_{1/3}$ |
| $L_i$ | $(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{2})_{-1/2}$ |
| $E_i^c$ | $(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{1})_1$ |

Each Standard Model Weyl fermion is placed in a chiral multiplet, so the MSSM also contains scalar quarks and scalar leptons. The supersymmetric gauge kinetic terms are
$$
S_{\mathrm{gauge}}
=\sum_{a=1,2,3}\frac{1}{4g_a^2}
\int d^4x\,d^2\theta\,
\mathrm{Tr}(W_a^\alpha W_{a\alpha})
+\text{h.c.},
$$
where the trace is only needed for the non-Abelian factors. The matter kinetic terms have the schematic form
$$
S_{\mathrm{matter}}
=\int d^4x\,d^4\theta\,
\sum_\Phi \Phi^\dagger e^{V_3+V_2+Y_\Phi V_1}\Phi,
$$
with the understanding that only the gauge factors acting on $\Phi$ are present, and representation matrices and conventional factors of the gauge couplings are suppressed.

The MSSM needs two Higgs chiral superfields,
$$
H_u:(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{2})_{1/2},
\qquad
H_d:(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{2})_{-1/2}.
$$
There are two reasons for this. First, the superpotential must be holomorphic, so one cannot use $H^\dagger$ to write both up-type and down-type Yukawa couplings. Second, the higgsinos contribute to gauge anomalies, and the pair $(H_u,H_d)$ is anomaly-free.

The renormalizable R-parity preserving MSSM superpotential is
$$
W_{\mathrm{MSSM}}
=(y_u)^{ij}Q_i\cdot H_u\,U_j^c
-(y_d)^{ij}Q_i\cdot H_d\,D_j^c
-(y_e)^{ij}L_i\cdot H_d\,E_j^c
+\mu H_u\cdot H_d.
$$
Here
$$
A\cdot B=\epsilon_{ab}A^aB^b
$$
is the $SU(2)_L$ invariant contraction. The first three terms reproduce the Standard Model Yukawa couplings after electroweak symmetry breaking, and the last term is the supersymmetric Higgs mass term.

R-parity is defined by
$$
R_p=(-1)^{3(B-L)+2s}.
$$
Equivalently, at the superfield level one may use matter parity $(-1)^{3(B-L)}$. Imposing it forbids the renormalizable baryon-number and lepton-number violating terms
$$
L_iH_u,\qquad
L_iL_jE_k^c,\qquad
L_iQ_jD_k^c,\qquad
U_i^cD_j^cD_k^c.
$$
This is the standard way to keep the MSSM close to the observed Standard Model at low energies.

The exact supersymmetric MSSM still predicts degenerate Standard Model particles and superpartners. Phenomenology therefore adds soft supersymmetry-breaking terms. In schematic form,
$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{soft}}^{\mathrm{MSSM}}
\supset
&-\sum_\Phi m_\Phi^2|\phi_\Phi|^2
-\left(
\frac12\sum_{a=1}^3 M_a\lambda_a\lambda_a
+B\mu H_u\cdot H_d
\right.\\
&\left.
+(A_u)^{ij}\widetilde Q_i\cdot H_u\,\widetilde U_j^c
-(A_d)^{ij}\widetilde Q_i\cdot H_d\,\widetilde D_j^c
-(A_e)^{ij}\widetilde L_i\cdot H_d\,\widetilde E_j^c
+\text{h.c.}
\right).
\end{align}
$$
These terms split the superpartner spectrum while keeping the ultraviolet behavior controlled in the sense of soft breaking.
Here $\phi_\Phi$ denotes the scalar component of the chiral superfield $\Phi$, while $\lambda_a$ are the gauginos.

## Supersymmetry breaking

In a globally supersymmetric theory the Hamiltonian is non-negative. A supersymmetric vacuum satisfies
$$
Q_\alpha|0\rangle=\bar Q_{\dot\alpha}|0\rangle=0,
$$
and has zero vacuum energy. Supersymmetry is spontaneously broken when the Lagrangian is supersymmetric but the vacuum is not annihilated by the supercharges:
$$
E_{\mathrm{vac}}>0.
$$
The broken fermionic generator then implies the existence of a massless Goldstone fermion, called the goldstino.

For ordinary chiral and vector multiplets, the auxiliary fields make the criterion especially transparent. After eliminating auxiliaries,
$$
V=\sum_i |F_i|^2+\frac12\sum_a D^aD^a.
$$
A supersymmetric vacuum requires all auxiliary expectation values to vanish:
$$
\langle F_i\rangle=0,
\qquad
\langle D^a\rangle=0.
$$

$F$-term breaking occurs when the equations
$$
F_i=-\frac{\partial W^*}{\partial\phi_i^*}=0
$$
cannot be solved simultaneously. This is the mechanism behind O'Raifeartaigh-type models.

$D$-term breaking occurs when the gauge-sector conditions
$$
D^a=0
$$
cannot be solved simultaneously with the other vacuum equations. A Fayet-Iliopoulos term in an Abelian gauge theory is the standard source of this possibility.

This should be distinguished from explicit breaking. In explicit breaking, one adds terms to the Lagrangian that are not invariant under supersymmetry transformations. Then the supersymmetry Ward identities are no longer exact. Soft breaking is a special controlled form of explicit breaking: the breaking terms are chosen so that they do not reintroduce the severe ultraviolet divergences that supersymmetry was meant to cancel.

## Witten index

The Witten index is
$$
\Delta=\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathcal H}(-1)^F e^{-\beta H}.
$$
At positive energy, unbroken supersymmetry pairs bosonic and fermionic states with the same energy, so their contributions cancel. The index therefore counts the signed number of supersymmetric ground states,
$$
\Delta=n_B^{(0)}-n_F^{(0)},
$$
and is stable under continuous deformations that do not change the behavior at infinity in field space.

If
$$
\Delta\neq0,
$$
then at least one zero-energy state must remain, so supersymmetry cannot be spontaneously broken. If
$$
\Delta=0,
$$
one cannot conclude that supersymmetry is necessarily broken. It only means that the index does not obstruct supersymmetry breaking. Flat directions and continuum spectra can require extra care, but the basic lesson is that the Witten index gives a topological obstruction to spontaneous breaking.

## Toward explicit and soft supersymmetry breaking

The model examples above motivate the general question of how to use supersymmetry as a useful organizing principle while allowing the observed low-energy spectrum to be non-supersymmetric. Hard explicit breaking destroys the ultraviolet cancellations in a generic way. Soft breaking keeps the most important ultraviolet improvement by allowing only controlled relevant operators, such as scalar masses, gaugino masses, and holomorphic scalar interactions:
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{soft}}
=-m_i^2\phi_i^*\phi_i
-\left(
\frac12 M_a\lambda^a\lambda^a
+\frac16 A_{ijk}\phi_i\phi_j\phi_k
+\frac12 B_{ij}\phi_i\phi_j
+\text{h.c.}
\right).
$$
This is the bridge from exact supersymmetric field theories to phenomenological supersymmetric models such as the MSSM, where supersymmetry is useful precisely because its explicit breaking can be organized softly.
