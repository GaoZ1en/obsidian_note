# Canonical Quantization for the Free Massive Vector Field in the Global AdS3 Spacetime with the Covariant Phase Formalism
### Abstract

In this paper, we take a canonical quantization for the free massive vector field in the global AdS3 spacetime with the covariant phase space formalism. Here, by taking use of the covariant phase space formalism, we simplify the canonical transformation step of the canonical quantization.

### 1 Introduction

How to take a canonical quantization for gravity is an important and long standing problem in physics [1]. And, benefiting from the AdS/CFT correspondence, it is more solid to study the canonical quantization for gravity in the asymptotic AdS spacetime [2]. Moreover, the recent interest in the gravitational fluctuation's contribution to the holographic entanglement entropy [3] provides more motivations to studying this problem.

It is believed that, by taking use of Dirac's canonical formalism for constrained systems [4- 6], one can take a canonical quantization for gravity at least perturbatively. However, for the momentum, there are only a few concrete results for the canonical quantization for gravity in the asymptotic AdS spacetime.

Here, by concrete results, we mean the results like: constructing the Hilbert space through a complete orthonormal basis, solving the eigenvalues and the eigenstates of the Hamiltonian, computing the amplitudes of diffeomorphism invariant operators,... 1

And the lack of concrete results may be from the technical difficulties. For example, it is very hard to compute the Dirac bracket, where one needs to take an inverse of an infinite dimensional matrix. For example, it is very hard to solve the equations of motion even perturbatively, where the different components of the field couple with each other.

Considering the current situation, it is therefore worthwhile to perform a concrete study for the canonical quantization for gravity in the asymptotic AdS spacetime.

In this paper, as a warm- up, we study the canonical quantization for a simpler model: the free massive vector field in the global AdS $\displaystyle{_3}$ spacetime. The model also shares the difficulties of constraints and different components' coupling. While, it has no gauge redundancies, which saves us from dealing with too many difficulties at the same time.

In dealing with the remaining difficulties, we take use of following tricks:

First, we take use of the covariant phase space formalism [9- 13] in the canonical transformation step of the canonical quantization. Here, the covariant phase space formalism is equivalent to and more convenient than Dirac's canonical formalism for constrained systems. 2

Second, we take use of the Killing symmetries and the corresponding Noether's theorem, in solving the differential equations and in computing the symplectic form. Here, we reorganize the modes into the highest weight representations of the Killing symmetries [16- 18]. And we directly read out the expression of the symplectic form through an argument based on the

Killing symmetries.

As applications of the quantized theory, we compute the correlation function and the thermal partition function. Our results are the same as the ones derived from other methods.

The plan for the rest of the paper is as follows. In section 2, we briefly review the covariant phase space formalism. In section 3, we introduce our model: the free massive vector field in the global AdS $\displaystyle{_3}$ spacetime. In section 4, by taking use of the covariant phase space formalism, we reformulate the model to a Hamiltonian system. In section 5, by introducing a mode expansion, we represent the Hamiltonian system with a proper set of variables. In section 6, we take a canonical quantization for the Hamiltonian system. ### 2 A brief review for the covariant phase space formalism

In this section, we briefly review the covariant phase space formalism. Here, we only introduce the relevant notions and statements, and we illustrate them with a simple example. We refer to [14, 15] for more details of the formalism and the proofs of the statements.

### 2.1 The formalism

We now introduce the covariant phase space formalism.

From a conceptual perspective, the covariant phase space formalism is a prescription that reformulates a Lagrangian system to a Hamiltonian system. Here, the Hamiltonian system is specified by two structures: the pre- phase space, and the symplectic form. The pre- phase space is defined as the set of solutions of the equations of motion. And the symplectic form can be read out from the action.

We now introduce the covariant phase space formalism in more detail.

The starting point of the covariant phase space formalism is a Lagrangian system. As we know, a Lagrangian system is specified by: the fundamental fields $\displaystyle{\phi_{a}(x)}$ , the Lagrangian density, and the action $\displaystyle{S}$ which is an integral of the Lagrangian density in the region $\displaystyle{M}$ sandwiched between an initial Cauchy surface $\displaystyle{\Sigma_{i}}$ and a final Cauchy surface $\displaystyle{\Sigma_{f}}$ . 3 In the framework of the covariant phase space formalism, one furthermore introduces the notion of the set of configurations, which is a set whose elements are the allowed configurations of the fundamental fields $\displaystyle{\phi_{a}(x)}$ . And one then view the action $\displaystyle{S}$ as a function of the set of configurations.

Starting from the Lagrangian system, the covariant phase space formalism can be used to reformulate it to a Hamiltonian system. The prescription is the following: One first takes a variation of the action

$$\begin{align}\delta S = \int_{M}d^{d}x E^{a}[\phi ;x]\delta \phi_{a}(x) + \theta [\phi ;\delta \phi ]|_{\Sigma_{f}} - \theta [\phi ,\delta \phi ]|_{\Sigma_{i}}, \quad (2.1)\end{align}$$

where one keeps the terms at the initial Cauchy surface $\displaystyle{\Sigma_{i}}$ and the final Cauchy surface $\displaystyle{\Sigma_{f}}$ . From the expression of the variation of the action (2.1), one reads out the equations of motion

$$\begin{align}E^{a}[\phi ;x] = 0. \quad (2.2)\end{align}$$

And one reads out the symplectic potential

$$\begin{align}\theta \equiv \theta [\phi ;\delta \phi ]|_{\Sigma}, \quad (2.3)\end{align}$$

which is a one- form field of the set of configurations. Then, one defines the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ as the set of solutions of the equations of motion (2.2). One defines the symplectic form $\displaystyle{\Omega}$ as the exterior derivative of the symplectic potential $\displaystyle{\theta}$ in the set of configuration as

$$\begin{align}\Omega = \delta \theta , \quad (2.4)\end{align}$$

which is a two- form field of the set of configurations. And, by taking a pull back of the symplectic form $\displaystyle{\Omega}$ from the set of configurations to the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ , one defines the symplectic form of the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\Omega |_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}$ , which is time independent

$$\begin{align}\Omega |_{\Sigma_{f},\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}} = \Omega |_{\Sigma_{i},\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}. \quad (2.5)\end{align}$$

The pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ and the symplectic form $\displaystyle{\Omega |_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}$ together specify a Hamiltonian system.

The Hamiltonian system constructed from the covariant phase space formalism can also be represented in the canonical formalism, where a Hamiltonian system is specified by the physical phase space and the bracket. For the application of the current paper, we only consider the system with no gauge redundancies, where the symplectic form $\displaystyle{\Omega |_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}$ is non- degenerate. 4 For such a system, the physical phase space is exactly the pre- phase space, where we also denote the physical phase space by $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ . And the bracket can be represented in terms of the symplectic form. Specifically, given two observables $\displaystyle{f}$ and $\displaystyle{g}$ which are functions of the physical phase space, their bracket can be represented as

$$\begin{align}\{f,g\} |_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}} = -X_{f}\cdot \delta g|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}} = X_{g}\cdot \delta f|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}, \quad (2.6)\end{align}$$

where $\displaystyle{X_{f}}$ and $\displaystyle{X_{g}}$ are determined from $\displaystyle{f}$ and $\displaystyle{g}$ through the Hamiltonian equation as

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{{X_{f}\cdot\Omega|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}=-\delta f|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}}\}\\ \ {{X_{g}\cdot\Omega|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}=-\delta g|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}.}}\end{array} \quad (2.7)\end{align}$$

Associated with the covariant phase space formalism, there is a widely discussed topic: the Noether's theorem. We now introduce the Noether's theorem.

As we know, the Noether's theorem is in terms of the symmetries and the conserved charges. In the framework of the covariant phase space formalism, a symmetry is represented as a vector field in the set of configurations

$$\begin{align}X_{\lambda} = \int d^{d}x\Big(\Delta_{\lambda}\phi_{a}(x)\frac{\delta}{\delta\phi_{a}(x)}\Big), \quad (2.8)\end{align}$$

where $\displaystyle{\Delta_{\lambda}\phi_{a}(x)}$ denotes the infinitesimal symmetry transformation of the field $\displaystyle{\phi_{a}(x)}$ . And the requirement of the symmetry is that the transformation of the action only contains terms at the initial Cauchy surface $\displaystyle{\Sigma_{i}}$ and the final Cauchy surface $\displaystyle{\Sigma_{f}}$ as $\displaystyle{^{5}}$

$$\begin{align}X_{\lambda}\cdot \delta S = \alpha_{\lambda}|_{\Sigma_{f}} - \alpha_{\lambda}|_{\Sigma_{i}}. \quad (2.9)\end{align}$$

Under such a setup, the corresponding Noether charge is

$$\begin{align}Q_{\lambda} = X_{\lambda}\cdot \theta -\alpha_{\lambda}. \quad (2.10)\end{align}$$

And the Noether's theorem is the following three statements:

(1) The Noether charge is time independent under the on-shell condition

$$\begin{align}Q_{\lambda}|_{\Sigma_{f},\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}} = Q_{\lambda}|_{\Sigma_{i},\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}. \quad (2.11)\end{align}$$

Here, in the subscript of the expression (2.11), $\displaystyle{\Sigma_{i}}$ and $\displaystyle{\Sigma_{f}}$ denote evaluating $\displaystyle{Q_{\lambda}}$ on the corresponding Cauchy surfaces, and $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ denotes evaluating $\displaystyle{Q_{\lambda}}$ at the pre- phase space, namely under the on- shell condition.

(2) The vector field $\displaystyle{X_{\lambda}}$ is tangent to the pre-phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ . Namely, the symmetry $\displaystyle{X_{\lambda}}$ maps a solution to a solution.

(3) The symmetry $\displaystyle{X_{\lambda}}$ and the Noether charge $\displaystyle{Q_{\lambda}}$ together satisfy the Hamiltonian equation $\displaystyle{^{6}}$

$$\begin{align}X_{\lambda}\cdot \Omega |_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}} = -\delta Q_{\lambda}|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}. \quad (2.12)\end{align}$$

Moreover, for the system with no gauge redundancies, the Hamiltonian equation (2.12) can be represented in the canonical formalism as

$$\begin{align}\{Q_{\lambda},\phi_{a}(x)\} |_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}} = -\Delta_{\lambda}\phi_{a}(x)|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}, \quad (2.13)\end{align}$$

where one can derive (2.13) by (2.6), (2.8), (2.12).

### 2.2 A simple example: the oscillator

So far, we have introduced the covariant phase space formalism. We now illustrate the formalism with a simple example: the oscillator.

The oscillator is defined by the following action

$$\begin{align}S = \int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}dt\Big(\frac{1}{2} m\dot{x}^{2} - \frac{1}{2} m\omega^{2}x^{2}\Big). \quad (2.14)\end{align}$$

We now apply the oscillator (2.14) to the covariant phase space formalism. Following the prescription, we first take a variation of the action

$$\begin{align}\delta S = \int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}dt\Big(-m(\dot{x} +\omega^{2}x)\Big)\delta x + m\dot{x}\delta x\Big|_{t = t_{f}} - m\dot{x}\delta x\Big|_{t = t_{i}}. \quad (2.15)\end{align}$$

From the variation of the action (2.15), we read out the equations of motion

$$\begin{align}\ddot{x} +\omega^{2}x = 0, \quad (2.16)\end{align}$$

And we read out the symplectic potential

$$\begin{align}\theta = m\dot{x}\delta x. \quad (2.17)\end{align}$$

Then, by solving the equations of motion (2.16), we get the general solution

$$\begin{align}x(t)|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2m\omega}} (e^{-i\omega t}a + e^{i\omega t}a^{*}). \quad (2.18)\end{align}$$

Here, we view $\displaystyle{(a,a^{*})}$ as a set of variables that parameterizes of the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ . By taking an exterior derivative for the symplectic potential (2.17) in the set of configurations, we get the symplectic form

$$\begin{align}\Omega = m\delta_{1}\dot{x}\delta_{2}x - m\delta_{1}x\delta_{2}\dot{x}. \quad (2.19)\end{align}$$

And, by applying the general solution (2.18) to the symplectic form (2.19), we get the symplectic form of the pre- phase

$$\begin{align}\Omega |_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}} = -i\delta_{1}a\delta_{2}a^{*} + i\delta_{1}a^{*}\delta_{2}a, \quad (2.20)\end{align}$$

which is indeed time independent. So far, we have reformulated the oscillator to a Hamiltonian system, which is specified by the set of variables $\displaystyle{(a, a^{*})}$ , the general solution (2.18), and the symplectic form (2.20).

We now study the Noether's theorem for the time shift symmetry. Specifically, we represent the time shift symmetry as

$$\begin{align}X_{t} = \int d t\Big[\dot{x} (t)\frac{\delta}{\delta x(t)}\Big], \quad (2.21)\end{align}$$

which is a vector field of the set of configurations. By applying the time shift symmetry (2.21) to the action (2.14), we get

$$\begin{align}X_{t}\cdot \delta S = \Big(\frac{1}{2} m\dot{x}^{2} - \frac{1}{2} m\omega^{2}x^{2}\Big)\Big|_{t = t_{f}} - \Big(\frac{1}{2} m\dot{x}^{2} - \frac{1}{2} m\omega^{2}x^{2}\Big)\Big|_{t = t_{i}}. \quad (2.22)\end{align}$$

From the expression (2.22), we verify that the time shift symmetry (2.21) is indeed a symmetry in the sense of the Noether's theorem. And we read out the expression of the corresponding $\displaystyle{\alpha_{t}}$ as

$$\begin{align}\alpha_{t} = \frac{1}{2} m\dot{x}^{2} - \frac{1}{2} m\omega^{2}x^{2}. \quad (2.23)\end{align}$$

By applying (2.17), (2.21), (2.23) to (2.10), we get the expression of the Noether charge as

$$\begin{align}H = X_{t}\cdot \theta -\alpha_{t} = \frac{1}{2} m\dot{x}^{2} + \frac{1}{2} m\omega^{2}x^{2}. \quad (2.24)\end{align}$$

And, by applying the general solution (2.18) to the expression of the Noether charge (2.24), we get the expression of the Noether charge at the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ as

$$\begin{align}H|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}} = \omega a^{*}a. \quad (2.25)\end{align}$$

We now provide some cross checks for the Noether's theorem:

(1) The expression of the Noether charge (2.25) is time independent.

(2) The time shift symmetry (2.21) maps a solution in the form of (2.18) to another solution still in the form of (2.18) as

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{r l} & {x(t)\big|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}\rightarrow \Big(x(t) + \epsilon X_{t}\cdot \delta x(t) + o(\epsilon)\Big)\Big|_{\mathcal{P}}}\\ & {\qquad = \Big(x(t) + \epsilon \dot{x} (t) + o(\epsilon)\Big)\big|_{\mathcal{P}}}\\ & {\qquad = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2m\omega}}\Big(e^{-i\omega t}(a - \epsilon \cdot i\omega a) + e^{i\omega t}(a^{*} + \epsilon \cdot i\omega a^{*})\Big) + o(\epsilon),} \end{array} \quad (2.26)\end{align}$$

where $\displaystyle{\epsilon}$ is an infinitesimal symmetry transformation parameter. And, when restricted to the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ , the time shift symmetry is represented as(3) The time shift symmetry (2.27), the symplectic form (2.20), and the Noether charge (2.25) together satisfy the Hamiltonian equation

$$\begin{align}X_{t}|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}} = -i\omega a\frac{\delta}{\delta a} +i\omega a^{*}\frac{\delta}{\delta a^{*}}. \quad (2.27)\end{align}$$

$$\begin{align}X_{t}\cdot \Omega |_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}} = -\delta H|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}. \quad (2.28)\end{align}$$

We now represent the Hamiltonian system in the canonical formalism. Since the symplectic form $\displaystyle{\Omega |_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}$ in (2.20) is non- degenerate, the physical phase space is exactly the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ . We then read out the brackets from the symplectic form $\displaystyle{\Omega |_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}$ (2.20). Specifically, by taking use of the Hamiltonian equation

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{{X_{a}\cdot\Omega|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}=-\delta a|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}}\end{array} \quad (2.29)\end{align}$$

we get

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{{X_{a}|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}=i\frac{\delta}{\delta a^{*}}}}\\ \ {{X_{a^{*}}|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}=-i\frac{\delta}{\delta a}.}}\end{array} \quad (2.30)\end{align}$$

And, by applying (2.30) to (2.6), we get the brackets between the set of variables $\displaystyle{(a,a^{*})}$ as

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{{\{a,a\}|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}=0}}\\ \ {{\{a,a^{*}\}|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}=-i}}\\ \ {{\{a^{*},a\}|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}=i}}\\ \ {{\{a^{*},a^{*}\}|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}=0.}}\end{array} \quad (2.31)\end{align}$$

Moreover, by taking use of (2.18), (2.25), (2.31), we can check

$$\begin{align}\{H,x(t)\} |_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}} = -\dot{x} (t)|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}, \quad (2.32)\end{align}$$

which is the Hamiltonian equation for the time shift symmetry in the canonical formalism.

In this section, we introduce our model: the free massive vector field in the global AdS $\displaystyle{_3}$ spacetime.

We first introduce the global AdS $\displaystyle{_3}$ spacetime. Specifically, we introduce a coordinate system $\displaystyle{(t,r,\phi)}$ with

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{{t\in(-\infty,+\infty)}}\\ \ {{r\in(0,+\infty)}}\\ \ {{\phi\sim\phi+2\pi.}}\end{array} \quad (3.1)\end{align}$$

And we represent the metric as

$$\begin{align}d s^{2} = -(r^{2} + 1)d t^{2} + \frac{d r^{2}}{r^{2} + 1} +r^{2}d\phi^{2}. \quad (3.2)\end{align}$$

For the application below, we compute the connection as

$$\begin{align}\left\{ \begin{array}{l l}{\Gamma_{t t}^{t} = 0}\\ \ {\Gamma_{t t}^{r} = r^{3} + r}\\ \ {\Gamma_{t t}^{\phi} = 0} \end{array} \right. \qquad \left\{ \begin{array}{l l}{\Gamma_{t r}^{t} = \frac{r}{r^{2} + 1}}\\ \ {\Gamma_{r r}^{r} = 0}\\ \ {\Gamma_{r r}^{\phi} = 0} \end{array} \right. \qquad \left\{ \begin{array}{l l}{\Gamma_{t\phi}^{t} = 0}\\ \ {\Gamma_{t\phi}^{r} = 0}\\ \ {\Gamma_{r\phi}^{\phi} = 0} \end{array} \right. \qquad \left\{ \begin{array}{l l}{\Gamma_{t\phi}^{t} = 0}\\ \ {\Gamma_{r\phi}^{r} = 0}\\ \ {\Gamma_{r\phi}^{\phi} = -r^{3} - r} \end{array} \right. \quad (3.3)\end{align}$$

the Riemann tensor as

$$\begin{align}R_{\mu \nu \rho \sigma} = g_{\mu \sigma}g_{\nu \rho} - g_{\mu \rho}g_{\nu \sigma}, \quad (3.4)\end{align}$$

and the Ricci tensor as

$$\begin{align}R_{\mu \nu} = -2g_{\mu \nu}. \quad (3.5)\end{align}$$

We now define the free massive vector field in the global AdS $\displaystyle{^3}$ spacetime. We adopt the asymptotic boundary conditions

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{A^{t} = o(r^{-2})}\\ \ {A^{r} = o(r^{-1})}\\ \ {A^{\phi} = o(r^{-2}).} \end{array} \quad (3.6)\end{align}$$

Here, for a quantity $\displaystyle{f}$ satisfying the asymptotic behavior

$$\begin{align}f = o(r^{\lambda}), \quad (3.7)\end{align}$$

we also assume that its derivatives satisfy the following asymptotic behavior

$$\begin{align}\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{n}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi^{p}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r^{m}} f = o(r^{\lambda -m}). \quad (3.8)\end{align}$$

We represent the action as

$$\begin{align}S = \int_{M}d^{3}x\sqrt{-g}\Big(-\frac{1}{4} F_{\mu \nu}F^{\mu \nu} - \frac{1}{2}\mu^{2}A_{\mu}A^{\mu}\Big). \quad (3.9)\end{align}$$

Here, we denote

$$\begin{align}A_{\mu} = g_{\mu \nu}A^{\nu}, \quad (3.10)\end{align}$$

and

$$\begin{align}F_{\mu \nu} = \nabla_{\mu}A_{\nu} - \nabla_{\nu}A_{\mu}, \quad (3.11)\end{align}$$

and

$$\begin{align}F^{\mu \nu} = g^{\mu \rho}g^{\nu \sigma}F_{\rho \sigma}. \quad (3.12)\end{align}$$

We set

$$\begin{align}\mu >0. \quad (3.13)\end{align}$$

And we choose $\displaystyle{M}$ to be the region sandwiched between the initial Cauchy surface $\displaystyle{\Sigma_{i}}$ at $\displaystyle{t = t_{i}}$ and the final Cauchy surface $\displaystyle{\Sigma_{f}}$ at $\displaystyle{t = t_{f}}$ .

We can check that, under the asymptotic boundary conditions (3.6), the near boundary region's contribution to the integral over $\displaystyle{M}$ in the action (3.9) is finite. 7

### 4 Reformulating the model to a Hamiltonian system

In this section, by taking use of the covariant phase space formalism, we reformulate our model to a Hamiltonian system. And we also provide a general discussion for the Noether's theorem associated with the Killing symmetries.

### 4.1 Reformulating the model to a Hamiltonian system

We now apply our model to the covariant phase space formalism.

Following the prescription, we first take a variation of the action

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{\delta S = \int_{M}d^{3}x\sqrt{-g}\Big[(\nabla^{\nu}F_{\nu \mu} - \mu^{2}A_{\mu})\delta A^{\mu} - \nabla^{\mu}(F_{\mu \nu}\delta A^{\nu})\Big]} \\
\ {= \int_{M}d^{3}x\sqrt{-g}(\nabla^{\nu}F_{\nu \mu} - \mu^{2}A_{\mu})\delta A^{\mu}} \\
\ {+\int_{\Sigma_{f}}d^{2}x\sqrt{\sigma}\tau^{\mu}F_{\mu \nu}\delta A^{\nu} - \int_{\Sigma_{i}}d^{2}x\sqrt{\sigma}\tau^{\mu}F_{\mu \nu}\delta A^{\nu}.} \end{array} \quad (4.1)\end{align}$$

Here, $\displaystyle{\sigma_{ab}}$ is the induced metric of the constant $\displaystyle{t}$ Cauchy surfaces with the expression

$$\begin{align}\sigma_{ab}dx^{a}dx^{b} = \frac{dr^{2}}{r^{2} + 1} +r^{2}d\phi^{2}. \quad (4.2)\end{align}$$

$\displaystyle{\tau^{\mu}}$ is the future- pointing unit normal vector orthogonal to the constant $\displaystyle{t}$ Cauchy surfaces with the expression

$$\begin{align}\tau^{\mu} = \Big(\frac{1}{(r^{2} + 1)^{\frac{1}{2}}},0,0\Big). \quad (4.3)\end{align}$$

The near boundary region's contributions to the integrals over $\displaystyle{M}$ , $\displaystyle{\Sigma_{i}}$ , $\displaystyle{\Sigma_{f}}$ in the final expression are finite. And, in deriving (4.1), we have used that the asymptotic boundary term from the integration by parts vanishes. 8

From the variation of the action (4.1), we read out the equations of motion as

$$\begin{align}\nabla_{\nu}F^{\nu \mu} - \mu^{2}A^{\mu} = 0, \quad (4.4)\end{align}$$

or equivalently as

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}\nabla_{\nu}\nabla^{\nu}A^{\mu} - (\mu^{2} - 2)A^{\mu} = 0\ \nabla_{\mu}A^{\mu} = 0. \end{array} \quad (4.5)\end{align}$$

And we read out the symplectic potential as

$$\begin{align}\theta = \int_{\Sigma}d^{2}x\sqrt{\sigma}\tau^{\mu}F_{\mu \nu}\delta A^{\nu}. \quad (4.6)\end{align}$$

Here, $\displaystyle{\Sigma}$ is a constant $\displaystyle{t}$ Cauchy surface. And $\displaystyle{\theta}$ is a one- form field of the set of configurations.

Then, we define the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ as the set of solutions of the equations of motion (4.4). By taking an exterior derivative of the symplectic potential (4.6) in the set of configurations, we get the symplectic form

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{\Omega = \int d^{2}x\sqrt{\sigma}\Big(\tau^{\mu}\delta_{1}F_{\mu \nu}\delta_{2}A^{\nu} - \tau^{\mu}\delta_{1}A^{\nu}\delta_{2}F_{\mu \nu}\Big)}\\ \ {= \int_{\Sigma}d^{2}x\sqrt{\sigma}\Big[\tau^{\mu}(\nabla_{\mu}\delta_{1}A_{\nu} - \nabla_{\nu}\delta_{1}A_{\mu})\delta_{2}A^{\nu} - \tau^{\mu}\delta_{1}A^{\nu}(\nabla_{\mu}\delta_{2}A_{\nu} - \nabla_{\nu}\delta_{2}A_{\mu})\Big],} \end{array} \quad (4.7)\end{align}$$

which is a two- form field of the set of configurations. And, by taking a pullback of the symplectic form from the set of configurations to the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ , we get the symplectic form $\displaystyle{\Omega |_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}$ of the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ .

So far, we have reformulated our model to a Hamiltonian system, which is specified by the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ and the symplectic form $\displaystyle{\Omega |_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}$ .

We now point out more structures in the Hamiltonian system, which arise from that our model is a free theory. Specifically, we view the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ as a linear space. We construct an isomorphism between the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ and the tangent space at the individual point of the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ as the following: Given a solution $\displaystyle{A_{i}^{\mu}}$ of the equations of motion (4.4) which is a point in the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ , we construct the corresponding vector in the tangent space at the individual point of the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ as

$$\begin{align}X_{A_{i}} = \int d^{3}xA_{i}^{\mu}(x)\frac{\delta}{\delta A^{\mu}(x)}. \quad (4.8)\end{align}$$

We also construct a two- form $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\Omega} [\cdot ;\cdot ]|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}$ of the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ as

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{r l} & {\widetilde{\Omega} [A_{1};A_{2}]\big|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}} = X_{A_{2}}\cdot (X_{A_{1}}\cdot \Omega \big|_{A = \widetilde{A}})\big|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}\\ & {\qquad = \int_{\Sigma}d^{2}x\sqrt{\sigma}\Big[\tau^{\mu}(\nabla_{\mu}A_{1,\nu} - \nabla_{\nu}A_{1,\mu})A_{2}^{\nu} - \tau^{\mu}A_{1}^{\nu}(\nabla_{\mu}A_{2,\nu} - \nabla_{\nu}A_{2,\mu})\Big]\Big|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}.} \end{array} \quad (4.9)\end{align}$$

Here, the two- form $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\Omega} [\cdot ;\cdot ]|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}$ contracts with the point of the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ that is already viewed as a linear space. $\displaystyle{A_{1}^{\mu}}$ , $\displaystyle{A_{2}^{\mu}}$ , $\displaystyle{\widetilde{A}^{\mu}}$ are the solutions of the equations of motion (4.4), which are points of the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ . The subscript $\displaystyle{A = \widetilde{A}}$ in $\displaystyle{\Omega |_{A = \widetilde{A}}}$ denotes evaluating the symplectic form $\displaystyle{\Omega}$ at the point $\displaystyle{A^{\mu} = \widetilde{A}^{\mu}}$ of the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ . $\displaystyle{X_{A_{1}}}$ and $\displaystyle{X_{A_{2}}}$ are constructed as

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{{X_{A_{1}}=\int d^{3}x A_{1}^{\mu}(x)\frac{\delta}{\delta A^{\mu}(x)}}}\\ \ {{X_{A_{2}}=\int d^{3}x A_{2}^{\mu}(x)\frac{\delta}{\delta A^{\mu}(x)},}}\end{array} \quad (4.10)\end{align}$$

which are vectors of the tangent space at the point $\displaystyle{A^{\mu} = \widetilde{A}^{\mu}}$ of the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ . And the subscript $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ is to remind that $\displaystyle{A_{1}^{\mu}}$ , $\displaystyle{A_{2}^{\mu}}$ , $\displaystyle{\widetilde{A}^{\mu}}$ satisfy the equations of motion.

### 4.2 The Noether's theorem associated with the Killing symmetries

For the application below, we now provide a general discussion for the Noether's theorem associated with the Killing symmetries.

We first represent the Killing symmetries. Specifically, we represent a Killing symmetry as a vector field in the set of configurations with the following expression

$$\begin{align}X_{\xi} = \int d^{3}x\mathcal{L}_{\xi}A^{\mu}(x)\frac{\delta}{\delta A^{\mu}(x)}. \quad (4.11)\end{align}$$

Here, $\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}_{\xi}A^{\mu}}$ is as

## 5 Representing the Hamiltonian system with a proper set of variables

So far, we have reformulated the model to a Hamiltonian system. In this section, by taking use of the mode expansion, we represent the Hamiltonian system with a proper set of variables.

### 5.1 The framework

Before going to the technical details, we first introduce the framework:

We solve out a set of complete and independent modes $\displaystyle{f_{N}^{\mu}(x)}$ of the equations of motion (4.4). With the set of modes $\displaystyle{f_{N}^{\mu}(x)}$ , we represent the general solution in the form of a mode expansion as

$$\begin{align}A^{\mu}(x)|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}} = \sum_{N}f_{N}^{\mu}(x)a_{N}. \quad (5.1)\end{align}$$

We view the coefficients $\displaystyle{a_{N}}$ in the mode expansion (5.1) as a set of variables that parameterize the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ which is the set of solutions. And, by applying the general solution (5.1) to the symplectic form (4.7), we compute the expression of the symplectic form represented with the set of variables $\displaystyle{a_{N}}$ as

$$\begin{align}\Omega |_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}} = \sum_{N_{1},N_{2}}\widetilde{\Omega} [f_{N_{1}};f_{N_{2}}]\big|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}\cdot \delta_{1}a_{N_{1}}\delta_{2}a_{N_{2}}, \quad (5.2)\end{align}$$

where $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\Omega} [\cdot ;\cdot ]|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}$ is defined in (4.9).

Below, we will explicitly solve the modes and compute the expression of the symplectic form.

### 5.2 The Killing symmetries

We will solve the modes and compute the expression of the symplectic form with the Killing symmetries. For the application below, we now introduce the Killing symmetries.

We first introduce the Killing fields of the metric (3.2). Specifically, we represent the independent Killing fields as

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{\xi_{1} = \frac{1}{2} e^{i(t - \phi)}\Big(\frac{r}{\sqrt{r^{2} + 1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t} -i\sqrt{r^{2} + 1}\frac{\partial}{\partial r} -\frac{\sqrt{r^{2} + 1}}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\Big)}\\ \ {\xi_{0} = \frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial t} -\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\Big)}\\ \ {\xi_{-1} = \frac{1}{2} e^{-i(t - \phi)}\Big(\frac{r}{\sqrt{r^{2} + 1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t} +i\sqrt{r^{2} + 1}\frac{\partial}{\partial r} -\frac{\sqrt{r^{2} + 1}}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\Big)}\\ \ {\bar{\xi}_{1} = \frac{1}{2} e^{i(t + \phi)}\Big(\frac{r}{\sqrt{r^{2} + 1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t} -i\sqrt{r^{2} + 1}\frac{\partial}{\partial r} +\frac{\sqrt{r^{2} + 1}}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\Big)}\\ \ {\bar{\xi}_{0} = \frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial t} +\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\Big)}\\ \ {\bar{\xi}_{-1} = \frac{1}{2} e^{-i(t + \phi)}\Big(\frac{r}{\sqrt{r^{2} + 1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t} +i\sqrt{r^{2} + 1}\frac{\partial}{\partial r} +\frac{\sqrt{r^{2} + 1}}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\Big).} \end{array} \quad (5.3)\end{align}$$

$$\begin{align}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi} = -\xi_{0} + \bar{\xi}_{0}. \quad (5.19)\end{align}$$

### 6 Canonical quantization

So far, we have reformulated our model to a Hamiltonian system. We now take a canonical quantization for the Hamiltonian system. And, through the canonical quantization, we promote the Hamiltonian system to a quantum mechanical system.

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{{A^{\mu}(x)=\sum_{k,\bar{k}=0}^{\infty}\left(f_{+,u;k,\bar{k}}{}^{\mu}(x)a_{u;k,\bar{k}}+f_{+,v;k,\bar{k}}{}^{\mu}(x)a_{v;n,\bar{n}}\right.}}\\ \ {{\left.\qquad+f_{-,u;k,\bar{k}}{}^{\mu}(x)a_{u;k,\bar{k}}{}^{\dagger}+f_{-,v;k,\bar{k}}{}^{\mu}(x)a_{v;k,\bar{k}}{}^{\dagger}\right)}}\end{array} \quad (6.1)\end{align}$$

## A The equivalence between the covariant phase space formalism and Dirac's canonical formalism

In this appendix, we provide an argument showing the equivalence between the covariant phase space formalism and Dirac's canonical formalism [4- 6].

Specifically, we consider a model that fits for Dirac's canonical formalism. We construct a Hamiltonian system by applying the model to the covariant phase space formalism. And we show that this Hamiltonian system is equivalent to the Hamiltonian system constructed from Dirac's canonical formalism.

### A.1 Convention

We first specify some conventions adopted in this appendix:- We distinguish between the variables and the values of the variables for a given configuration. For example, we denote the variables by $\displaystyle{q^{a}}$ , $\displaystyle{(q^{a}, \dot{q}^{a})}$ , $\displaystyle{(q^{a}, p_{a})}$ . And we denote the values of the variables for a given configuration at a given time by $\displaystyle{q^{a}(t)}$ , $\displaystyle{(q^{a}(t), \frac{d}{dt} q^{a}(t))}$ , $\displaystyle{(q^{a}(t), p_{a}(t))}$ .

values of the variables for a given configuration at a given time by $\displaystyle{q^{a}(t)}$ , $\displaystyle{(q^{a}(t), \frac{d}{dt} q^{a}(t))}$ , $\displaystyle{(q^{a}(t), p_{a}(t))}$ .

- We never use the notation $\displaystyle{\dot{q}^{a}(t)}$ . Instead, we denote by $\displaystyle{\frac{d}{dt} q^{a}(t)}$ for the time derivative of the variable $\displaystyle{q^{a}}$ for a given configuration at a given time.

- We distinguish between the variables and the corresponding components of a map to a set of variables that contains these variables. For example, we denote the momenta variables as $\displaystyle{p_{a}}$ . And we denote the momenta components of the Legendre transformation by $\displaystyle{p_{a}(q, \dot{q})}$ , which are functions of $\displaystyle{(q^{a}, \dot{q}^{a})}$ .

- We refer to the system (A.18) as the Legendre transformed system instead of the Hamiltonian system, since we have already used the Hamiltonian system for a different structure.

- When referring to a function of a set of variables, we also associate the set of variables to the function's notation, for example $\displaystyle{L(q, \dot{q})}$ , $\displaystyle{H(q, p)}$ . When referring to the value of the function for a given configuration at a given time, we explicitly apply the values of the variables to the corresponding positions of the function, for example $\displaystyle{L(q(t), \frac{d}{dt} q(t))}$ , $\displaystyle{H(q(t), p(t))}$ .

### A.2 The setup

{setup}

We now introduce the setup.

We consider a general $\displaystyle{0 + 1}$ dimensional model that fits for Dirac's canonical formalism with the following action

$$\begin{align}S = \int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}} d t L\Big(q(t), \frac{d}{d t} q(t)\Big). \quad (A.1)\end{align}$$

Here, $\displaystyle{q^{a}}$ with $\displaystyle{a = 1, 2, \ldots , N}$ are the set of variables. And the Lagrangian $\displaystyle{L(q, \dot{q})}$ is viewed as a function of $\displaystyle{(q^{a}, \dot{q}^{a})}$ . Moreover, we assume that there are constraints but no gauge redundancies in the model (A.1). Here, the precise meaning of no gauge redundancies will be specified below.

We now apply the model (A.1) to the covariant phase space formalism. Following the prescription, we take a variation of the action (A.1)

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{\delta S = \int_{t_i}^{t_f}dt(-1)\left(\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}^a}\left(q(t),\frac{d}{dt} q(t)\right)\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q^a}\left(q(t),\frac{d}{dt} q(t)\right)\right)\delta q^a (t)}\\ \ {+\left.\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}^a}\left(q(t),\frac{d}{dt} q(t)\right)\delta q^a (t)\right|_{t = t_f} - \left.\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}^a}\left(q(t),\frac{d}{dt} q(t)\right)\delta q^a (t)\right|_{t = t_i}.} \end{array} \quad (A.2)\end{align}$$

From the variation of the action (A.2), we read out the equations of motion as

$$\begin{align}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}^a}\left(q(t),\frac{d}{dt} q(t)\right)\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q^a}\left(q(t),\frac{d}{dt} q(t)\right) = 0. \quad (A.3)\end{align}$$

And we read out the symplectic potential as

$$\begin{align}\theta = \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}^a}\left(q(t),\frac{d}{dt} q(t)\right)\delta q^a (t). \quad (A.4)\end{align}$$

Then, we define the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ as the set of solutions of the equations of motion (A.3). By taking an exterior derivative of the symplectic potential (A.4) in the set of configurations, we define the symplectic form as

$$\begin{align}\Omega = \delta_{1}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}^{a}}\left(q(t),\frac{d}{dt} q(t)\right)\right)\delta_{2}q^{a}(t) - \delta_{1}q^{a}(t)\delta_{2}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}^{a}}\left(q(t),\frac{d}{dt} q(t)\right)\right). \quad (A.5)\end{align}$$

And, by taking a pull back of the symplectic form $\displaystyle{\Omega}$ (A.5) from the set of configurations to the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ , we define the symplectic form of the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\Omega |_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}$ . The pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ and the symplectic form $\displaystyle{\Omega |_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}$ together specify a Hamiltonian system.

We will show below that the Hamiltonian system constructed here is equivalent to the Hamiltonian system constructed from Dirac's canonical formalism.

### A.3 The Legendre transformation

We will show below the equivalence between Hamiltonian systems through the Legendre transformation. And we now first introduce the Legendre transformation.

We first introduce some structures appearing in the Legendre transformation:

We define the momenta components of the Legendre transformation $\displaystyle{p_{a}(q,\dot{q})}$ as

$$\begin{align}p_{a}(q,\dot{q}) = \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}^{a}} (q,\dot{q}). \quad (A.6)\end{align}$$

We consider the map from the set $\displaystyle{(q^{a},\dot{q}^{a})}$ to the set $\displaystyle{(q^{a},p_{a})}$ as

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{q^{a} = q^{a}}\\ \ {}\\ \ {p_{a} = p_{a}(q,\dot{q}).} \end{array} \quad (A.7)\end{align}$$

We read out the primary constraints $\displaystyle{\phi_{m}(q,p)}$ , for $\displaystyle{m = 1,2,\dots,M}$ , such that the subset of $\displaystyle{(q^{a},p_{a})}$ satisfying

$$\begin{align}\phi_{m}(q,p) = 0, \quad (A.8)\end{align}$$

is the image of the map (A.7). And we also read out that, for a given point $\displaystyle{(q^{a},p_{a})}$ satisfying $\displaystyle{\phi_{m}(q,p) = 0}$ , for $\displaystyle{m = 1,2,\dots,M}$ , the inverse image of the map (A.7) is multi- valued.

We now introduce a parametrization for the multi- valued inverse image of the map (A.7) for a given point $\displaystyle{(q^{a},p_{a})}$ satisfying $\displaystyle{\phi_{m}(q,p) = 0}$ , for $\displaystyle{m = 1,2,\dots,M}$ . Specifically, we point out that there is a choice of the Hamiltonian $\displaystyle{H(q,p)}$ , which is a function of $\displaystyle{(q^{a},p_{a})}$ , and which satisfies

$$\begin{align}\left(p_{a}\dot{q}^{a} - L(q,\dot{q})\right)\big|_{p_{a} = p_{a}(q,\dot{q})} = H(q,p)\big|_{p_{a} = p_{a}(q,\dot{q})}. \quad (A.9)\end{align}$$

And, by taking a variation of (A.9) and by comparing with the definition of the primary constraints around (A.8), we get the following statement: There is a choice of $\displaystyle{u^{m}(q,\dot{q})}$ , for $\displaystyle{m = 1,2,\dots,M}$ , which are functions of $\displaystyle{(q^{a},\dot{q}^{a})}$ , and which satisfy

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{r l} & {\dot{q}^{a} = \Big(\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{a}} (q,p) + u^{m}(q,\dot{q})\frac{\partial\phi_{m}}{\partial p_{a}} (q,p)\Big)\Big|_{p_{a} = p_{a}(q,\dot{q})}}\\ & {-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q^{a}} (q,\dot{q}) = \Big(\frac{\partial H}{\partial q^{a}} (q,p) + u^{m}(q,\dot{q})\frac{\partial\phi_{m}}{\partial q^{a}} (q,p)\Big)\Big|_{p_{a} = p_{a}(q,\dot{q})}.} \end{array} \quad (A.10)\end{align}$$

Here, the set $\displaystyle{u^{m}(q,\dot{q})}$ , for $\displaystyle{m = 1,2,\dots,M}$ , is a parametrization for the multi- valued inverse image of the map (A.7) for a given point $\displaystyle{(q^{a},p_{a})}$ satisfying $\displaystyle{\phi_{m}(q,p) = 0}$ , for $\displaystyle{m = 1,2,\dots,M}$ .

Having introduced the previous structures, we now introduce the Legendre transformation. Here, the Legendre transformation is a one- to- one map from the set $\displaystyle{(q^{a},\dot{q}^{a})}$ to the subset of $\displaystyle{(q^{a},p_{a},u^{m})}$ satisfying $\displaystyle{\phi_{m}(q,p) = 0}$ , for $\displaystyle{m = 1,2,\dots,M}$ . The Legendre transformation is expressed as

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{q^{a} = q^{a}}\\ \ {}\\ \ {p_{a} = p_{a}(q,\dot{q})}\\ \ {}\\ \ {u^{m} = u^{m}(q,\dot{q}).} \end{array} \quad (A.11)\end{align}$$

And the inverse Legendre transformation is expressed as

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{q^{a} = q^{a}}\\ \ {\dot{q}^{a} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{a}} (q,p) + u^{m}\frac{\partial\phi_{m}}{\partial p_{a}} (q,p).} \end{array} \quad (A.12)\end{align}$$

For the application below, we derive the following two statements from the one- to- one map of the Legendre transformation (A.11) and (A.12): First, given the equations

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{p_{a} = p_{a}(q,\dot{q})}\\ \ {}\\ \ {u^{m} = u^{m}(q,\dot{q}),} \end{array} \quad (A.13)\end{align}$$

we have

$$\begin{align}\phi_{m}(q,p) = 0, \quad (A.14)\end{align}$$

and we can solve $\displaystyle{\dot{q}^{a}}$ from (A.13) as

$$\begin{align}\dot{q}^{a} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{a}} (q,p) + u^{m}\frac{\partial\phi_{m}}{\partial p_{a}} (q,p). \quad (A.15)\end{align}$$

Second, given the equations

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}\dot{q}^{a} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{a}} (q,p) + u^{m}\frac{\partial\phi_{m}}{\partial p_{a}} (q,p) \ \phi_{m}(q,p) = 0, \end{array} \quad (A.16)\end{align}$$

we can solve $\displaystyle{p_{a}}$ , $\displaystyle{u^{m}}$ from (A.16) as

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{p_{a} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}^{a}} (q,\dot{q})}\\ \ {u^{m} = u^{m}(q,\dot{q}).} \end{array} \quad (A.17)\end{align}$$

### A.4 The Legendre transformed system

Having introduced the Legendre transformation, we now perform the first step in showing the equivalence between the Hamiltonian systems. Here, we consider the Legendre transformed system defined by the following action

$$\begin{align}S_{H} = \int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}d t\Big(p_{a}(t)\frac{d}{d t} q^{a}(t) - H\big(q(t),p(t)\big) - u^{m}(t)\phi_{m}\big(q(t),p(t)\big)\Big). \quad (A.18)\end{align}$$

We construct a Hamiltonian system by applying the Legendre transformed system (A.18) to the covariant phase space formalism. And we show that the Hamiltonian system constructed in subsection A.2 is equivalent to the Hamiltonian system constructed here.

We now apply the Legendre transformed system (A.18) to the covariant phase space formalism. Following the prescription, we take a variation of the action

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{\delta S_{H} = \int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}d t\Big(-\Big(\frac{d}{d t} p_{a}(t) + \frac{\partial H}{\partial q^{a}}\big(q(t),p(t)\big) + u^{m}(t)\frac{\partial\phi_{m}}{\partial q^{a}}\big(q(t),p(t)\big)\Big)\delta q^{a}(t)}\\ \ {-\Big(-\frac{d}{d t} q^{a}(t) + \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{a}}\big(q(t),p(t)\big) + u^{m}(t)\frac{\partial\phi_{m}}{\partial p_{a}}\big(q(t),p(t)\big)\Big)\delta p_{a}(t)}\\ \ {-\phi_{m}\big(q(t),p(t)\big)\delta u^{m}(t)\Big)}\\ \ {+p_{a}(t)\delta q^{a}(t)|_{t = t_{f}} - p_{a}(t)\delta q^{a}(t)|_{t = t_{i}}.} \end{array} \quad (A.19)\end{align}$$

From the variation of the action (A.19), we read out the equations of motion as

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}\frac{d}{d t} q^{a}(t) = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{a}} (q(t),p(t)) + u^{m}(t)\frac{\partial\phi_{m}}{\partial p_{a}} (q(t),p(t))\ \displaystyle \frac{d}{d t} p_{a}(t) = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q^{a}} (q(t),p(t)) - u^{m}(t)\frac{\partial\phi_{m}}{\partial q^{a}} (q(t),p(t))\ \displaystyle \phi_{m}(q(t),p(t)) = 0. \end{array} \quad (A.20)\end{align}$$

And we read out the symplectic potential as

$$\begin{align}\theta_{H} = p_{a}(t)\delta q^{a}(t). \quad (A.23)\end{align}$$

Then, we define the pre- phase space as the set of solutions of the equations of motion (A.20), (A.21), (A.22), where, for the moment, we denote the pre- phase space as $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{H}}$ . By taking an exterior derivative of the symplectic potential (A.23) in the set of configurations, we define the symplectic form as

$$\begin{align}\Omega_{H} = \delta_{1}p_{a}(t)\delta_{2}q^{a}(t) - \delta_{1}q^{a}(t)\delta_{2}p_{a}(t). \quad (A.24)\end{align}$$

And by taking a pullback of the symplectic form from the set of configurations to the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{H}}$ , we define the symplectic form of the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\Omega_{H}|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{H}}}$ . The pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{H}}$ and the symplectic form $\displaystyle{\Omega_{H}|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{H}}}$ together specify a Hamiltonian system.

We now show that the Hamiltonian system constructed in subsection A.2 is equivalent to the Hamiltonian system constructed here. Specifically, we show the following two facts: first, the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ can be identified to the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{H}}$ ; second, the symplectic form $\displaystyle{\Omega |_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}$ is equivalent to the symplectic form $\displaystyle{\Omega_{H}|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{H}}}$ .

We now construct an identification from the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ to the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{H}}$ . Specifically, we study the general solution of the equations of motion (A.20), (A.21), (A.22). Here, we first solve $\displaystyle{p_{a}(t)}$ and $\displaystyle{u^{m}(t)}$ from the equations of motion (A.20), (A.22) as

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{{p_{a}(t)|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{H}}=p_{a}\big(q(t),\frac{d}{d t}q(t)\big)\Big|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{H}}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}^{a}}\big(q(t),\frac{d}{d t}q(t)\big)\Big|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{H}}}}\\ \ {{u^{m}(t)|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{H}}=u^{m}\big(q(t),\frac{d}{d t}q(t)\big)\Big|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{H}},}}\end{array} \quad (A.25)\end{align}$$

where, in solving (A.25), we have used the statement mentioned around (A.16), (A.17). We then apply (A.25) to the equations of motion (A.21), from which we get the equations of motion for $\displaystyle{q^{a}(t)}$ as

$$\begin{align}\frac{d}{d t}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}^{a}}\big(q(t),\frac{d}{d t}q(t)\big)\Big|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{H}} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial q^{a}}\big(q(t),\frac{d}{d t}q(t)\big)\Big|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{H}}, \quad (A.26)\end{align}$$

We now show that the symplectic form $\displaystyle{\Omega |_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}$ is equivalent to the symplectic form $\displaystyle{\Omega_{H}|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}$ .

Specifically, we perform the following computation

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{r l} & {\Omega |_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}} = \left(\delta_{1}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial q^{a}}\left(q(t),\frac{d}{d t} q(t)\right)\right)\delta_{2}q^{a}(t) - \delta_{1}q^{a}(t)\delta_{2}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial q^{a}}\left(q(t),\frac{d}{d t} q(t)\right)\right)\right)\Big|_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}}\\ & {\qquad = \left(\delta_{1}p_{a}(t)\delta_{2}q^{a}(t) - \delta_{1}q^{a}(t)\delta_{2}p_{a}(t)\right)\Big|_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}} = \Omega_{H}|_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}},} \end{array} \quad (A.27)\end{align}$$

## A.5 A further reformulation for the Hamiltonian system constructed in subsection A.4

So far, we have shown that the Hamiltonian system constructed in subsection A.2 is equivalent to the Hamiltonian system constructed in subsection A.4. We now take a further reformulation for the Hamiltonian system constructed in subsection A.4.

We first study the general solution of the equations of motion (A.20), (A.21), (A.22).

To study the general solution, we reexpress the equations of motion (A.20), (A.21), (A.22) as

$$\begin{align}\frac{d}{d t} F\Big|_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}} = \Big(\{F,H\}_{P} + u^{m}(t)\{F,\phi_{m}\}_{P}\Big)\Big|_{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}} \quad (A.28)\end{align}$$

We refer to the subset of $\displaystyle{(q^{a},p_{a})}$ satisfying the constraint equations $\displaystyle{\phi_{j}(q,p) = 0}$ , for $\displaystyle{j = 1,2,\dots,J}$ , as the constraint surface $\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}$ . $$\begin{align}H_{T}(z) = \Big(H(q,p) + \sum_{m = 1}^{M}u^{m}(q,p)\phi_{m}(q,p)\Big)\Big|_{\left\{ \begin{array}{l l}{q^{a} = q^{a}(z)}\\ \ {p_{a} = p_{a}(z)} \end{array} \right.}, \quad (A.37)\end{align}$$

$$\begin{align}\{F,G\}_{D} = \Big(\{\widetilde{F},\widetilde{G}\}_{P} - \{\widetilde{F},\phi_{j}\}_{P}\cdot C^{j k}\cdot \{\phi_{k},\widetilde{G}\}_{P}\Big)\Big|_{\left\{ \begin{array}{l}{q^{a} = q^{a}(z)}\\ \ {p_{a} = p_{a}(z)} \end{array} \right.}. \quad (A.38)\end{align}$$

where $\displaystyle{j, k, l = 1, 2, \ldots , J}$ . $$\begin{align}\frac{d}{d t} F\big|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}} = \{F, H_{T}\}_{D}\big|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}, \quad (A.41)\end{align}$$

for $\displaystyle{F}$ being any functions of the constraint surface $\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}$ parameterized by $\displaystyle{z^{r}}$ , and

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{q^{a}(t)|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}} = q^{a}\big(z(t)\big)|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}\\ \ {p_{a}(t)|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}} = p_{a}\big(z(t)\big)|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}},} \end{array} \quad (A.42)\end{align}$$

and

$$\begin{align}u^{m}(t)|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}} = u^{m}\Big(q\big(z(t)\big),p\big(z(t)\big)\Big)\Big|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}. \quad (A.43)\end{align}$$

Here, in (A.41), the $\displaystyle{F}$ and $\displaystyle{\{F, H_{T}\}_{D}}$ are viewed as applying $\displaystyle{z^{r}(t)}$ in the position of $\displaystyle{z^{r}}$ .

We now study the general solution from the equations of motion (A.41), (A.42), (A.43). By analyzing the equations of motion (A.41), (A.42), (A.43), we see that the general solution is uniquely determined by the value $\displaystyle{z_{0}^{r} \equiv z^{r}(t = 0)}$ , which can be any point on the constraint surface $\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}$ . In more detail, given a point $\displaystyle{z_{0}^{r}}$ on the constraint surface $\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}$ , we construct the corresponding solution as the following: First, we solve $\displaystyle{z^{r}(t)|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}$ from (A.41) with the initial conditions

$$\begin{align}z^{r}(t = 0)|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}} = z_{0}^{r}. \quad (A.44)\end{align}$$

Second, we read out $\displaystyle{q^{a}(t)|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}$ , $\displaystyle{p_{a}(t)|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}$ , $\displaystyle{u^{m}(t)|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}$ from (A.42), (A.43). From now on, we parameterize the general solution with the constraint surface $\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}$ or equivalently with $\displaystyle{z_{0}^{r}}$ in this way.

With the knowledge of the general solution, we now take a further reformulation for the Hamiltonian system constructed in subsection A.4. Specifically, we parameterize the pre-phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ with the constraint surface $\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}$ or equivalently with $\displaystyle{z_{0}^{r}}$ , with $\displaystyle{r = 1,2,\dots,2N - J}$ , as mentioned in the previous paragraph. And we represent the symplectic form as

$$\begin{align}\Omega |_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}} = \Omega_{z^{r}z^{s}}(z_{0})\delta_{1}z_{0}^{r}\delta_{2}z_{0}^{s}, \quad (A.45)\end{align}$$

with

$$\begin{align}\Omega_{z^{r}z^{s}}(z) = \frac{\partial p_{a}}{\partial z^{r}} (z)\frac{\partial q^{a}}{\partial z^{s}} (z) - \frac{\partial q^{a}}{\partial z^{r}} (z)\frac{\partial p_{a}}{\partial z^{s}} (z). \quad (A.46)\end{align}$$

Here, in deriving (A.45) and (A.46), we have used (A.24), (A.27), (A.42), (A.44). And we have evaluated the symplectic form $\displaystyle{\Omega |_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}$ at $\displaystyle{t = 0}$ .

Below, we will represent the reformulated Hamiltonian system in the canonical formalism, and show its equivalence to the Hamiltonian system constructed from Dirac's canonical formalism.

### A.6 A property of the symplectic form $\displaystyle{\Omega_{z^{r}z^{s}}(z)}$

To represent the Hamiltonian system in the canonical formalism, we now introduce a property of the symplectic form $\displaystyle{\Omega_{z^{r}z^{s}}(z)}$ defined in (A.46).

The property is the following: The symplectic form $\displaystyle{\Omega_{z^{r}z^{s}}(z)}$ is non- degenerate. And its inverse $\displaystyle{(\Omega^{- 1})^{z^{r}z^{s}}(z)}$ is the matrix of the Dirac's brackets between the variables $\displaystyle{z^{r}}$ that parameterize the constraint surface $\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}$ as

$$\begin{align}(\Omega^{-1})^{z^{r}z^{s}}(z) = \{z^{r},z^{s}\}_{D}. \quad (A.47)\end{align}$$

Here, in the Dirac's bracket $\displaystyle{\{z^{r},z^{s}\}_{D}}$ , we have viewed the variables $\displaystyle{z^{r}}$ as functions of the constraint surface $\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}$ .

To prove this property, we first introduce some structures:

We extend the variables $\displaystyle{z^{r}}$ from the constraint surface $\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}$ to the set $\displaystyle{(q^{a},p_{a})}$ . Namely, we introduce functions $\displaystyle{\widetilde{z}^{r}(q,p)}$ , for $\displaystyle{r = 1,2,\dots,2N - J}$ , such that

$$\begin{align}\widetilde{z}^{r}\big(q(z),p(z)\big) = z^{r}. \quad (A.48)\end{align}$$

We view the functions $\displaystyle{\widetilde{z}^{r}(q,p)}$ and the constraints $\displaystyle{\phi_{j}(q,p)}$ as defining a new set of variables for the set $\displaystyle{(q^{a},p_{a})}$ . Specifically, we introduce a set of variables $\displaystyle{(z^{r},\phi_{j})}$ , with $\displaystyle{r = 1,2,\dots,2N - J}$ and $\displaystyle{j = 1,2,\dots,J}$ . We construct a one- to- one map from the set $\displaystyle{(q^{a},p_{a})}$ to the set $\displaystyle{(z^{r},\phi_{j})}$ as

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{z^{r} = \widetilde{z}^{r}(q,p)}\\ \ {\phi_{j} = \phi_{j}(q,p).} \end{array} \quad (A.49)\end{align}$$

We denote the inverse map as

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{q^{a} = q^{a}(z,\phi)}\\ \ {p_{a} = p_{a}(z,\phi),} \end{array} \quad (A.50)\end{align}$$

where the $\displaystyle{q^{a}(z,\phi)}$ , $\displaystyle{p_{a}(z,\phi)}$ in (A.50) are functions of $\displaystyle{(z^{r},\phi_{j})}$ satisfying

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{q^{a}\big(\widetilde{z} (q,p),\phi (q,p)\big) = q^{a}}\\ \ {p_{a}\big(\widetilde{z} (q,p),\phi (q,p)\big) = p_{a}.} \end{array} \quad (A.51)\end{align}$$

And we also point out that $\displaystyle{q^{a}(z,\phi)}$ , $\displaystyle{p_{a}(z,\phi)}$ reduce to $\displaystyle{q^{a}(z)}$ , $\displaystyle{p_{a}(z)}$ when setting $\displaystyle{\phi = 0}$ as

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{q^{a}(z,\phi = 0) = q^{a}(z)}\\ \ {p_{a}(z,\phi = 0) = p_{a}(z),} \end{array} \quad (A.52)\end{align}$$

which can be shown by applying $\displaystyle{q^{a}(z)}$ , $\displaystyle{p_{a}(z)}$ to (A.51) in the position of $\displaystyle{q^{a}}$ , $\displaystyle{p_{a}}$ and by taking use of (A.36), (A.48).

We introduce some collective notations. Specifically, we denote $\displaystyle{Q^{A} = (q^{a},p_{a})}$ . We denote $\displaystyle{Z^{R} = (z^{r},\phi_{j})}$ . We denote the map (A.49) from the set $\displaystyle{Q^{A} = (q^{a},p_{a})}$ to the set $\displaystyle{Z^{R} = (z^{r},\phi_{j})}$ as $\displaystyle{Z^{R} = Z^{R}(Q)}$ . And we denote the inverse map (A.50) from the set $\displaystyle{Z^{R} = (z^{r},\phi_{j})}$ to the set $\displaystyle{Q^{A} = (q^{a},p_{a})}$ as $\displaystyle{Q^{A} = Q^{A}(Z)}$ .

We introduce some structures in terms of the set of variables $\displaystyle{Q^{A} = (q^{a},p_{a})}$ . Specifically, we define the symplectic form of the set $\displaystyle{Q^{A} = (q^{a},p_{a})}$ as

$$\begin{align}\Omega^{(0)}_{Q^{A}Q^{B}} = \left( \begin{array}{cc}\Omega^{(0)} & \Omega^{(0)}\ \Omega^{(0)} & \Omega^{(0)}^{q^{a}q^{b}} \end{array} \right) \Omega^{(0)}_{p_{a^{\prime}}p_{b^{\prime}}} = \left( \begin{array}{cc}0 & -\delta_{a^{\prime}}^{b^{\prime}}\ \delta_{a^{\prime}}^{b} & 0 \end{array} \right). \quad (A.53)\end{align}$$

We compute the matrix of the Poisson brackets between the set of variables $\displaystyle{Q^{A} = (q^{a},p_{a})}$ as

$$\begin{align}\Omega^{(0)Q^{A}Q^{B}} = \left( \begin{array}{cc}Q^{(0)q^{a}q^{b}} & Q^{(0)q^{a}p_{b^{\prime}}}\ Q^{(0)p_{a^{\prime}}q^{b}} & Q^{(0)p_{a^{\prime}}p_{b^{\prime}}} \end{array} \right) = \left( \begin{array}{cc}\{q^{a},q^{b}\} P & \{q^{a},p_{b^{\prime}}\} P\ \{p_{a^{\prime}},q^{b}\} P & \{p_{a^{\prime}},p_{b^{\prime}}\} P \end{array} \right) = \left( \begin{array}{cc}0 & \delta_{a^{\prime}}^{b}\ -\delta_{a^{\prime}}^{b} & 0 \end{array} \right). \quad (A.54)\end{align}$$

And we check that $\displaystyle{\Omega^{(0)}_{Q^{A}Q^{B}}}$ (A.53) and $\displaystyle{\Omega^{(0)Q^{A}Q^{B}}}$ (A.54) are inverse to each other as

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{\Omega_{Q^{A}Q^{B}}^{(0)}\Omega^{(0)Q^{B}Q^{C}} = \delta_{Q^{A}}^{C}}\\ \ {\Omega^{(0)Q^{A}Q^{B}}\Omega^{(0)}_{Q^{B}Q^{C}} = \delta^{Q^{A}}_{Q^{C}}.} \end{array} \quad (A.55)\end{align}$$

We introduce some structures in terms of the set of variables $\displaystyle{Z^{R} = (z^{r},\phi_{j})}$ . Specifically, we define the symplectic form of the set $\displaystyle{Z^{R} = (z^{r},\phi_{j})}$ as

$$\begin{align}\widetilde{\Omega}_{Z^{R}Z^{S}}(Z) = \Omega^{(0)}_{Q^{A}Q^{B}}\frac{\partial Q^{A}}{\partial Z^{R}} (Z)\frac{\partial Q^{B}}{\partial Z^{S}} (Z). \quad (A.56)\end{align}$$

We check that the $\displaystyle{z z}$ - component of $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\Omega}_{Z^{R}Z^{S}}(Z)}$ (A.56), when restricting to $\displaystyle{\phi = 0}$ , is exactly $\displaystyle{\Omega_{z^{r}z^{s}}(z)}$ (A.46) as

$$\begin{align}\widetilde{\Omega}_{z^{r}z^{s}}(z,\phi = 0) = \Omega_{z^{r}z^{s}}(z), \quad (A.57)\end{align}$$

where we have used (A.53). We define the matrix of the Poisson brackets between the set of functions $\displaystyle{Z^{R}(Q) = (\widetilde{z}^{r}(q,p),\phi_{j}(q,p))}$ as

$$\begin{align}\widetilde{\Omega}^{Z^{R}Z^{S}}(Q) = \left( \begin{array}{cc}\Omega^{z^{r}z^{s}}(q,p) & \Omega^{z^{r}\phi_{k}}(q,p) \ \Omega^{\phi_{j}z^{s}}(q,p) & \Omega^{\phi_{j}\phi_{k}}(q,p) \end{array} \right) = \left( \begin{array}{c}\{\widetilde{z}^{r},\widetilde{z}^{s}\}_{P} \ \{\phi_{j},\widetilde{z}^{s}\}_{P} \end{array} \right)\{ \begin{array}{c}\{\widetilde{z}^{r},\phi_{k}\}_{P} \ \{\phi_{j},\phi_{k}\}_{P} \end{array} \right). \quad (A.58)\end{align}$$

We check that the matrix $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\Omega}^{Z^{R}Z^{S}}(Q)}$ (A.58) equals to

$$\begin{align}\widetilde{\Omega}^{Z^{R}Z^{S}}(Q) = \Omega^{(0)Q^{A}Q^{B}}\frac{\partial Z^{R}}{\partial Q^{A}}(Q)\frac{\partial Z^{S}}{\partial Q^{B}}(Q), \quad (A.59)\end{align}$$

where we have used (A.54). And, we check that

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{r l} & {\widetilde{\Omega}_{Z^{R}Z^{S}}(Z)\cdot \widetilde{\Omega}^{Z^{S}Z^{T}}(Q(Z)) = \delta_{Z^{R}}^{Z^{T}}}\\ & {\widetilde{\Omega}^{Z^{R}Z^{S}}(Q(Z))\cdot \widetilde{\Omega}_{Z^{S}Z^{T}}(Z) = \delta^{Z^{R}}_{Z^{T}},} \end{array} \quad (A.60)\end{align}$$

where we have used (A.55), (A.56), (A.59) and the inverse function theorem

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{l}{\frac{\partial Z^{R}}{\partial Q^{A}}(Q(Z))\cdot \frac{\partial Q^{A}}{\partial Z^{S}}(Z) = \delta^{Z^{R}}_{Z^{S}}}\\ \ {\frac{\partial Q^{A}}{\partial Z^{R}}(Z)\cdot \frac{\partial Z^{R}}{\partial Q^{B}}(Q(Z)) = \delta^{Q^{A}}_{Q^{B}}.} \end{array} \quad (A.61)\end{align}$$

With the above defined structures, we now prove the property of the symplectic form $\displaystyle{\Omega_{z^{r}z^{s}}(z)}$ mentioned in the beginning of this subsection. We rewrite the equations (A.60) in component form as

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{r l} & {\widetilde{\Omega}_{z^{r}z^{s}}(z,\phi)\cdot \{\widetilde{z}^{s},\widetilde{z}^{t}\}_{P}\Bigg|\Bigg\{ \begin{array}{l l}{q^{a} = q^{a}(z,\phi)}\\ \ {p_{a} = p_{a}(z,\phi)} \end{array} +\widetilde{\Omega}_{z^{r}\phi_{k}}(z,\phi)\cdot \{\phi_{k},\widetilde{z}^{t}\}_{P}\Bigg|\Bigg\{ \begin{array}{l l}{q^{a} = q^{a}(z,\phi)}\\ \ {p_{a} = p_{a}(z,\phi)} \end{array} = \delta_{r}^{t}}\\ & {\widetilde{\Omega}_{z^{r}z^{s}}(z,\phi)\cdot \{\widetilde{z}^{s},\phi_{l}\}_{P}\Bigg|\Bigg\{ \begin{array}{l l}{q^{a} = q^{a}(z,\phi)}\\ \ {p_{a} = p_{a}(z,\phi)} \end{array} +\widetilde{\Omega}_{z^{r}\phi_{k}}(z,\phi)\cdot \{\phi_{k},\phi_{l}\}_{P}\Bigg|\Bigg\{ \begin{array}{l l}{q^{a} = q^{a}(z,\phi)}\\ \ {p_{a} = p_{a}(z,\phi)} \end{array} = 0}\\ & {\widetilde{\Omega}_{\phi_{j}z^{s}}(z,\phi)\cdot \{\widetilde{z}^{s},\widetilde{z}^{t}\}_{P}\Bigg|\Bigg\{ \begin{array}{l l}{q^{a} = q^{a}(z,\phi)}\\ \ {p_{a} = p_{a}(z,\phi)} \end{array} +\widetilde{\Omega}_{\phi_{j}\phi_{k}}(z,\phi)\cdot \{\phi_{k},\widetilde{z}^{t}\}_{P}\Bigg|\Bigg\{ \begin{array}{l l}{q^{a} = q^{a}(z,\phi)}\\ \ {p_{a} = p_{a}(z,\phi)} \end{array} = 0}\\ & {\widetilde{\Omega}_{\phi_{j}z^{s}}(z,\phi)\cdot \{\widetilde{z}^{s},\phi_{l}\}_{P}\Bigg|\Bigg\{ \begin{array}{l l}{q^{a} = q^{a}(z,\phi)}\\ \ {p_{a} = p_{a}(z,\phi)} \end{array} +\widetilde{\Omega}_{\phi_{j}\phi_{k}}(z,\phi)\cdot \{\phi_{k},\phi_{l}\}_{P}\Bigg|\Bigg\{ \begin{array}{l l}{q^{a} = q^{a}(z,\phi)}\\ \ {p_{a} = p_{a}(z,\phi)} \end{array} = \delta_{l}^{j},} \end{array} \quad (A.62)\end{align}$$

and

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{r l} & {\{\widetilde{z}^{r},\widetilde{z}^{s}\} P\Bigg\{\underset {p_{a} = p_{a}(z,\phi)}{\overset{q^{a} = q^{a}(z,\phi)}{\sim}}\cdot \widetilde{\Omega}_{z^{s}z^{t}}(z,\phi) + \{\widetilde{z}^{r},\phi_{k}\} P\Bigg\{\underset {p_{a} = p_{a}(z,\phi)}{\overset{q^{a} = q^{a}(z,\phi)}{\sim}}\cdot \widetilde{\Omega}_{\phi_{k}z^{t}}(z,\phi) = \delta^{r}t}\\ & {\{\widetilde{z}^{r},\widetilde{z}^{s}\} P\Bigg\{\underset {p_{a} = p_{a}(z,\phi)}{\overset{q^{a} = q^{a}(z,\phi)}{\sim}}\cdot \widetilde{\Omega}_{z^{s}\phi_{l}}(z,\phi) + \{\widetilde{z}^{r},\phi_{k}\} P\Bigg\{\underset {p_{a} = p_{a}(z,\phi)}{\overset{q^{a} = q^{a}(z,\phi)}{\sim}}\cdot \widetilde{\Omega}_{\phi_{k}\phi_{l}}(z,\phi) = 0}\\ & {\{\phi_{j},\widetilde{z}^{s}\} P\Bigg\{\underset {p_{a} = p_{a}(z,\phi)}{\overset{q^{a} = q^{a}(z,\phi)}{\sim}}\cdot \widetilde{\Omega}_{z^{s}z^{t}}(z,\phi) + \{\phi_{j},\phi_{k}\} P\Bigg\{\underset {p_{a} = p_{a}(z,\phi)}{\overset{q^{a} = q^{a}(z,\phi)}{\sim}}\cdot \widetilde{\Omega}_{\phi_{k}z^{t}}(z,\phi) = 0}\\ & {\{\phi_{j},\widetilde{z}^{s}\} P\Bigg\{\underset {p_{a} = p_{a}(z,\phi)}{\overset{q^{a} = q^{a}(z,\phi)}{\sim}}\cdot \widetilde{\Omega}_{z^{s}\phi_{l}}(z,\phi) + \{\phi_{j},\phi_{k}\} P\Bigg\{\underset {p_{a} = p_{a}(z,\phi)}{\overset{q^{a} = q^{a}(z,\phi)}{\sim}}\cdot \widetilde{\Omega}_{\phi_{k}\phi_{l}}(z,\phi) = \delta_{j}^{l}.} \end{array} \quad (A.63)\end{align}$$

For the set of equations (A.62), by solving $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\Omega}_{z^{r}\phi_{j}}(z,\phi)}$ from the second equation and by applying the solution of $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\Omega}_{z^{r}\phi_{j}}(z,\phi)}$ to the first equation, we get

$$\begin{align}\widetilde{\Omega}_{z^{r}z^{s}}(z,\phi)\cdot \Big(\{\widetilde{z}^{s},\widetilde{z}^{t}\} P - \{\widetilde{z}^{s},\phi_{j}\} P\cdot C^{jk}\cdot \{\phi_{k},\widetilde{z}^{t}\} P\Big)\Bigg\vert_{\left\{ \begin{array}{l l}{q^{a} = q^{a}(z,\phi)}\\ \ {p_{a} = p_{a}(z,\phi)} \end{array} \right.} = \delta_{r}^{t}. \quad (A.64)\end{align}$$

For the set of equations (A.63), by solving $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\Omega}_{\phi_{j}z^{r}}(z,\phi)}$ from the third equation and by applying the solution of $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\Omega}_{\phi_{j}z^{r}}(z,\phi)}$ to the first equation, we get

$$\begin{align}\Big(\{\widetilde{z}^{r},\widetilde{z}^{s}\} P - \{\widetilde{z}^{r},\phi_{j}\} P\cdot C^{jk}\cdot \{\phi_{k},\widetilde{z}^{s}\} P\Big)\Bigg\vert_{\left\{ \begin{array}{l l}{q^{a} = q^{a}(z,\phi)}\\ \ {p_{a} = p_{a}(z,\phi)} \end{array} \right.} \cdot \widetilde{\Omega}_{z^{s}z^{t}}(z,\phi) = \delta^{r}t. \quad (A.65)\end{align}$$

Here, in deriving (A.64) and (A.65), we have used the definition of $\displaystyle{C_{jk}}$ (A.35) and $\displaystyle{C^{jk}}$ (A.40).

Then, by restricting $\displaystyle{\phi = 0}$ for (A.64) and (A.65), we get

$$\begin{align}\begin{array}{r l} & {\Omega_{z^{r}z^{s}}(z)\cdot \{z^{s},z^{t}\} _{D} = \delta_{r}^{t}}\\ & {\{z^{r},z^{s}\} _{D}\cdot \Omega_{z^{s}z^{t}}(z) = \delta^{r}t,} \end{array} \quad (A.66)\end{align}$$

where, in the derivation, we have also used (A.38), (A.52) and (A.57). From (A.66), we can prove the property mentioned at the beginning of this subsection, that is, $\displaystyle{\Omega_{z^{r}z^{s}}(z)}$ is nondegenerate and its inverse is $\displaystyle{\{z^{r},z^{s}\}_{D}}$ .

### A.7 Represent the Hamiltonian system in the canonical formalism

Given the property mentioned in subsection A.6 around (A.47), we now represent the Hamiltonian system reformulated in subsection A.5 in the canonical formalism.

Since the symplectic form $\displaystyle{\Omega_{z^{r}z^{s}}(z_{0})}$ is non- degenerate, the physical phase space is exactly the pre- phase space $\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}$ , which is parameterized by the constraint surface $\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}$ or equivalently by the set of variables $\displaystyle{z_{0}^{r}}$ , with $\displaystyle{r = 1,2,\ldots ,2N - J}$ . And we can also read out the brackets from the symplectic form (A.45). Specifically, by taking use of the Hamiltonian equation

$$\begin{align}X_{z_{0}^{r}}\cdot \Omega |_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}} = -\delta z_{0}^{r}, \quad (A.67)\end{align}$$

we get

$$\begin{align}X_{z_{0}^{r}} = -(\Omega^{-1})^{z^{r}z^{s}}(z_{0})\frac{\delta}{\delta z_{0}^{s}} = (\Omega^{-1})^{z^{s}z^{r}}(z_{0})\frac{\delta}{\delta z_{0}^{s}}. \quad (A.68)\end{align}$$

And, by applying (A.68) to (2.6), we get

$$\begin{align}\{z_{0}^{r},z_{0}^{s}\} |_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}} = (\Omega^{-1})^{z^{r}z^{s}}(z_{0}) = \{z^{r},z^{s}\}_{D}|_{z^{r} = z_{0}^{r}}, \quad (A.69)\end{align}$$

where, in the last equation, we have also used (A.47).

The Hamiltonian system represented here is exactly the Hamiltonian system constructed from Dirac's canonical formalism.
